facts: |
  Rixon, a casino patron excluded under s 79 of the Casino Control Act 1992 (NSW), was approached at a
  roulette table by an employee who placed a hand on his shoulder to get his attention before telling him he
  must go to an interview room. The trial judge rejected Rixon's evidence that he was "grabbed" and "spun
  round" and found only that the employee placed a hand on his shoulder without force or injury.
issue: (1) Whether a touching without anger or hostility can still be a battery; (2) whether the touching here was a battery; (3) what intention assault requires.
held: |
  The absence of anger or hostility does not stop a touching being a battery — the real test is whether the
  contact exceeds what is generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of daily life; on the facts, touching
  the shoulder to gain attention did not exceed that standard, so there was no battery. On assault, the
  intention required is the intention to create apprehension of contact, not an intention to actually carry
  out the contact, and that intention was not made out here.
ratio: Battery is not limited to hostile or angry touching; the question is whether the touching exceeds physical contact generally acceptable in everyday life. Assault requires intention to create apprehension of imminent contact, not intention to inflict the contact itself.
significance: Modern Australian statement of the "unlawful touching" / personal inviolability principle, and of the intention element of assault.
why-here: The key authority for "any touching without lawful excuse can be a battery" and for distinguishing the intention needed for assault from the intention needed for battery.
