name: Commonwealth v Tasmania (Tasmanian Dam Case)
citation: "[1983] 158 CLR 1"
aglc: Commonwealth v Tasmania [1983] 158 CLR 1
facts: |
  In 1974 Australia became party to the World Heritage Convention. The Commonwealth passed legislation to
  protect part of Tasmania's wilderness from the building of a dam on the Gordon River. The Tasmanian
  Government challenged the validity of the Commonwealth legislation on the grounds that the Commonwealth had
  no power to legislate on state matters, such as the dam.
issue: |
  Whether the Commonwealth had the power, under the external affairs power in s 51(xxix) of the Constitution, to
  stop the Tasmanian Government from damming the Gordon River under Commonwealth legislation.
held: |
  The High Court majority held that, even though the dam would normally be a matter for Tasmania, in light of
  Australia's international obligations the Commonwealth was entitled to intervene. Being a party to the World
  Heritage Convention directly relates to external affairs, which allows the Commonwealth to pass laws that give
  effect to Australia's international legal obligations. However, if the Commonwealth's use of the external
  affairs power was no more than a device to exercise legislative power, it would probably not be legitimate.
ratio: |
  Legislation giving effect to a treaty to which Australia is party is a valid exercise of the external affairs
  power, even on a subject otherwise within state competence, provided the power is not used merely as a device.
significance: |
  The leading illustration of how the external affairs power lets the Commonwealth transform treaty
  obligations into domestic law in a federation — the lecture adds the same conclusion would follow for
  customary international law.
why-here: Used to show that treaty obligations can give the Commonwealth legislative power over matters that would otherwise belong to the states.
