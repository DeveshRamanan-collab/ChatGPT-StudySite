facts: |
  A car driven by Chapman collided with another vehicle near an Adelaide intersection; Chapman's vehicle
  overturned and he was thrown onto the roadway, unconscious. Dr Cherry, driving a third car that stopped, went
  to aid the unconscious Chapman on a dark, wet night with poor visibility and no one to warn oncoming traffic.
  While attending to Chapman, Dr Cherry was struck by a car driven by Hearse and died of his injuries. Dr
  Cherry's executor sued Hearse, who joined Chapman as a third party claiming contribution.
issue: Whether Chapman owed Dr Cherry a duty of care, given that Chapman could not have foreseen the precise sequence of events that led to Dr Cherry's death.
held: |
  The High Court (Dixon CJ, Kitto, Taylor, Menzies and Windeyer JJ) dismissed Chapman's appeal against the
  order for contribution, rejecting his argument that he owed no duty of care to Dr Cherry. The test for the
  existence of a duty of care does not require reasonable foreseeability of the precise sequence of events or
  the precise manner in which the injury was sustained; it was enough that injury to a class of persons of
  which Dr Cherry was one — a person likely to render aid at the scene of a road accident — might reasonably
  have been foreseen as a consequence of the collision.
ratio: To establish a duty of care, it is not necessary to show that the precise manner in which the plaintiff's injuries were sustained was reasonably foreseeable; it is sufficient that injury to a class of persons of which the plaintiff was one might reasonably have been foreseen as a consequence of the defendant's conduct.
significance: Leading Australian authority on what "reasonable foreseeability" requires at the duty stage — foreseeability of a general class of harm and class of person, not of the exact chain of events.
why-here: The go-to authority whenever a defendant argues they could not have foreseen the exact way an injury occurred.
