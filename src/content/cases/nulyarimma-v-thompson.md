facts: |
  Australia is party to the 1948 Genocide Convention. Members of an Aboriginal community claimed that the
  sponsorship of Native Title Act amendments and the failure to seek World Heritage listing of the Lake Eyre
  region were acts of genocide. They argued that genocide is part of customary international law, and that even
  without legislation criminalising genocide within Australia, Australian courts could try individuals accused of
  genocide.
issue: |
  Whether the customary international law prohibition of genocide was part of Australian law.
held: |
  The Federal Court ruled that even if genocide is recognised as part of customary international law, it does not
  form part of Australian law in the absence of domestic legislation declaring genocide a crime. It accepted that
  the prohibition of genocide constituted a jus cogens norm but, without implementing legislation, it had no
  effect on Australian law. Wilcox J (at [25]) distinguished treating a civil law rule like foreign sovereign
  immunity as domestic law from a norm "criminalising conduct that is not made punishable by the domestic law".
  Merkel J dissented, identifying criteria for customary international law to become a source of the common law.
ratio: |
  A customary international law crime, even a jus cogens one, cannot be tried in an Australian court without
  domestic legislation making it an offence.
significance: |
  The key Australian authority on custom and domestic law; Merkel J's dissenting criteria (general acceptance,
  reception not inconsistent with statute or declared common law, reception as from the date the rule was
  established) have been cited by other judges since.
