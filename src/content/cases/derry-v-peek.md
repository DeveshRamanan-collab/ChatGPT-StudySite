facts: A company issued a prospectus stating it had a right to run steam trains (as the lecture puts it). It expected to get consent from the Board of Trade, but consent was ultimately refused; the shares lost value and investors lost money. (Facts as summarised in the Topic 7 lecture; the full report citation is not given in the materials.)
issue: Whether a misstatement that was negligent, but not intentional or reckless, could found an action in deceit.
held: The House of Lords ruled that negligent misstatement will not suffice for deceit — rather, it must be an intentional or reckless misstatement. As the case pre-dated the modern law of negligence, the investors had no cause of action.
ratio: Deceit requires an intentional or reckless misstatement; mere negligence is not enough.
significance: Left a gap — no remedy for a merely negligent misstatement — which Hedley Byrne v Heller later filled by creating liability for negligent misstatement at common law.
why-here: "Explains why negligent misstatement needed its own duty of care: deceit was closed to careless (as opposed to dishonest) statements."
