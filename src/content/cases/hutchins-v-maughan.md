facts: |
  The defendant laid poisonous baits on unfenced land. The complainant's dogs picked the baits up some days
  later and died as a result. The complainant sued in trespass (having already lost on negligence and
  nuisance before the police magistrate).
issue: |
  Whether the complainant's injury (the loss of his dogs) was directly/immediately occasioned by the
  defendant's act of laying the baits, or was merely consequential upon it, so that trespass would or would
  not lie.
held: |
  Herring CJ held that trespass did not lie. The baits were laid before the complainant brought his dogs onto
  the land, so the injury was consequential upon the complainant's own subsequent act of bringing the dogs
  there, not directly or immediately occasioned by the laying of the baits.
ratio: |
  Trespass requires the injury to be the direct and immediate result of the defendant's act; where the
  plaintiff's own intervening act is needed to produce the harm, the injury is merely consequential and belongs
  to action on the case, not trespass. Directness is often "an issue of time" — had the baits been thrown at
  the dogs (rather than laid before they arrived), trespass would have been made out.
significance: |
  The leading illustration of the trespass/action-on-the-case distinction and the meaning of "directness",
  drawing on the historical statements in Reynolds v Clarke (1725) 93 ER 747, Scott v Shepherd (1773) 96 ER 525
  and Leame v Bray (1803) 102 ER 724.
why-here: Used to teach the directness element that separates trespass from action on the case.
