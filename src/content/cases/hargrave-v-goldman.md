facts: |
  Lightning struck a red gum tree on the defendant's Western Australian farm. The defendant had the tree
  felled but left the resulting fire to burn itself out. The weather then turned, the fire revived, and it
  spread to destroy the plaintiff's neighbouring properties.
issue: |
  Whether an occupier who did not create a natural danger, but knew of it and did not act, owed neighbouring
  occupiers a duty of care for harm caused by the omission to abate it.
held: |
  The lecture material identifies the relevant factors for liability — reasonable foreseeability of harm,
  the occupier-neighbour special relationship, the defendant's knowledge of the danger, and their control
  over it — as the ingredients needed for an omission-based duty of care in this setting.
ratio: |
  An omission to abate a danger on one's land can found a duty of care to neighbouring occupiers where there
  is a special relationship (here, occupier-neighbour), the defendant knew of the danger, and had the
  practical ability (control) to deal with it — foreseeability alone is not enough.
significance: |
  Establishes the extra ingredients — special relationship, knowledge and control — that must be added to
  foreseeability before an omission attracts a duty of care.
why-here: Used to show what must be added to foreseeability before an omission (rather than a positive act) attracts a duty of care.
