facts: |
  A father was suspected of sexually abusing his daughter. A medical practitioner and social workers were
  alleged to have acted negligently in examining the child and investigating the possibility of abuse. No
  criminal charges were laid, but the investigation caused the breakdown of the family and, the father
  claimed, shock, distress, psychiatric injury and consequential financial loss. He sued the investigators,
  alleging they owed him a duty to exercise reasonable care in conducting the investigation.
issue: Whether professionals investigating a suspicion of child sexual abuse owe a duty of care to the person under suspicion.
held: |
  The High Court held the defendants owed the father no duty of care. If mere reasonable foreseeability of
  harm entailed legal liability, this would impose an intolerable burden of potential liability on those
  investigating abuse allegations and would result in negligence subverting other principles of law and
  statutory provisions designed to protect children. The Court considered the coherence of the law — the
  paramount duty to protect children's interests — and the difficulty of limiting the extent and potential
  indeterminacy of liability, since a duty could extend beyond the father to friends, teachers or anyone else
  suspected of abuse.
ratio: A duty of care will not be recognised, however foreseeable the harm, where doing so would conflict with a duty owed under other legal principles or statute, would create indeterminate liability, or would threaten the coherence of the law.
significance: Leading authority on the policy factors — coherence, indeterminacy and conflicting duties — that can negate a duty of care in a novel case.
why-here: The key authority for arguing that policy factors defeat a duty of care despite foreseeable harm, especially in child-protection or third-party investigation scenarios.
