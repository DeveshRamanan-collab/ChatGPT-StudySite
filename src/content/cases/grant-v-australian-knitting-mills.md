facts: |
  Dr Grant bought woollen underpants from a retailer who had purchased them from the manufacturer, Australian
  Knitting Mills. He wore them for a day and began to itch; within a week he developed dermatitis so severe
  that he spent seventeen weeks in hospital. The garments contained free sulphites, a chemical irritant left
  in through negligence in manufacturing, which was a hidden and latent defect not detectable by any
  reasonable examination. Grant won at trial, but the High Court held the evidence insufficient; he appealed
  to the Privy Council.
issue: Whether the manufacturer of a product with a hidden defect owed the ultimate consumer a duty of care, applying Donoghue v Stevenson, and whether that principle extends beyond food or drink to garments worn externally.
held: |
  The appeal was allowed. Applying Donoghue v Stevenson, the Privy Council held the manufacturer owed Dr Grant
  a duty of care: the case fell squarely within Lord Atkin's principle, the sulphites being a hidden defect
  like the snail in the opaque bottle. It was immaterial that the garment was worn externally rather than
  consumed internally, or that Dr Grant also had a contract claim against the retailer; the manufacturer's
  duty arises independently of contract, provided the product reaches the consumer with no reasonable
  possibility of intermediate examination.
ratio: A manufacturer owes a duty of care to the ultimate consumer of its product wherever a hidden, undetectable defect caused by negligent manufacture reaches the consumer without any reasonable possibility of intermediate examination, regardless of whether the product is consumed internally or applied externally.
significance: Confirms and extends the Donoghue v Stevenson manufacturer/consumer duty as an established category of duty of care.
why-here: The standard second authority (after Donoghue) for the established manufacturer/consumer category of duty of care.
