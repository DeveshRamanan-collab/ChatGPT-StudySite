facts: The defendant parked a truck in the middle of a six-lane road in the early morning hours to unload fruit and vegetables, a practice used for a long time. The plaintiff, who was drunk and speeding, was injured when his car collided with the truck. At trial the defendant was held liable in negligence with a finding of 70% contributory negligence against the plaintiff; the Full Court of South Australia attributed the entire responsibility to the plaintiff's own negligence.
issue: Whether the defendant's negligence in parking the truck was a cause of the plaintiff's injury, given the plaintiff's own negligence, and how causation is to be tested.
held: The High Court held that the defendant's negligence was the cause of the plaintiff's injury, though the judges disagreed about the reasoning on causation. The plaintiff's own negligence did not break the chain, because the truck created the very risk that materialised.
ratio: "Mason CJ: the 'but for' test was never the exclusive test of causation in negligence (it does not work where two or more acts or events are involved or the chain is broken by an intervening event); causation is a question of fact which 'must be determined by applying common sense to the facts of each particular case'; and the chain is not broken where 'the defendant's wrongful conduct has generated the very risk of injury resulting from the negligence of the plaintiff or a third party'. McHugh J: 'but for' should be the exclusive test, with a 'scope of the risk' test allowing policy factors to be employed as part of remoteness."
significance: "The common law starting point on factual causation: Mason CJ's three limitations of the 'but for' test and his 'common sense' approach, which the Ipp Report criticised and CLA s 5D(1) restructured. The textbook flowchart also lists it as an example of multiple sufficient causes."
