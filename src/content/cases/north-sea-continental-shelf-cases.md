facts: |
  Germany, Denmark and the Netherlands disputed how to delimit the continental shelf boundaries between their
  North Sea coastlines; Denmark and the Netherlands argued that the equidistance method in the Geneva
  Convention on the Continental Shelf, to which Germany was not a party, reflected a rule of customary
  international law binding on Germany regardless.
issue: |
  Whether a treaty rule (the equidistance principle) had, notwithstanding a relatively short period of
  application, crystallised into or generated a rule of customary international law binding on a non-party
  state, and what is required of state practice to establish a customary rule.
held: |
  The Court held that the equidistance rule had not become a rule of customary international law binding on
  Germany: within the period in question, however short, state practice — including that of specially
  affected states — had to be "extensive and virtually uniform", and it was not.
ratio: |
  A short period of practice is not itself a bar to the formation of a new customary rule, but the practice
  within that period, including that of states whose interests are specially affected, must be extensive and
  virtually uniform in the sense of the rule invoked; and evidence of opinio juris requires that acts be
  carried out from a sense of legal obligation, not habit, courtesy or convenience.
significance: |
  Leading authority on the generality, uniformity and duration required of state practice, on the
  specially-affected-states requirement, and (with Nicaragua) on how a treaty provision can crystallise a
  developing customary rule.
why-here: Central authority for the practice element of customary international law (duration, uniformity, specially affected states) and for distinguishing opinio juris from mere habit or courtesy.
