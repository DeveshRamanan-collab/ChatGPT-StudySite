facts: |
  Penfolds sold wine in bottles embossed with words stating the bottle remained Penfolds's property; customers
  bought only the wine, not the bottles. Elliott ran a business selling bulk wine to customers who brought
  their own bottles; on at least one occasion he used two of Penfolds's bottles, given to him by his brother,
  to sell wine. Penfolds sued Elliott in trespass and conversion and sought an injunction.
issue: (1) Whether Penfolds could sue Elliott in trespass to goods without being in possession of the bottles; (2) whether Elliott's use of the bottles amounted to conversion.
held: |
  The trespass claim failed: trespass protects possession, and Penfolds was not in possession of the bottles
  at the relevant time — Elliott was, having received them from his brother as (sub-)bailee. On conversion, a
  majority (Latham CJ, Starke, McTiernan and Williams JJ) held Elliott's use of the bottles for his own wine
  was conversion, being inconsistent with Penfolds's dominion over the bottles, even though Penfolds lacked
  possession. Dixon J dissented on the conversion point, describing the use as "temporary and harmless" and
  not serious enough.
ratio: Trespass to goods requires actual possession by the plaintiff at the time of interference; conversion can be established by a plaintiff with only a right to immediate possession where the defendant's dealing with the goods is inconsistent with the owner's dominion.
significance: Leading Australian authority distinguishing trespass to goods (possession-based) from conversion (dominion-based), and illustrating how "serious interference" can divide a court.
why-here: Used for both trespass to goods (why the claim failed) and conversion (why the claim succeeded) on the same facts — a model contrast case.
