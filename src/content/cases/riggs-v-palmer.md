facts: On 13 August 1880 Francis B. Palmer made his will, giving small legacies to his two daughters, Mrs Riggs and Mrs Preston (the plaintiffs), and the remainder of his estate to his grandson, the defendant Elmer E. Palmer. Elmer, who lived with him as a member of the family and was sixteen at his death, knew of the provisions and, to prevent his grandfather revoking them and to obtain speedy possession of the property, wilfully murdered him by poisoning. The will was made in due form and admitted to probate.
issue: "Elmer Palmer claims the property: can he have it?"
held: The majority held he could not. Statutes regulating wills, literally construed, would give the property to the murderer; but a thing within the letter of a statute is not within the statute unless it is within the intention of its makers, and all laws and contracts may be controlled in their operation by general, fundamental maxims of the common law. A minority would have applied the rigid rules established by the legislature, since the laws are silent and refusing the inheritance would impose an additional punishment.
