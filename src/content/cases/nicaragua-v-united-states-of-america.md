facts: |
  Nicaragua brought proceedings against the United States over US support for Contra rebels and US mining of
  Nicaraguan harbours, raising questions about the content of customary international law on the use of force
  and non-intervention, including its relationship to UN Charter provisions on the same subject.
issue: |
  How strictly must state practice conform to an asserted customary rule for the rule to exist, and how does
  the existence of a treaty rule (the UN Charter's prohibition on the use of force) relate to a parallel
  customary rule on the same subject.
held: |
  The Court held that a customary rule does not require practice in "absolutely rigorous conformity" with it;
  it is enough that state conduct is "in general" consistent with the rule, and that instances of inconsistent
  conduct are generally treated as breaches of the rule rather than as evidence of a new rule — even conduct
  defended by reference to an exception within the rule confirms, rather than weakens, it. The Court also
  confirmed that a rule can exist as both a treaty rule and a customary rule simultaneously, each retaining a
  separate existence.
ratio: |
  Customary international law tolerates imperfect state practice provided departures are generally treated as
  breaches rather than as the emergence of a new rule; and a treaty rule and a customary rule on the same
  subject can coexist, with separate content and application.
significance: |
  A leading and wide-ranging ICJ judgment on the identification of customary international law and on the
  relationship between treaty and custom (codification, crystallisation, and generation of new custom).
why-here: Central authority both for how tolerant the practice element is of imperfect compliance, and for the three ways a treaty and a customary rule can relate to each other.
