facts: |
  Hungary and Czechoslovakia signed a treaty for the construction of a system of locks on the Danube, to
  facilitate hydroelectricity and navigation, with works in each country forming a unified system. The project
  was highly criticised in Hungary for its ecological impact, and in 1989 Hungary stopped work on its territory
  pending further assessment. Czechoslovakia investigated alternatives and decided it could divert the Danube
  into its territory. In May 1992 Hungary transmitted a note verbale unilaterally terminating the treaty; in
  October 1992 Czechoslovakia began work to dam the river.
issue: |
  Whether Hungary breached the treaty, and whether Czechoslovakia's diversion of the Danube was a lawful
  countermeasure; the Court also considered the plea of necessity (paras 49–55).
held: |
  Hungary's refusal to complete the project was a breach of its obligations under the treaty. But an important
  consideration in weighing the lawfulness of countermeasures is their effect: by unilaterally assuming control
  of a shared resource, Czechoslovakia deprived Hungary of its right to an equitable share of the Danube, and
  with the ecological impact the diversion was disproportionate — not a lawful countermeasure. A plea of
  necessity can only be invoked exceptionally, requiring an essential interest, a grave and imminent peril,
  that breach was the only way to safeguard it, no serious impairment of another state's essential interest,
  and no contribution by the invoking state.
ratio: |
  Countermeasures must be proportionate to be lawful; necessity is exceptional and its conditions are cumulative.
significance: |
  The lecture's leading illustration of countermeasures (art 22, proportionality in art 51) and of the
  narrow construction of necessity (art 25).
