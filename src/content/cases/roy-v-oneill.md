facts: |-
  A police officer attended a unit to check compliance with a domestic violence order protecting one
  occupant. The officer entered the curtilage of the premises, approached the front door, and while there
  required the other occupant, Ms Roy, to provide a breath sample; she returned a positive reading and was
  charged. Ms Roy argued the officer's purpose was outside any implied licence, and that even if a licence
  existed it ended once he asked for the breath sample.
issue: Whether the officer's entry onto the curtilage, and his conduct there, fell outside any implied licence to enter, making him a trespasser.
held: |-
  The appeal was dismissed; the officer was not a trespasser. The implied licence to approach the front
  door for lawful communication included checking on the wellbeing of the protected occupant, and was not
  negated merely because the officer also had a further purpose (checking DVO compliance) in mind.
ratio: A person who enters for a purpose within an implied licence does not become a trespasser merely because they also have another, alien purpose in mind; an entrant becomes a trespasser only where their sole purpose is outside the licence, the licence has been revoked and they remain, or they act in a manner inconsistent with the licence.
significance: Confirms and extends Halliday v Nevill — a mixed purpose does not defeat the implied licence so long as one purpose is within it.
why-here: The central case for implied-licence problems involving an entrant (often police) with more than one purpose.
