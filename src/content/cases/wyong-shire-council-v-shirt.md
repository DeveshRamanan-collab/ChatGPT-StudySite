facts: While water skiing, Shirt struck his head on the bottom of a shallow lake and became quadriplegic; the water was only about 3.5–4 feet deep at that point. He alleged he was misled by a "deep water" sign, which the Council had erected to warn swimmers about deeper channels alongside a jetty.
issue: Whether the Council breached its duty of care — and what the common law test for breach requires of a risk that is unlikely to occur.
held: At first instance a jury found that the Council breached its duty of care. The High Court found the jury's finding on breach was not unreasonable, and upheld the decision.
ratio: "The common law test for breach has two limbs: (1) reasonable foreseeability of a real risk of injury arising from the defendant's conduct, and (2) the reasonableness of the defendant's response to that risk. Mason J: \"foreseeability of the risk of injury and the likelihood of that risk occurring are two different things … A risk of injury which is quite unlikely to occur … may nevertheless be plainly foreseeable\"; a risk which is not far-fetched or fanciful is real and therefore foreseeable. A reasonable man might well have concluded that the sign was ambiguous and could induce a water skier to ski in that zone, mistakenly believing it to be deep."
significance: The common law test for breach. Its first limb corresponds to s 5B(1)(a); its "not far-fetched or fanciful" probability threshold was raised by the Civil Liability Act to "not insignificant" (s 5B(1)(b)). The flowchart and the slides both say it remains good authority.
why-here: Cite it with s 5B for what "foreseeable" means, and to keep foreseeability of a risk separate from its likelihood.
