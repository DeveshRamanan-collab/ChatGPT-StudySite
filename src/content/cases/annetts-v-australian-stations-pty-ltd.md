facts: |
  The plaintiffs allowed their 16-year-old son to work as a jackaroo only after the employer promised he
  would be constantly supervised. Seven weeks later the employer sent him alone into a remote property; he
  went missing, and the parents learned over four months of his disappearance and eventual death from
  dehydration in the desert.
issue: |
  Whether the employer owed the boy's parents a duty of care to avoid causing them psychiatric injury from
  his death.
held: |
  The High Court (heard together with Tame) held that the employer owed the parents a duty of care; it
  rejected sudden shock and direct perception as rigid preconditions, relying instead on the pre-existing
  relationship between the parties and the employer's assurance of supervision.
ratio: |
  A duty to avoid psychiatric harm can exist without sudden shock or direct perception where there is a
  pre-existing relationship between plaintiff and defendant, making the class of potential plaintiffs
  determinate.
significance: |
  Shows a duty for psychiatric harm can be grounded in a specific relationship and assumption of
  responsibility, not only in witnessing a shocking event.
why-here: Used to contrast with Tame — the same foreseeability test, opposite outcome, because of the pre-existing relationship and assurance given here.
