facts: |
  Consumers contracted Hepatitis A after eating oysters grown and distributed by Graham Barclay from Wallis
  Lake, NSW, after the lake was contaminated by faecal matter following a storm; the "depuration" process
  used only reduced, but did not eliminate, the viral load.
issue: |
  Whether the oyster grower/distributor, the local council and the NSW government owed consumers a duty of
  care in relation to the contamination, and whether any such duty was breached.
held: |
  The High Court, by majority, held that the NSW government did not owe a duty of care to consumers, and
  that although Graham Barclay did owe a duty of care, that duty was not breached.
ratio: |
  Even where a risk of harm to consumers is reasonably foreseeable and of significant magnitude, there is no
  breach if the probability of the risk occurring does not justify the difficult, expensive and inconvenient
  precautions that would be needed to eliminate it entirely.
significance: |
  Applies ordinary negligence and product-liability reasoning to a modern food-safety supply chain, and
  shows breach turning on a cost-benefit assessment of precautions, not mere foreseeability.
why-here: The key modern product-liability case for showing that foreseeability of harm does not automatically mean the standard of care has been breached.
