plain: The law rarely makes you liable for failing to act, or for what someone else does, unless you knew of the danger and had control over it.
concrete: Lightning struck a red gum on Goldman's farm in Western Australia. He had the tree felled but left the fire to burn itself out. The weather turned, the fire revived and spread to the Hargraves' properties. Because the harm came from an omission, foreseeability alone was not enough; the duty question turned on the occupier–neighbour relationship, Goldman's knowledge of the fire and his control over it.
formal: "Where physical harm is caused by an omission, more than reasonable foreseeability is required (Hargrave v Goldman: special relationship, knowledge, control). Generally there is no duty to rescue (Stuart v Kirkland-Veenstra). An occupier's duty rests on \"control over, and knowledge of, the state of the premises\" and did not extend to third parties' crime it could not control (Modbury Triangle v Anzil). CLA ss 55–58 protect good samaritans."

terms:
  - term: Omission
    gloss: A failure to act. Where it causes physical harm, more than reasonable foreseeability is needed to found a duty.
  - term: No duty to rescue
    gloss: The general rule that a person need not act to save another; police owed no duty to a man apparently preparing to take his own life (Stuart v Kirkland-Veenstra).
  - term: Control
    gloss: The defendant's practical power over the source of the harm; central to duty for omissions and for third parties' conduct.
  - term: Good samaritan (s 56)
    gloss: A person who, in good faith and without expecting payment or other reward, assists someone apparently injured or at risk of being injured.
misconceptions:
  - claim: An occupier must protect lawful entrants from any criminal attack on its premises.
    tempting: An occupier's duty does include taking reasonable care to protect entrants from third parties' conduct.
    correction: "Modbury Triangle v Anzil: the centre had no control over the attackers, and neither reliance nor assumption of responsibility was made out, so its duty did not extend to their criminal conduct."
  - claim: If you see someone in danger and could easily help, the law requires you to.
    tempting: Most people think, morally, that a bystander should help.
    correction: "Generally there is no legal duty to rescue: the High Court held police owed no duty to a person apparently preparing to take his own life (Stuart v Kirkland-Veenstra)."
  - claim: A good samaritan who makes a careless mistake while helping is liable for it.
    tempting: Negligence normally attracts liability, whoever commits it.
    correction: "Section 57 CLA: no personal liability for acts or omissions in an emergency while assisting, unless s 58 applies (the helper caused the risk, or was significantly impaired by alcohol or drugs)."

what-if:
  - change: In Modbury, there had been repeated attacks in the car park after the lights went off.
    effect: Gleeson CJ said a duty to take reasonable steps may arise where criminal conduct has a high degree of foreseeability and predictability; past attacks would strengthen that argument.
  - change: In Hargrave v Goldman, Goldman had not known that lightning had set the tree alight.
    effect: Knowledge of the danger would be missing, and for an omission foreseeability alone is not enough, so the case for a duty would be much weaker.
  - change: A helper at a car crash had caused the crash herself, then carelessly worsened the victim's injuries while assisting.
    effect: "Section 58(1): good samaritan protection does not apply where the helper's own intentional or negligent act or omission caused the injury or risk she responded to."
