plain: Public nuisance is an unlawful act or omission that endangers the public or blocks a right everyone shares. It is a crime, but an individual can sue if she suffered special damage.
concrete: "In Walsh v Ervin [1952] VLR 361 a farmer ploughed and planted crops on a public highway and put up gates that stopped the plaintiff driving onto his own land. Obstructing a highway is a classic public nuisance. The court held the plaintiff's special damage need not be money: delay and inconvenience of a substantial kind, different from what the rest of the public suffered, could be enough."
formal: "Public nuisance is an unlawful act or omission which (i) endangers the lives, safety, property or comfort of the public or (ii) obstructs the enjoyment of a right common to all. It is a common law crime; a tort action requires title to sue (special or particular damage, not limited to pecuniary loss: Walsh v Ervin), interference with a public right, and substantial and unreasonable interference."

terms:
  - term: Special or particular damage
    gloss: Damage to the plaintiff different from the public's generally; not limited to pecuniary loss — substantial delay and inconvenience can qualify (Walsh v Ervin).
  - term: Public right
    gloss: A right common to all, such as using a highway. The general public must be affected by the alleged nuisance.
  - term: Substantial and unreasonable
    gloss: Not every annoyance, discomfort or inconvenience is public nuisance; a temporary obstruction of a highway may fall short.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Anyone inconvenienced by a public nuisance can sue.
    tempting: Everyone affected seems to be a victim.
    correction: Public nuisance is a common law crime; an individual's tort action needs special or particular damage, different from the public's generally (Lecture 1, slides 21–22).
  - claim: Special damage means financial loss.
    tempting: "\"Damage\" sounds like money."
    correction: "Walsh v Ervin: special damage is not limited to pecuniary loss; substantial delay and inconvenience, dissimilar to the public's, may be enough (slide 24)."
  - claim: Any obstruction of a road is a public nuisance.
    tempting: Obstructing a highway is the classic example.
    correction: The interference must be substantial and unreasonable; not every annoyance or inconvenience counts, and a temporary obstruction of a highway may not (slide 26).

    answer: The public's right to use the highway — a right common to all, so a classic public nuisance.
  - ask: Who should ordinarily deal with that?
    hint: Public nuisance is a crime.
    answer: "The community, through the state: addressing public nuisance is generally the state's responsibility."
  - ask: What let this plaintiff sue privately?

    answer: "Special or particular damage: the obstruction stopped his own vehicle access to his land."
  - ask: Did it matter that he may have lost no money?
    hint: What did the court say about pecuniary loss?
    answer: No. Special damage is not limited to pecuniary loss; substantial delay and inconvenience dissimilar to the public's may suffice.
  - ask: Would a road blocked for ten minutes by a delivery truck be enough?
    hint: Substantial and unreasonable.
    answer: Probably not. Not every inconvenience is public nuisance, and a temporary obstruction of a highway may fall short.
elements:
  - name: Title to sue
    test: Special or particular damage, different from that suffered by the public generally; not limited to pecuniary loss (Walsh v Ervin).
    cases:
      - walsh-v-ervin
  - name: Interference with a public right
    test: Endangering the public's lives, safety, property or comfort, or obstructing a right common to all. The general public must be affected.
  - name: Substantial and unreasonable
    test: Not every annoyance, discomfort or inconvenience; a temporary obstruction of a highway may not be enough.
