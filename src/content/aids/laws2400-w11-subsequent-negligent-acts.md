plain: When someone else's carelessness, or the plaintiff's own, follows the defendant's negligence, the chain does not automatically break. It holds most firmly where the defendant created the very risk that materialised.
concrete: "Early one morning the defendant's truck was parked in the middle of a six-lane road to unload fruit and vegetables. The plaintiff, drunk and speeding, drove into it. His own negligence did not break the chain of causation: as Mason CJ put it, the defendant's wrongful conduct had 'generated the very risk of injury resulting from the negligence of the plaintiff or a third party'."
formal: Whether a subsequent negligent act severs the causal connection is a matter of 'fact and degree' (Mahony v J Kruschich (Demolitions) Pty Ltd). Negligence of a third party or even the plaintiff does not necessarily sever the chain (Mahony; Burns v Pearce), especially where the defendant's wrongful conduct generated the very risk of injury and that injury occurs in the ordinary course of things (March v Stramare).
terms:
  - term: Fact and degree
    gloss: "How Mahony describes the question whether later negligence severs the chain: decided on the facts of each case, not by a fixed rule."
  - term: The very risk
    gloss: The risk of injury the defendant's wrongful conduct itself generated. Later negligence that realises it, in the ordinary course of things, does not usually break the chain.
misconceptions:
  - claim: A later negligent act by a third party always breaks the chain.
    tempting: The third party is the immediate cause of the injury.
    correction: Negligence of a third party, or even the plaintiff, does not necessarily sever the chain (Mahony; Burns v Pearce). It is a matter of fact and degree.
  - claim: The plaintiff's own carelessness after the breach always takes the injury outside the scope of liability.
    tempting: It seems fair to blame the plaintiff for their own carelessness.
    correction: Even the plaintiff's negligence does not necessarily sever the chain. In March v Stramare the drunk, speeding plaintiff's negligence did not.
  - claim: A careless later act should be tested as voluntary human action, which ordinarily severs the chain.
    tempting: Carelessness is still something the person chose to do.
    correction: "Later negligence has its own branch: fact and degree (Mahony). The voluntary-act test is for deliberate, volitional intervention."

  - name: Fact and degree
