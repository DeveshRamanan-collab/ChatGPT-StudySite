plain: "Sometimes 'but for' fails unfairly: two causes are each enough on their own, or science cannot say which exposure caused the harm. The flowchart calls these the gap cases."
concrete: In Cook v Lewis (1961), hunters were shooting and a single bullet struck the plaintiff. The jury could not decide which of the two negligent hunters had fired it. Asked of each hunter separately, the 'but for' question cannot be answered against either, so both would escape. The Supreme Court of Canada refused that result and held both negligent defendants liable.
formal: "In March v Stramare Mason CJ noted three limits of 'but for': it cannot distinguish causes from necessary preconditions, it struggles with intervening events, and it fails with multiple sufficient causes. The Negligence Review Panel made no recommendation on these, so the common law applies. The flowchart's appropriate cases for s 5D(2): multiple sufficient causes (Bonnington; March) and material contribution to harm (Fairchild; McGee; Amaca v Ellis; Henville)."
terms:
  - term: Multiple sufficient causes
    gloss: Two or more events, each of which is sufficient on its own to cause the harm.
  - term: Market share liability
    gloss: "One solution courts have used where several defendants were potentially negligent: each pays a portion of the compensation in line with its market share."
  - term: Material contribution to harm (flowchart)
    gloss: Negligent conduct that materially increases the risk of harm where the state of scientific or medical knowledge makes it impossible to prove the cause.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Multiple sufficient causes means two events that were both needed for the harm, like the parked truck and the drunk driver.
    tempting: March v Stramare involved two negligent actors.
    correction: In March both acts were necessary. Multiple sufficient causes means two or more events, each of which is sufficient on its own to cause the harm.
  - claim: Section 5D(2) expressly leaves multiple sufficient causes to the common law.
    tempting: Section 5D(2) refers to 'established principles'.
    correction: Common law principles continue to apply because the Negligence Review Panel made no recommendations on how to deal with multiple sufficient causes.
  - claim: Material contribution to harm and increased risk of harm are the same thing.
    tempting: The flowchart describes material contribution as conduct that 'materially increases the risk of harm'.
    correction: "The lectures keep them apart: increased risk is not enough (Amaca v Ellis); material contribution is causal and must be more than negligible (Bonnington). Use the lecture's terms."

    hint: Would the walker have died anyway?
    answer: "Yes: the other shot would have killed him. So 'but for' clears the first hunter and, by the same reasoning, the second."
  - ask: Is that an acceptable result?
    hint: Two negligent hunters, one dead walker, nobody liable?
    answer: No. It does not make sense; in Cook v Lewis the court held both negligent hunters liable when it could not tell whose bullet struck.
  - ask: Several makers produced the same defective device, and the plaintiff cannot say whose device injured her. What have courts done?
    hint: Think of each maker's share of sales.
    answer: "Market share liability: each defendant pays a portion of the compensation in line with its market share."
  - ask: Why does the common law, not the CLA, govern multiple sufficient causes?
    hint: What did the Negligence Review Panel recommend?
    answer: "Nothing: the Panel made no recommendations on how to deal with them, so common law principles continue to apply."
  - ask: On the flowchart, where do the two kinds of gap case lead?
    hint: One subsection of s 5D.
    answer: To s 5D(2), which can establish factual causation in an appropriate (in NSW, exceptional) case, where there is also a clear breach and an evidentiary gap.
