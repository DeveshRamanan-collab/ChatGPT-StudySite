plain: Assault is deliberately making someone reasonably expect that unwanted physical contact is about to happen. No touching is needed, and fear is not required.
concrete: In Zanker v Vartzokas (1988) 34 A Crim R 11, a girl accepted a lift in the defendant's van. After she declined his offer of money for sexual favours, he said he would take her to his mate's house, where the mate would "really fix you up". The harm lay at another place, later — but she was in his van, and the court held the threat sufficiently imminent in the circumstances.
formal: Assault is a direct and intentional act by the defendant which creates in the plaintiff a reasonable apprehension of an imminent physical interference with their person. The intention is to cause apprehension, not to commit the battery (Rixon v Star City [56]–[59]). Apprehension is judged objectively; fear is unnecessary; words can suffice. Imminent need not mean immediate (Zanker v Vartzokas).
terms:
  - term: Apprehension
    gloss: Anticipating physical contact in the expectation it will take place. Fear is not necessary; the test is what a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position would apprehend.
  - term: Imminence
    gloss: There must be at least the possibility of physical contact, and soon — but imminent does not necessarily mean immediate (Zanker v Vartzokas).
  - term: Conditional threat
    gloss: A threat of violence unless the plaintiff complies can be assault if it creates apprehension of imminent violence — but not if the defendant is entitled to use that force.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Assault requires that the defendant intended to carry out the battery.
    tempting: A threat seems hollow if there was no plan to follow through.
    correction: The intention required is to cause apprehension of the battery, not to commit it (Rixon v Star City [56]–[59]). Pointing an unloaded gun can be assault.
  - claim: The plaintiff must have felt afraid.
    tempting: Being threatened usually feels like fear.
    correction: "Fear is not necessary. The question is apprehension, tested objectively: would a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position have apprehended the threat? (Lecture 1, slide 16)."
  - claim: "\"Imminent\" means the contact must be immediate."
    tempting: In everyday speech, imminent and immediate sound the same.
    correction: "Imminence does not necessarily mean immediate: in Zanker v Vartzokas, harm threatened at a later destination was imminent because she could not escape the van (slide 17)."
socratic:
  - ask: In Zanker, when and where was the threatened harm to happen?
    hint: At his mate's house.
    answer: Later, at another place — not at that moment.
  - ask: Why was the threat still imminent?
    hint: Could she get away in the meantime?
    answer: She was in his van with no effective way out before the threat could be carried out, so imminence did not require immediacy.
  - ask: Did she need to be afraid?
    hint: Slide 16 on fear.
    answer: No. Only apprehension is required, and it must be one a reasonable person in her position would have.
  - ask: Did the defendant have to intend to carry out the threat?
    hint: Rixon v Star City [56]–[59].
    answer: No. He needed only to intend to create in her an apprehension of imminent harmful or offensive contact.
  - ask: Can words alone be enough?
    hint: Slide 16.
    answer: Yes, if they directly raise in the plaintiff's mind the apprehension of interference. Zanker's threat was spoken words.
elements:
  - name: Positive act
    test: A positive act by the defendant, which may be mere words or a gesture.
  - name: Direct interference
    test: The apprehension must follow directly from the defendant's act.
  - name: Intentional act
    test: Intention to create apprehension of a battery, not to commit it (Rixon). The weight of Australian authority requires intention; but see the dicta in NSW v McMaster.
    cases:
      - rixon-v-star-city-pty-ltd
      - nsw-v-mcmaster
  - name: Reasonable apprehension of imminent contact
    test: Judged objectively; fear unnecessary; at least the possibility of contact. Imminent need not be immediate (Zanker v Vartzokas).
    cases:
      - zanker-v-vartzokas
  - name: No lawful justification
    test: The act must be without lawful justification.
