plain: "When a relationship is new to the law, foreseeability alone is not enough: courts weigh features of the relationship, step by step, to decide whether a duty exists."
concrete: Mr Stavar was exposed to asbestos while working at an oil refinery. His wife washed his contaminated work clothes and, exposed to the dust, developed cancer. An employer's duty to its employee was established, but a duty to the employee's wife was not, so the case was novel. Weighing the salient features, the NSW Court of Appeal in Caltex Refineries v Stavar held that a duty of care could be found.
formal: "In novel cases more than reasonable foreseeability is required; Australian courts take an incremental approach and weigh the salient features (Caltex Refineries v Stavar): foreseeability, control, vulnerability, reliance, assumption of responsibility, proximity, knowledge, indeterminacy of liability, coherence with statute and the common law, among others. The list is non-exhaustive and need not all be met. Policy can negate a duty (Sullivan v Moody)."

terms:
  - term: Novel case
    gloss: "A case outside the established categories: reasonable foreseeability is necessary but not enough."
  - term: Salient features
    gloss: Non-exhaustive factors about the parties' relationship (control, vulnerability, reliance, coherence and more) weighed to decide a duty in novel cases.
  - term: Incremental approach
    gloss: Extending duty cautiously, by analogy with decided cases, rather than by one general test.
  - term: Coherence
    gloss: Whether a duty would fit with statutes and other legal principles rather than conflict with them (Sullivan v Moody).
misconceptions:
  - claim: The plaintiff must satisfy every salient feature to establish a duty.
    tempting: Caltex Refineries v Stavar sets out a long list that looks like a checklist.
    correction: The list is not exhaustive and the plaintiff is not required to meet all the factors; they are weighed together.
  - claim: If harm to the plaintiff was obviously foreseeable, a duty must be owed.
    tempting: Foreseeability is the heart of the neighbour principle.
    correction: "Sullivan v Moody: if mere foreseeability entailed liability, it would impose an intolerable burden and subvert other legal principles. Coherence and indeterminacy defeated the father's claim."
  - claim: Salient features are the general Australian test for every duty question.
    tempting: The High Court has followed the salient factors approach since the late 1990s.
    correction: "It is a tool for specific circumstances: novel cases and non-paradigm situations such as omissions, pure mental harm, pure economic loss and public authorities. Most cases are established categories."

elements:
  - name: "Step 1: compensable harm"
    test: Is the plaintiff's harm compensable? Physical injury ordinarily yes; psychiatric injury and economic loss only within further limits.
  - name: "Step 2: reasonable foreseeability"
    test: Was injury to the plaintiff or their class reasonably foreseeable, and the type of injury suffered too?
    cases:
      - chapman-v-hearse
      - mount-isa-mines-ltd-v-pusey
  - name: "Step 3: salient features"
    test: Weigh control of the risk, vulnerability, reliance, assumption of responsibility, proximity, category of relationship and the defendant's knowledge of harm.
    cases:
      - caltex-refineries-pty-ltd-v-stavar
  - name: "Step 4: policy negating a duty"
    test: Would a duty create indeterminate liability, impose on autonomy, conflict with other duties or statute, or threaten the structure of the common law?
    cases:
      - sullivan-v-moody
      - tame-v-new-south-wales
