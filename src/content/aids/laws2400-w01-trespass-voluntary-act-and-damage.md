plain: For trespass the defendant must have done a positive, willed act — not a fit, a push by someone else, or an omission. But the plaintiff need not prove any damage.
concrete: "The text (p 45) gives three things that are not positive acts: an act during an epileptic fit, an act caused by the force of a third party, and a mere omission to act. So a person shoved into someone else by a third party has not committed trespass, however directly the contact happened. Where there is a positive act, though, no damage need be proved."
formal: "In trespass the act must be a \"positive act\"; a mere passive act will not suffice (an epileptic fit, the force of a third party and an omission are not positive acts: text p 45). Trespass is actionable per se: no proof of damage is required, to protect bodily integrity, liberty and property. In an action on the case the plaintiff must establish damage."
terms:
  - term: Positive act
    gloss: Conduct the defendant actually performs; a merely passive act, an act during an epileptic fit, or one caused by a third party's force does not qualify.
  - term: Omission
    gloss: A failure to act. Not a positive act, so it cannot satisfy the voluntary act element of trespass (text p 45).
  - term: Actionable per se
    gloss: "Actionable without proof of damage: the interference itself founds the claim. True of trespass, not of an action on the case."
misconceptions:
  - claim: If the plaintiff was hit directly, the voluntary act element is met.
    tempting: Directness and a physical act seem to go together.
    correction: They are separate elements. An act during a seizure or one caused by a third party's push is not a positive act, however direct the contact (Lecture 2, slide 20; text p 45).
  - claim: No damage, no trespass claim.
    tempting: Most claims need a loss before there is anything to compensate.
    correction: "Trespass is actionable per se: no proof of damage is required, to protect bodily integrity, liberty and property. Only an action on the case needs damage (Lecture 2, slide 22)."
  - claim: "\"I didn't mean any harm\" defeats the voluntary act element."
    tempting: Not intending harm sounds like not acting voluntarily.
    correction: The voluntary act element asks only whether the act itself was positively performed. The defendant's state of mind belongs to the separate fault element, where intention need only go to the act.

    hint: Whose force moved A?
    answer: No. An act caused by the force of a third party is not a positive act, so this element fails against A.
  - ask: What other conduct is not a positive act?
    hint: The text (p 45) gives three examples.
    answer: An act during an epileptic fit, an act caused by a third party's force, and an omission to act.
  - ask: D deliberately taps B on the shoulder; B suffers no injury or loss. Is anything missing for trespass?
    hint: Does trespass need damage?
    answer: No. The tap is a positive act, and trespass is actionable per se, so B need not prove any harm.
  - ask: Why does the law let B sue without a loss?
    hint: What does trespass protect?
    answer: Its rationale is protecting bodily integrity, liberty and property from direct interference, whether or not measurable loss follows.
  - ask: Would that change in an action on the case?
    hint: Compare slide 22.
    answer: Yes. In an action on the case the plaintiff must establish that she suffered damage.

    effect: No positive act, so trespass fails on the voluntary act element, even though the contact was direct and no damage need be proved.
  - change: A trespasser walks across a lawn but causes no visible damage.
    effect: Trespass to land can still succeed because it is actionable per se. In an action on the case the absence of damage would be fatal.
  - change: The defendant does nothing — he simply fails to act.
    effect: An omission to act is not a positive act, so the voluntary act element of trespass is not met.
