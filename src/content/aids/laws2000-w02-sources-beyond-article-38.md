plain: Some rules bind states even though Article 38 doesn't list them — public promises, peremptory norms and some decisions of international organisations — and non-binding soft law still shapes the law.
concrete: "In 1919 Norway's foreign minister, Mr Ihlen, told the Danish minister that Norway \"would not make any difficulty\" over Denmark's claim to all of Greenland. When Norway later occupied part of Eastern Greenland, the Permanent Court of International Justice held the promise \"unconditional and definitive\": Norway was obliged not to contest Danish sovereignty over Greenland as a whole, and so not to occupy any part of it."
formal: "Unilateral declarations intended to bind create legal undertakings; no quid pro quo or acceptance is required (Nuclear Tests [1974]). Jus cogens: a norm \"accepted and recognized by the international community of States as a whole\" from which \"no derogation is permitted\" (VCLT art 53). Members \"agree to accept and carry out the decisions of the Security Council\" (UN Charter art 25). Soft law is not an independent source."
terms:
  - term: unilateral statement
    gloss: A public declaration by a state's representative, made with the intention to be bound, creating a legal obligation without any reciprocal promise or acceptance.
  - term: jus cogens (peremptory norm)
    gloss: A norm accepted by the community of states as a whole from which no derogation is permitted, even by treaty (VCLT art 53).
    example: "ILC examples: genocide, crimes against humanity, aggression, slavery, torture and self-determination."
  - term: soft law
    gloss: Non-legally binding instruments, such as General Assembly declarations, that can evidence practice or opinio juris, aid interpretation, or lead to treaties.
  - term: hard law
    gloss: The legally binding sources, such as treaties and custom, contrasted with soft law.
misconceptions:
  - claim: A unilateral statement binds only once the other state accepts it.
    tempting: Most binding promises, like contracts, need acceptance.
    correction: "Nuclear Tests: no quid pro quo, acceptance, reply or reaction from other states is required; the declaring state's intention to be bound is enough."
  - claim: General Assembly declarations bind because almost every state votes for them.
    tempting: Wide support looks like consent.
    correction: Soft law is not an independent source. It can evidence practice or opinio juris and lead to custom or treaties — the UDHR led to the ICCPR and ICESCR.
  - claim: A treaty provision that breaches jus cogens is simply cut out and the rest of the treaty survives.
    tempting: Domestic courts often sever invalid clauses.
    correction: The lecture says no treaty can contain even one provision violating jus cogens; such a treaty will be terminated.

  - ask: Could two states validly agree by treaty to permit torture between them?
    hint: VCLT art 53.
    answer: No. The prohibition of torture is jus cogens; no derogation is permitted, and such a treaty would be terminated.
  - ask: How can a Security Council decision bind a state that voted against it?
    hint: Look at the treaty the state already ratified.
    answer: Under Article 25 of the UN Charter, members agreed in advance to accept and carry out Council decisions.
  - ask: Is a General Assembly declaration law?
    hint: Hard law or soft law?
    answer: No, it is soft law. But it can evidence practice or opinio juris and lead to custom or new treaties.
