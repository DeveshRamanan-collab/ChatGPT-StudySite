plain: "Custom usually binds every state, with two exceptions: a custom may bind only a region or even two states, and a state that objected from the start is not bound."
concrete: Portugal held a small enclave inside India, reachable only by a route through Indian territory. For centuries Portugal had used that route. When India denied any right of passage, the ICJ looked at the long practice and found a customary right of passage binding as between Portugal and India — even though no treaty between them created one.
formal: "Particular custom: a customary rule may bind only a group of states, regional or even bilateral (Right of Passage over Indian Territory [1960] ICJ Rep 6; ILC Conclusion 16). Persistent objector: a state that consistently objects to a rule from the beginning of its formation is not bound by it (Fisheries [1951] ICJ Rep 116; ILC Conclusion 15). This reconciles custom with consent."

terms:
  - term: persistent objector
    gloss: A state that has consistently objected to a customary rule from the beginning of its formation, and so is not bound by it.
  - term: particular custom
    gloss: "A customary rule binding only a limited group of states: a region, or even just two states."
  - term: crystallise
    gloss: When practice plus opinio juris becomes an established customary rule; a persistent objection must come before this point.
misconceptions:
  - claim: A state can opt out of a customary rule whenever it becomes inconvenient.
    tempting: International law rests on consent, so withdrawing consent seems enough.
    correction: The objection must start at the beginning of the rule's formation and be maintained consistently; objecting midway does not work. That is why the rule is rarely invoked successfully.
  - claim: Customary law needs many states; it cannot exist between just two.
    tempting: Custom is defined as general practice among states.
    correction: "Right of Passage: centuries of practice created a customary right binding only Portugal and India, with no treaty between them."
  - claim: In Fisheries the Court held Norway was a persistent objector to an established 10-mile rule.
    tempting: The case is the leading persistent objector authority.
    correction: The Court found the 10-mile rule was not custom at all; it added that even if it were, Norway, having always opposed it, would not be bound.
