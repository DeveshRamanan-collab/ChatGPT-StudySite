plain: Court decisions and expert writings don't make international law; they help show what the law is, and an ICJ judgment formally binds only the states in that case.
concrete: "The lecturer's example: if Australia and France litigate before the ICJ, the judgment binds those two states in that case. If Australia later sues another state on similar facts and similar law, the first judgment does not bind the new case. Still, ICJ judgments cite earlier rulings in almost every paragraph, and states invoke ICJ cases as bargaining chips when negotiating settlements."
formal: "Article 59 ICJ Statute: \"The decision of the Court has no binding force except between the parties and in respect of that particular case.\" Judicial decisions and \"the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists\" (Art 38(1)(d)) are subsidiary, law-interpreting sources. Yet decisions carry substantial interpretative weight, and the ICJ rarely departs from its jurisprudence (Guillaume 2011)."

terms:
  - term: Article 59 ICJ Statute
    gloss: An ICJ decision binds only the parties and only in respect of that particular case — there is no binding precedent.
  - term: teachings of the most highly qualified publicists
    gloss: Writings of leading international law scholars — a subsidiary means under Article 38(1)(d) for clarifying ambiguous law.
  - term: International Law Commission
    gloss: UN body of elected, geographically representative experts on codification and progressive development; its draft articles, such as on state responsibility, are often cited by the ICJ.
  - term: ad hoc tribunals
    gloss: Courts set up by the Security Council for specific situations, such as the tribunals for the former Yugoslavia and Rwanda, then phased out.
misconceptions:
  - claim: ICJ decisions bind later cases as precedent, as in Australian courts.
    tempting: ICJ judgments cite earlier decisions in almost every paragraph.
    correction: "Article 59: a decision binds only the parties and that case, respecting consent to the Court's non-compulsory jurisdiction. Consistency is practice, not obligation."
  - claim: Because judgments are not binding precedent, they carry little weight.
    tempting: "'Non-binding' sounds optional."
    correction: They carry substantial interpretative weight; the ICJ rarely departs from its jurisprudence, for legitimacy, predictability and the peaceful settlement of disputes.
  - claim: ILC draft articles are binding law.
    tempting: Courts cite them constantly, especially the Draft Articles on State Responsibility.
    correction: They are subsidiary, non-binding expert work that helps interpret the law; they can become treaties if states adopt them.
