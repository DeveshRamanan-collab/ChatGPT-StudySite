plain: You can now sue for a recognised psychiatric illness caused by carelessness if a person in your position would foreseeably suffer it. At common law, rigid hurdles like "sudden shock" are gone.
concrete: Mr and Mrs Annetts let their 16-year-old son James work as a jackaroo only after the employer assured them he would be constantly supervised. Weeks later he was sent alone to a remote property, went missing, and died of dehydration in the desert. His parents learned of it by telephone over months and developed psychiatric illness. The High Court held the employer owed them a duty, with no sudden shock or direct perception.
formal: "For pure psychiatric harm, the plaintiff must show it was reasonably foreseeable that a person in their position would suffer a recognised psychiatric illness. In Tame v NSW; Annetts v Australian Stations, Gummow and Kirby JJ held that normal fortitude, sudden shock and direct perception are not \"a pre-condition to liability for negligently inflicted psychiatric harm\". Tame: not foreseeable, no duty. Annetts: relationship plus foreseeability, duty owed."
terms:
  - term: Nervous shock
    gloss: The traditional label for psychiatric harm, which was not recoverable.
  - term: Pure psychiatric harm
    gloss: Psychiatric harm suffered by a plaintiff who was not physically injured.
  - term: Control mechanisms
    gloss: Normal fortitude, sudden shock, and direct perception of the event or its aftermath. Tame/Annetts held none is a pre-condition to liability.
  - term: Recognised psychiatric illness
    gloss: A recognised form of psychiatric harm, as opposed to mere distress; required for recovery.

  - ask: An officer wrongly records Mrs Tame's blood alcohol as 0.14, then corrects it. Over a year later she develops psychotic depression on learning of it. Was that foreseeable?
    hint: Would a person in her position foreseeably develop a psychiatric illness?
    answer: No. Her reaction was extreme and idiosyncratic; the risk was far-fetched or fanciful, so no duty was owed.
  - ask: The Annetts neither saw their son die nor suffered a single sudden shock. Does that defeat their claim?
    hint: Are sudden shock and direct perception preconditions?
    answer: No. Tame/Annetts rejected both as pre-conditions to liability for negligently inflicted psychiatric harm.
  - ask: What made the parents' illness foreseeable and their claim contained?
    hint: What passed between them and the employer?
    answer: "A pre-existing relationship: they sought and obtained assurances that James would be supervised, so the class of plaintiffs was not indeterminate."
  - ask: Why have courts historically treated psychiatric harm differently from physical harm?
    hint: Gummow and Kirby JJ examined four reasons.
    answer: It is less objectively observable; litigation may discourage rehabilitation; liability may be indeterminate; and it may place an unreasonable or disproportionate burden on defendants.
  - ask: Does Tame alone still govern a NSW claim?
    hint: Part 3 of the CLA.
    answer: No. CLA ss 30–32 now govern; s 32 reflects Tame but expressly requires foreseeability judged against a person of normal fortitude.

    objection: "Feminist critique: claimants have often been women, so privileging physical injury over emotional harm affects women disproportionately, and the reasonable person test itself is problematic."
