plain: States must try to settle their disputes by peaceful means and never by force. They need not reach a settlement — only keep trying peacefully, by means they choose.
concrete: "Canada and Denmark (for Greenland) disputed Hans Island, a 1.3 square kilometre barren rock between Canada and Greenland, for about 50 years. Each side's forces planted flags and left bottles behind — Canadian whisky, Danish schnapps — in the so-called Whiskey War. Neither breached international law by failing to settle: they kept the dispute peaceful. In the end they agreed to share the island, with the border following a rift across it."
formal: "Art 2(3) UN Charter: \"All Members shall settle their international disputes by peaceful means in such a manner that international peace and security, and justice, are not endangered.\" It works with art 2(4). Reiterated in the OAS Charter, Friendly Relations Declaration (1970), Manila Declaration (1982) and AU Constitutive Act; binding in custom (Nicaragua, para 290). An obligation of conduct, not result. Art 33(1) lists the means."

    tempting: Art 2(3) says members "shall settle" their disputes.
    correction: "It is an obligation of conduct, not result: states are not obliged to reach any particular settlement, which is why some disputes last decades (Hans Island)."
  - claim: Once peaceful means have failed after long effort, force becomes permissible.
    tempting: Art 2(3) comes first and art 2(4) seems to follow from it.
    correction: The duty to keep trying peacefully continues while the dispute lasts, and the obligation not to use force (art 2(4)) always remains.

    correction: It is also binding under customary international law (Nicaragua, 1986, para 290), so it binds whether or not a state accepted the declarations.
