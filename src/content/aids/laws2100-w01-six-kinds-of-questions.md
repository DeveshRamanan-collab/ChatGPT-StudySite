plain: "Jurisprudence asks different kinds of question: doctrinal, normative, empirical, metatheoretical, methodological and conceptual. You can only judge a claim once you know which kind it is."
concrete: The lecture's examples. Solving a hypothetical is a cluster of doctrinal questions. Asking whether a family law arrangement is desirable is normative. Going out to see what a new statute actually did to a community is empirical. Asking how to choose between rival theories, which is not like picking an ice-cream flavour, is metatheoretical. Asking how to investigate the nature of law is methodological. 'What is a contract?' is conceptual.

terms:
  - term: Normative claim
    gloss: "A claim about how things ought to be (the is/ought distinction): justificatory if it defends the status quo, critical if it criticises it."
  - term: Empirical claim
    gloss: "A claim about the world: explanatory or causal (why something happens) or about effects (what a law actually does)."
  - term: Metatheoretical question
    gloss: "A question about theories themselves: what are the success criteria for a given theory?"
    example: Is Hart's theory really general and descriptive?
  - term: Methodological question
    gloss: How should we conduct research in this field? Settled in the natural sciences, harder in the social sciences, more debatable still in philosophy.
