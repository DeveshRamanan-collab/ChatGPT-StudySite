plain: It is unsettled whether customary international law automatically forms part of Australian law. Either way, it yields to statutes and settled common law, and a customary crime needs legislation before courts can try it.
concrete: "Members of an Aboriginal community claimed that sponsoring Native Title Act amendments, and failing to seek World Heritage listing of the Lake Eyre region, were acts of genocide. Australia was party to the Genocide Convention, but genocide was not yet a crime in Australian law. They argued genocide was part of customary international law, so Australian courts could try it anyway. The Federal Court disagreed: even accepting the prohibition is jus cogens, without legislation it had no effect in Australian law."
formal: "Transformation: custom becomes Australian law only when formally adopted by Parliament or the courts. Incorporation: custom is automatically a source of Australian law unless a rule is to the contrary. Australia has adopted neither clearly; but custom \"cannot generally be adopted where inconsistent with domestic legislation or essential common law doctrine\". Criminal norms need legislation (Nulyarimma v Thompson [1999] 96 FCR 153, Wilcox J). Teoh: no \"back door\" importation."
terms:
  - term: doctrine of transformation
    gloss: Custom becomes Australian law only when formally adopted by Parliament or by the courts as part of the common law.
  - term: doctrine of incorporation
    gloss: Custom is automatically a source of Australian law, unless there is a rule to the contrary.
  - term: jus cogens
    gloss: A peremptory norm of general international law, such as the prohibition of genocide — accepted as such in Nulyarimma, yet still not triable without legislation.
  - term: Merkel J's criteria
    gloss: "Dissent in Nulyarimma: general acceptance by nations; reception, not inconsistent with statute or declared common law; then a true declaration of the common law from when the rule was established."
misconceptions:
  - claim: Australia has clearly adopted the doctrine of incorporation for custom.
    tempting: Custom is often described as binding automatically, unlike treaties.
    correction: Australia has not clearly adopted either approach; both transformation and incorporation have been recognised in High Court decisions.
  - claim: Because the prohibition of genocide is jus cogens, Australian courts could try genocide without legislation.
    tempting: A peremptory norm sounds strong enough to apply anywhere.
    correction: "Nulyarimma: the Federal Court accepted the prohibition is jus cogens but held that, without implementing legislation, it had no effect on Australian law. Criminal norms need legislation."
  - claim: Courts can develop the common law to give effect to any treaty Australia has ratified.
    tempting: Brennan J said the common law may have to "march in step" with international law.
    correction: Influence, yes; importation, no. Judicial development "must not be seen as a back door means of importing an unincorporated convention into Australian law" (Mason CJ and Deane J, Teoh).

    hint: Transformation or incorporation?
    answer: How custom enters Australian law. Under transformation it needs adoption by Parliament or the courts; under incorporation it is automatically a source unless a contrary rule exists. Australia has not settled which.
  - ask: Does it matter that the prohibition of genocide is jus cogens?
    hint: What did the Federal Court accept?
    answer: No. The Court accepted the prohibition is jus cogens, but held that without implementing legislation it had no effect on Australian law.
  - ask: Why did Wilcox J treat a criminal rule differently from, say, foreign sovereign immunity?
    hint: Civil versus criminal norms.
    answer: Treating a civil rule as domestic law is one thing; letting a domestic court try and punish conduct that domestic law does not make punishable is another. A criminal norm needs legislation.
  - ask: For a civil customary rule that no statute contradicts, what test could counsel use?
    hint: Merkel J, dissenting.
    answer: General acceptance by the community of nations; reception — not inconsistent with statute or declared common law; and, once received, a true declaration of the common law as from the date the rule was established.
