plain: States are bound to respect human rights by treaties — nine core UN treaties and regional conventions — and by customary law, some of which, like the ban on torture, is jus cogens.
concrete: Take the prohibition of torture. It appears in the Convention against Torture (10 December 1984) and ICCPR art 7, but the slides also list torture among customary human rights norms — and some such norms are considered jus cogens, from which no derogation is permitted. So a state that never ratified the Convention against Torture is still bound not to torture.
formal: "Treaty law: the nine core instruments — ICERD (1965), ICCPR and ICESCR (1966), CEDAW (1979), CAT (1984), CRC (1989), Migrant Workers (1990), Enforced Disappearance and Disabilities (2006) — and regional treaties (ECHR, ACHR). Customary law: e.g. torture, genocide, racial discrimination; some are jus cogens. A treaty is void if it conflicts with a peremptory norm (VCLT art 53)."
terms:
  - term: core instruments
    gloss: The nine core UN human rights treaties, from ICERD (21 December 1965) to the Disabilities and Enforced Disappearance Conventions (December 2006).
  - term: customary human rights norm
    gloss: A human rights rule binding as custom, independently of treaty ratification — e.g. the prohibitions of torture, genocide and racial discrimination.
  - term: jus cogens
    gloss: A norm accepted and recognised by the international community as one from which no derogation is permitted, modifiable only by a later norm of the same character.
