plain: "A duty for careless words turns on two questions: did the speaker know (or ought it to have known) you would rely, and was it reasonable for you to rely?"
concrete: "Tepko, landowners planning a subdivision, needed a water-supply costing for their bank. The Water Board refused several times, then reluctantly gave a \"ball-park\" figure. The landowners showed it to the bank, the figure was heavily overstated, and the refinancing collapsed. The High Court found no duty: the Board neither knew nor ought to have known of that reliance, and relying on a reluctant, provisional estimate was not reasonable."
formal: "Shaddock (1981): duty found; it covers information as well as advice, with no separate requirement of special skill. San Sebastian (1986): a duty can arise without any request; Brennan J requires that the representor realise the representee will trust its special competence, that reliance be reasonable, and foreseeable loss. Tepko (2001): no duty; needed known reliance, an assumption of responsibility, or both."

terms:
  - term: Reasonable reliance
    gloss: The plaintiff actually relied on the statement and it was reasonable to do so, given its nature, the occasion and any warnings.
  - term: Knowledge of reliance
    gloss: "The defendant knew, or ought to have known, that the plaintiff would rely on the statement in this way: tested actually and objectively (Tepko)."
  - term: Statement of intention
    gloss: A statement about what someone plans to do, such as a proposed redevelopment scheme; relying on a mere intention was not reasonable (San Sebastian).
misconceptions:
  - claim: A council officer without planning expertise cannot owe a duty for wrong planning information.
    tempting: Lord Morris in Hedley Byrne spoke of a person with special skill.
    correction: "Shaddock: the duty covers information as well as advice, and there is no separate requirement of special skill. A known lack of a needed skill can still make reliance harder to prove."
  - claim: San Sebastian failed because the developers never asked the council for the information.
    tempting: Most misstatement duties begin with a request, as in Shaddock.
    correction: San Sebastian says a duty can arise without any request. The claim failed because the statement was one of intention, and relying on a mere intention was not reasonable, though the reliance was genuine.
  - claim: In Tepko the knowledge factor asks only what the Board actually knew.
    tempting: Knowledge sounds like the defendant's state of mind.
    correction: "The test is also objective: the Board \"neither knew nor should have known\" of the reliance. Known reliance, an assumption of responsibility, or both, were needed."

    hint: Where else could the purchaser get this information?
    answer: The council was the only source of the information, and the reliance was clear. The court suggested the phone call alone might not have been enough.
  - ask: The council officer had no special skill. Does that defeat the duty?
    hint: Shaddock's second proposition.
    answer: No. The duty covers information as well as advice, and there is no separate requirement that the speaker possess special skill.
  - ask: In San Sebastian, nobody asked the council anything. Can a duty still arise?
    hint: Think of inviting or intending reliance.
    answer: "Yes. A duty can arise without a request: known special competence, an assumption of responsibility, inviting or intending reliance, or an interest in the recipient acting."
  - ask: So why did the developers lose?

    answer: It was a statement of intention. Relying on a mere intention was not reasonable, even though the developers genuinely relied on it.
  - ask: In Tepko, the Board gave the figure reluctantly, after refusing several times. Which two features failed?
    hint: One about the Board, one about the landowners.

elements:
  - name: Kind of statement
    test: Was it information or advice (Shaddock), a statement of intention (San Sebastian) or a reluctant, provisional estimate (Tepko)? The answer shapes whether reliance was reasonable.

  - name: Knowledge of reliance
    test: Did the defendant know, or ought it to have known, that the plaintiff would rely on the statement in this way?
    cases:
      - tepko-pty-ltd-v-water-board
      - shaddock-and-associates-pty-ltd-v-parramatta-city-council
  - name: Assumption of responsibility
    test: Did the defendant assume responsibility, for example by inviting or intending reliance or professing special competence? Tepko needed known reliance, an assumption of responsibility, or both.

      - san-sebastian-pty-ltd-v-minister
      - tepko-pty-ltd-v-water-board

what-if:
  - change: In Shaddock, the council had given only the telephone answer, with no certificate.
    effect: "The duty is less certain: the court suggested the phone call alone might not have been enough. The certificate firmed up both the reliance and its reasonableness."
  - change: In Tepko, the Board had volunteered a firm figure knowing the bank would lend on it.
    effect: "Both failed features would now point towards a duty: known reliance, and reliance on a firm figure is more reasonable than on a reluctant ball-park estimate."
  - change: In San Sebastian, the council had stated as a fact that the scheme had been approved.
    effect: The statement would no longer be a mere intention, so reliance could be reasonable; with the council inviting reliance, a duty becomes arguable.
boundary:
  - text: "The Tepko dissenters would have found a duty: a statutory authority, with special knowledge, and serious business consequences."
    why: Good reasoning can reach a different conclusion on the same facts. The majority held that, without known reliance or an assumption of responsibility, those features were not enough.
  - text: A plaintiff knew the speaker lacked a skill the question plainly required.
    why: Shaddock removes special skill as a separate requirement, but here reliance becomes harder to prove, so a duty is less likely.
