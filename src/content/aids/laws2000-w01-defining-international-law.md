plain: International law is the set of rules states, and sometimes others, follow in dealing with one another, and a rule binds a state because that state agreed to it.
concrete: "Before 1884 there was no single reference line for telling the time. At the International Meridian Conference, countries agreed in a Final Act to use Greenwich as the primary meridian, adding or subtracting hours from there. The lecturer uses this to show that international law is \"everywhere\": states consented to a shared standard, and it now shapes how everyone sets a clock — alongside vehicle standards, telecommunications and the internet services used to deliver the lecture."
formal: In S.S. Lotus (1927) the PCIJ held that international law governs relations between independent states; binding rules "emanate from their own free will as expressed in conventions or by usages generally accepted as expressing principles of law", so "restrictions upon the independence of States cannot therefore be presumed". The AG's Department adds who it serves; the Practitioner's Guide stresses voluntary consent.

terms:
  - term: convention
    gloss: "In the Lotus passage, a treaty: a written agreement through which states express their consent to be bound."
    example: Asked what 'convention' meant in Lotus, the class answered 'treaty'.
  - term: usage
    gloss: In Lotus, general state practice accepted as expressing principles of law — what the unit calls customary international law.
  - term: subjects of international law
    gloss: "Those international law addresses: primarily states, also international organisations, and increasingly individuals (e.g. under international criminal and human rights law)."
  - term: Permanent Court of International Justice (PCIJ)
    gloss: The League of Nations' court (1920–1946), predecessor of the ICJ; its decisions, such as Lotus, are still cited.

    tempting: Law students expect a single settled definition, and the three read quite differently.
    correction: "The lecture treats all three as valid but partial: the AG's Department stresses subjects, the Practitioner's Guide voluntary consent, and Lotus the source of binding force."
  - claim: Under Lotus a state is restricted unless some rule permits what it is doing.
    tempting: Much domestic regulation works by permission, so presuming limits feels natural.
    correction: "Lotus says the reverse: \"restrictions upon the independence of States cannot therefore be presumed\". A state is free unless a convention or generally accepted usage binds it."
  - claim: International law only matters when states go to war or cross borders.
    tempting: News coverage of international law centres on violations and the use of force.
    correction: "The lecture stresses it is \"everywhere\": time zones (Meridian Conference 1884), vehicle standards (1998 regulations), telecommunications (ITU) and internet services all rest on it."
socratic:
  - ask: If there is no world parliament, who makes the rules of international law?
    hint: Think about who signs treaties and whose practice forms custom.
    answer: "States themselves: they are both the makers of international law and the subjects it binds."
  - ask: So where must a rule's binding force come from?
    hint: The PCIJ in Lotus used the phrase "their own free will".
    answer: From the states' own will — their consent, in Lotus' words "their own free will".
  - ask: In what two forms can that will be expressed, according to Lotus?
    hint: One is written down; the other is a pattern of behaviour.
    answer: Conventions (treaties) and usages generally accepted as expressing principles of law (custom).
  - ask: If neither a treaty nor a usage covers an act, is the state restricted?
    hint: Read the last sentence of the Lotus passage.
    answer: No. Restrictions on a state's independence "cannot therefore be presumed", so the state remains free to act.
  - ask: What does the AG's Department definition add that Lotus leaves out?
    hint: Look at whose relations the rules facilitate.
    answer: "Its addressees: states and international organisations, and in some cases individuals, groups and transnational companies."

what-if:
  - change: The state has ratified a treaty forbidding the conduct.
    effect: A convention now expresses its free will, so the restriction is consented to rather than presumed; under Lotus the state is bound.
  - change: The only rule relied on is a General Assembly declaration.
    effect: The lecture says the General Assembly cannot create binding obligations. Unless the declaration reflects a treaty or accepted usage, Lotus says no restriction is presumed.
  - change: No treaty applies, but states generally follow a practice they accept as law.
    effect: That is a "usage generally accepted as expressing principles of law" — custom — so a restriction can exist without any treaty.
