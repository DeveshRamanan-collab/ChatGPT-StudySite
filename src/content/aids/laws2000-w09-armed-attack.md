plain: Self-defence needs an "armed attack" — one of the gravest forms of force, judged by scale and effects. Every armed attack is a use of force, but not every use of force is one.
concrete: In Nicaragua (1986) the ICJ found the United States had used force unlawfully, including by mining Nicaragua's territorial waters and attacking naval and port facilities. But it separated the most grave forms of force — armed attacks — from less grave forms. Arming, funding and supplying the contras, and certain frontier incidents, stayed below the gravity threshold. So force can breach art 2(4) without giving the victim a right of self-defence.
formal: Art 51 permits self-defence only "if an armed attack occurs" — not synonymous with "use of force" in art 2(4). With no clear benchmark, the threshold depends on scale and effects (casualties, property damage). Nicaragua distinguished "the most grave forms of the use of force (those constituting an armed attack) from other less grave forms". Attacks on territory or military vessels may qualify; private ships do not (Oil Platforms).
