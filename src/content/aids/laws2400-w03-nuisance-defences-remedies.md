plain: "Nuisance defences include a 20-year prescriptive right, the plaintiff's conduct or consent, statutory authorisation and contributory negligence — but not \"coming to the nuisance\". Remedies: damages, injunction, or careful self-help."
formal: "Defences: prescriptive right (interference continued for more than 20 years); plaintiff's conduct or consent (the most pleaded), though coming to the nuisance is no defence; statutory authorisation, if the authorised activity could not be performed without the nuisance; contributory negligence. Remedies: damages (reasonably foreseeable harm), injunction (damages inadequate; recurrence), abatement (cautiously, reasonable methods)."
terms:
  - term: Prescriptive right
    gloss: A right to continue an interference that has continued for more than 20 years; a defence to nuisance.
  - term: Coming to the nuisance
    gloss: Moving in next to an existing nuisance. It is no defence that the plaintiff came to the nuisance.
  - term: Statutory authorisation
    gloss: A defence only where the authorised activity could not have been performed without committing the nuisance.
  - term: Abatement
    gloss: "Self-help for nuisance: an alternative to legal proceedings, used cautiously and only by reasonable methods."
misconceptions:
  - claim: Someone who moves in next to an existing factory cannot complain about its noise.
    tempting: They chose to live there knowing about it.
    correction: It is no defence that the plaintiff "came to the nuisance" (Lecture 2, slide 13).
  - claim: Holding a government permit is a complete defence to nuisance.
    tempting: The activity looks officially approved.
    correction: Statutory authorisation applies only if the authorised activity could not have been performed without committing the nuisance (Lecture 2, slide 13).
  - claim: Any harm flowing from a nuisance is recoverable.
    tempting: The defendant caused it, so should pay for all of it.
    correction: To claim damages for a type of harm, the plaintiff must show that type of harm was reasonably foreseeable (Lecture 2, slide 15).
socratic:
  - ask: A quarry's dust has drifted next door for 25 years. What defence might it raise?
    hint: Time.
    answer: "A prescriptive right: the interference has continued for more than 20 years."
  - ask: The neighbour bought the house knowing about the dust. Is that a defence?
    hint: Coming to the nuisance.
    answer: No. It is no defence that the plaintiff came to the nuisance.
  - ask: The quarry holds a government permit. Is that enough?
    hint: When does statutory authorisation work?
    answer: Only if the authorised quarrying could not have been carried out without committing the nuisance.
  - ask: What damages could the neighbour claim?
    hint: Three kinds of loss; one limit.
    answer: Damage to the land, financial loss (lost trade, lower property value) and loss of subjective enjoyment — for types of harm that were reasonably foreseeable.
  - ask: The dust will keep coming. Which remedy fits best?
    hint: Adequacy of damages; recurrence.
    answer: An injunction, normally granted where damages are not an adequate remedy, with the chance of recurrence a key factor.
elements:
  - name: Prescriptive right
    test: The interference complained of has continued for more than 20 years.
  - name: Plaintiff's conduct or consent
    test: The most pleaded defence. But it is no defence that the plaintiff came to the nuisance.
  - name: Statutory authorisation
    test: Only if the authorised activity could not have been performed without committing the nuisance.
  - name: Contributory negligence
    test: Another possible defence to nuisance.
  - name: Remedy
    test: Damages for reasonably foreseeable types of harm; an injunction where damages are inadequate (consider recurrence); or abatement, cautiously and by reasonable methods.
what-if:
  - change: The interference has continued for 15 years, not 25.
    effect: "No prescriptive right: the interference must have continued for more than 20 years."
  - change: The nuisance was a one-off event unlikely to recur.
    effect: An injunction is less likely, since recurrence is a key factor and damages may be adequate; damages would be the natural remedy.
  - change: To stop overhanging branches, the plaintiff cuts down the neighbour's whole tree.
    effect: Abatement must be cautious and use only reasonable methods; going beyond what is needed to abate the nuisance falls outside the remedy.
boundary:

  - text: A fall in the plaintiff's property value caused by the nuisance.
    why: Recoverable as financial loss (decline in property value), provided that type of harm was reasonably foreseeable.
transfer:
  - scenario: A couple buy a house beside a long-established late-night music venue, then sue over the noise. The venue says it holds a liquor licence and that the couple "knew what they were buying".
    answer: Coming to the nuisance is no defence. The licence helps only if it amounts to statutory authorisation — the authorised activity could not be carried out without the nuisance. If the noise has continued for more than 20 years, a prescriptive right may be argued. Because the noise recurs, an injunction may suit better than damages.
