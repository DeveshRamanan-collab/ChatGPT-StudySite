plain: Conversion is seriously dealing with someone's goods as if they were yours; detinue is wrongfully refusing to hand goods back after a clear demand. Neither needs the plaintiff to have the goods.
concrete: "In Bunnings Group Ltd v CHEP Australia Ltd (2011) NSWCA 342, Bunnings used timber pallets belonging to CHEP. Storing or displaying goods on the pallets as they were delivered was held not to be conversion. But using empty pallets to transport Bunnings' own goods was conversion: a serious dealing asserting dominion contrary to CHEP's rights. The case shows the fine judgment required."
formal: "Conversion, which grew from the action on the case of trover, is a wrongful dealing with goods inconsistent with the plaintiff's possessory rights: serious, and an assertion of dominion; strict liability. Title: possession or a right to immediate possession, with some proprietary title (Hocking v Director General of the National Archives). Detinue: right to immediate possession, a clear demand and a wrongful refusal; the court may order return."

terms:
  - term: Conversion
    gloss: A serious dealing with goods amounting to an assertion of dominion contrary to the rights of the person entitled to possession; strict liability.
  - term: Dominion
    gloss: The owner's control over goods. Conduct inconsistent with it — sale, taking possession, inconsistent use, refusing a demand — can be conversion.
  - term: Right to immediate possession
    gloss: Enough for conversion (with some proprietary title) and for detinue, but not for trespass, which needs actual possession.
  - term: Detinue
    gloss: The tort of wrongful detention of chattels; unique in that the court may, in its discretion, order the goods returned.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Acting in good faith is a defence to conversion.
    tempting: An honest auctioneer who sells goods by mistake seems blameless.
    correction: "Conversion is a tort of strict liability: good faith or mistake, e.g. by an auctioneer, is no defence (Lecture 2, slide 12)."
  - claim: Any handling of another person's goods is conversion.
    tempting: Any use seems inconsistent with the owner's rights.
    correction: The interference must be serious, an assertion of dominion. In Bunnings, storing goods on CHEP's pallets as delivered was not conversion; transporting Bunnings' own goods on them was.
  - claim: Only an owner in possession can sue in detinue.
    tempting: Getting the goods back sounds like an owner's remedy.
    correction: Detinue needs at least a right to immediate possession, a demand and a wrongful refusal; no actual possession or ownership is required (Lecture 2, slide 20).

elements:
  - name: "Conversion: title to sue"
    test: Possession, or a right to immediate possession, when the wrongful act was done — with some proprietary title, not a merely contractual or custodial right (Hocking).
    cases:
      - hocking-v-director-general-of-the-national-archives-of-australia
  - name: "Conversion: serious dealing"
    test: "A serious interference asserting dominion contrary to the possessor's rights: sale, taking possession, inconsistent use (Penfolds), or refusing an unconditional demand (Bunnings)."
    cases:
      - penfolds-wines-pty-ltd-v-elliott
      - bunnings-group-ltd-v-chep-australia-ltd
  - name: "Detinue: right to immediate possession"
    test: At least a right to immediate possession; no actual possession or ownership is required.
  - name: "Detinue: demand"
    test: A written or oral demand giving specific, clear and unequivocal instructions about delivery of the goods.
  - name: "Detinue: wrongful refusal"
    test: An express refusal to deliver. A reasonable refusal, e.g. while verifying the plaintiff's entitlement, may not be wrongful.
