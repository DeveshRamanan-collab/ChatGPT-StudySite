plain: Battery is a direct, deliberate or careless act that makes physical contact with another person's body without consent or other lawful justification. No injury is needed.
concrete: In Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd (2001) 53 NSWLR 98, Rixon, who was excluded from the casino, was standing at a roulette table when an employee placed a hand on his shoulder, without force, to get his attention before telling him to come to an interview room. The Court of Appeal held this was not a battery — not because it lacked hostility, but because a touch to engage attention is generally acceptable in everyday life.
formal: "Battery is a direct and intentional or negligent act of the defendant which causes physical contact with the body of the plaintiff without consent or other lawful justification. Elements: a voluntary and positive act; direct interference; intentional or negligent contact; bodily contact (which may be through an object); and unlawful touching. Hostility is not required (Rixon v Star City)."

terms:
  - term: Unlawful touching
    gloss: Contact beyond what is generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of daily life, without consent or other lawful excuse. It need not be hostile (Rixon).
  - term: Personal inviolability
    gloss: Every person's body is inviolate, so any touching, however slight, may be a battery absent a lawful excuse (Collins v Wilcock, applied in Rixon).
  - term: Exigencies of everyday life
    gloss: Ordinary contacts — jostling in a crowd, a tap on the shoulder to get attention — that do not amount to unlawful touching.
  - term: Continuing act
    gloss: "A failure to undo an earlier positive act, linked to it, can be one continuous positive act (Fagan: car left on an officer's foot)."
misconceptions:
  - claim: A touch is only a battery if it is angry or hostile.
    tempting: Old authority spoke of "the least touching of another in anger" (Cole v Turner), and the trial judge in Rixon relied on hostility.
    correction: "Rixon v Star City: absence of anger or hostility is not a satisfactory basis for finding no battery. The test is whether the contact exceeds what is generally acceptable in everyday life."
  - claim: For battery, the defendant's own body must touch the plaintiff.
    tempting: "\"Hit, touch or push\" suggests body-to-body contact."
    correction: "Contact through a weapon, a car or a substance is enough: in Binsaris v Northern Territory, tear gas touching the plaintiff's eyes sufficed (Lecture 1, slide 10)."
  - claim: Every punch is both a battery and an assault.
    tempting: The two torts often occur in quick succession.
    correction: An unseen blow is a battery without an assault, because the plaintiff never apprehended it. Battery usually, but not always, involves assault (Lecture 1, slide 19).

elements:
  - name: Voluntary and positive act
    test: A positive act, not passive presence (Innes v Wylie). A failure to undo an earlier positive act can be one continuous act (Fagan).
    cases:
      - innes-v-wylie
      - fagan-v-metropolitan-police-commissioner
  - name: Direct interference
    test: The contact must follow directly from the act; setting in motion a chain of events that ends in contact can be direct (Scott v Shepherd).
    cases:
      - scott-v-shepherd
  - name: Intentional or negligent
    test: Fault is required. Except in highway cases, the defendant must prove the act was neither intentional nor negligent.
  - name: Physical interference
    test: "Contact with the plaintiff's body: a hit, touch or push, or contact through a weapon, car or substance (tear gas in Binsaris)."
    cases:
      - binsaris-v-northern-territory
  - name: Unlawful touching
    test: No consent or other lawful excuse, and beyond what is generally acceptable in everyday life; hostility is not required (Rixon). Consent is a defence (Dean v Phung).
    cases:
      - rixon-v-star-city-pty-ltd
      - dean-v-phung
