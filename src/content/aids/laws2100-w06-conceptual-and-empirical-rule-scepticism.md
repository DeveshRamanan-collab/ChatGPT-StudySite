plain: Rule-sceptics doubt that rules matter. Conceptual sceptics deny that law is rules at all; empirical sceptics accept that rules may exist but deny that rules are what decide cases.
concrete: "Pick up an appellate judgment with a dissent. Both sides cite rules; both use canons of interpretation and distinguish precedents to reach opposite results. The empirical rule-sceptic concludes that, in a complex system, there is always legal support for both sides, so the rules cannot be what decides: judges respond to the facts and pick the doctrine they need."
formal: "Conceptual rule-scepticism: the real law is not 'paper rules'; we are under a legal duty not to do X if and only if the courts say so. Empirical rule-scepticism: judges respond to the underlying facts rather than to legal rules; so many rules might apply that both sides find support; statutes and precedents can be read in conflicting ways (slide 7; CL ch VII, s 2)."
