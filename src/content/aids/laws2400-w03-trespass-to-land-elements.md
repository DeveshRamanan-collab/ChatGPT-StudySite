plain: Trespass to land is directly entering or interfering with land someone else possesses, deliberately or carelessly, without their consent or other lawful excuse. No damage is needed.
concrete: "The lecture's everyday examples: parking a car on another person's land, spraying graffiti on a building, gatecrashing a private party, and dumping old furniture on someone else's property. Each is a direct, positive act on land in another's possession. None needs damage, because trespass to land is actionable per se — and a person who honestly but wrongly thinks the land is theirs still trespasses."
formal: "Trespass to land entails direct interference with land in the possession of the plaintiff. Elements: a direct and positive act interfering with land; intentional or negligent; land in the plaintiff's possession; without consent or other lawful justification. It is actionable per se; except in highway cases the defendant must disprove fault; mistake is no defence where there was an intentional or negligent trespassory act."
terms:
  - term: Trespassory conduct
    gloss: Entry without consent, and also driving onto land, remaining after permission is withdrawn, throwing an object or animal onto land, and encroaching scaffolding, cranes or structures.
  - term: Actionable per se
    gloss: No proof of damage is needed for trespass to land; the interference itself founds the claim.
  - term: Lawful justification
    gloss: A legal excuse for the entry other than consent, such as the exercise of a lawful duty.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Honestly believing the land is mine is a defence.
    tempting: Trespass sounds like knowingly going where you should not.
    correction: Mistake is no defence as long as there was an intentional or negligent trespassory act (Lecture 1, slide 6); the defendant need not intend to trespass (flowchart p 142).
  - claim: You must set foot on the land to commit trespass.
    tempting: Trespass is usually pictured as walking onto land.
    correction: Driving onto land, remaining after permission is withdrawn, throwing an object or animal onto it, and encroaching scaffolding or cranes are all trespassory conduct (slide 12).
  - claim: The plaintiff must prove the defendant was at fault.
    tempting: In most claims the plaintiff proves every element.
    correction: Except in highway cases, the plaintiff is not required to prove fault; the onus is on the defendant to disprove it (slide 6).

    hint: Was it direct and positive?
    answer: "She drove onto and parked on the land: a direct and positive act interfering with land in your possession."
  - ask: Must you prove the car caused any damage?
    hint: Actionable per se.
    answer: No. Trespass to land is actionable per se; no damage need be proved.
  - ask: She says she thought it was visitor parking for her friend's unit. Does that help her?
    hint: Mistake and the act.
    answer: No. She intended to drive and park there; mistake is no defence where the act itself was intentional or negligent.
  - ask: Who has to prove her state of mind?
    hint: Except in highway cases…
    answer: She must disprove fault. Outside highway cases the plaintiff need not prove it.
  - ask: What could still answer the claim?
    hint: The last element.
    answer: Consent, express or implied (such as an implied licence), or another lawful justification, such as the exercise of a lawful duty.
elements:
  - name: Direct and positive act
    test: "A direct, positive act interfering with land: entering, driving on, throwing things onto it, encroaching structures, or remaining after permission is withdrawn."
  - name: Intentional or negligent
    test: The act must be intentional or negligent. Mistake is no defence; outside highway cases the defendant must disprove fault.
  - name: In the plaintiff's possession
    test: The land must be in the plaintiff's exclusive possession at the time of the trespass — ownership is not required.
    cases:
      - newington-v-windeyer
  - name: Without consent or lawful justification
    test: No express or implied consent (such as an implied licence), and no other lawful excuse, such as the exercise of a lawful duty.
    cases:
      - halliday-v-nevill
