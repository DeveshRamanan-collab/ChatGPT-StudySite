plain: The defendant is liable only for harm of a kind that could reasonably be foreseen. If that kind was foreseeable, it does not matter that the harm was worse, or happened differently, than expected.
formal: Remoteness of harm is concerned with the extent of the defendant's liability for losses, determined by a reasonable foreseeability test (The Wagon Mound (No 1)). A risk is reasonably foreseeable when it is a 'real risk' which a reasonable man in the position of the defendant's servant would not brush aside as far-fetched (The Wagon Mound (No 2)). Extent and precise manner need not be foreseeable.
terms:
  - term: Kind or type of harm
    gloss: The category of damage suffered. Remoteness asks whether harm of that kind was reasonably foreseeable (Tremain v Pike; Nader; Commonwealth v McLean; Metrolink v Inglis).
  - term: Real risk
    gloss: A risk that would occur to a reasonable man in the position of the defendant's servant and that he would not brush aside as far-fetched (The Wagon Mound (No 2)).
  - term: Extent and manner
    gloss: How badly, and exactly how, the harm happened. Neither need be reasonably foreseeable (Hughes v Lord Advocate; Jolley; Pusey; Gittani Stone; Waller v James).
misconceptions:
  - claim: Harm is too remote if it happened in an unusual way the defendant could not have predicted.
    tempting: An odd sequence of events seems to show the harm was unforeseeable.
    correction: The precise manner of the harm's occurrence need not be reasonably foreseeable (eg Hughes v Lord Advocate). It is the kind of harm that must be.
  - claim: Remoteness sets a lower, or a higher, threshold of foreseeability than duty and breach.
    tempting: The flowchart calls remoteness a 'different test' of reasonable foreseeability.
    correction: "The meaning is the same: a real risk, not far-fetched (The Wagon Mound (No 2)). What differs is the object: what damage was foreseeable as a consequence of the negligence."
  - claim: A finding that a duty was owed settles remoteness, since both ask about foreseeability.
    tempting: Both stages use reasonable foreseeability.
    correction: They ask different questions. Duty asks whether injury to a class including the plaintiff was foreseeable; remoteness asks whether the kind of damage suffered was (San Sebastian, citing Mount Isa Mines v Pusey).
socratic:
  - ask: What is remoteness of harm concerned with?
    hint: The flowchart's first box.
    answer: The extent of the defendant's liability for losses, which is determined by a reasonable foreseeability test (The Wagon Mound (No 1)).
  - ask: What does 'reasonably foreseeable' mean here?
    hint: The Wagon Mound (No 2).
    answer: "A real risk: one that would occur to a reasonable person in the defendant's position and that he would not brush aside as far-fetched. It means the same as at duty and breach."
  - ask: Then what is different about the remoteness question?
    hint: What is being foreseen?
    answer: "Its object: the plaintiff must prove that the kind of damage suffered was foreseeable as a possible outcome of the kind of carelessness charged against the defendant."
  - ask: The defendant says the harm was far worse than anyone could have expected. Does that make it too remote?
    hint: Extent.
    answer: No. The extent of the harm need not be reasonably foreseeable (eg Jolley; Waller v James), provided harm of that kind was.
  - ask: For psychiatric harm, how precisely must the kind be foreseen?
    hint: Tame, in week 6.
    answer: Only the class of injury, psychiatric illness, need be foreseeable, not the particular type of disorder that eventuated (Gummow and Kirby JJ).
