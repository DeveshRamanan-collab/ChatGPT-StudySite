plain: The ICC can act only on one of four crimes, committed after the Statute applied, brought by a recognised trigger and — unless the Security Council referred it — linked to a party.
concrete: In May 2021 the ICC Prosecutor, using the proprio motu power, asked to investigate crimes committed in the Philippines' "war on drugs" campaign; Pre-Trial Chamber I authorised the investigation in September 2021. After a deferral request and a decision to resume in 2023, a warrant for crimes against humanity was issued against Mr Duterte in March 2025, and Philippine authorities arrested and surrendered him to the Court.
formal: "Art 5: genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, aggression. Art 11: crimes after entry into force (for a later Party, after entry into force for it, absent an art 12(3) declaration). Art 13: State Party referral, Security Council referral under Chapter VII, or the Prosecutor proprio motu. Art 12(2): for (a) and (c), the territorial (or flag) or nationality state must be a Party or have accepted jurisdiction."
terms:
  - term: ratione temporis
    gloss: "Jurisdiction in time (art 11): only crimes after the Statute's entry into force, or after it entered into force for a later Party."
  - term: proprio motu
    gloss: The Prosecutor opening an investigation on her or his own initiative (art 15), which Pre-Trial Chamber judges must authorise.
  - term: art 12(3) declaration
    gloss: A non-party's declaration, lodged with the Registrar, accepting the Court's jurisdiction over a crime; the state must then co-operate without delay or exception.
    example: Ukraine's 2014 declaration for 21 November 2013 – 22 February 2014.
  - term: trigger
    gloss: "How a situation reaches the Court: State Party referral, Security Council Chapter VII referral, or the Prosecutor proprio motu (art 13)."
misconceptions:
  - claim: When the Security Council refers a situation, the territorial or nationality state must still be a Party.
    tempting: The ICC is a treaty court built on state consent.
    correction: Art 12(2) applies only to art 13(a) and (c). A Chapter VII referral under art 13(b) needs no territorial or nationality link — as with Libya (Res 1970, 2011).
  - claim: A national of a State Party who commits piracy on that state's ship is within the ICC's jurisdiction.
    tempting: Both art 12(2) links — flag state and nationality — are satisfied.
    correction: Preconditions matter only for an art 5 crime. Piracy is not one of the four, so the Court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction.
  - claim: Any soldier who takes part in an act of aggression commits the crime of aggression.
    tempting: Taking part in an unlawful war seems enough.
    correction: Art 8 bis requires a person "in a position effectively to exercise control over or to direct the political or military action of a State" — a leadership crime.
socratic:
  - ask: What is the first question for any ICC case?
    hint: Art 5.
    answer: Is the conduct genocide, a crime against humanity, a war crime or the crime of aggression? Nothing else is within the Court's jurisdiction.
  - ask: What if the crimes happened before the state joined the Statute?
    hint: Art 11(2).
    answer: Only crimes after the Statute entered into force for that state are covered, unless it lodged an art 12(3) declaration.
  - ask: How can a situation reach the Court?
    hint: Three triggers in art 13.
    answer: A State Party referral, a Security Council referral under Chapter VII, or the Prosecutor acting proprio motu with Pre-Trial Chamber authorisation.
  - ask: For a State Party referral, which state must be a Party?
    hint: Art 12(2).
    answer: The state where the conduct occurred (or the flag state of the vessel or aircraft), or the accused's state of nationality — or one that has accepted jurisdiction under art 12(3).
  - ask: Why did the Libya referral need no such link?
    hint: Art 13(b).
    answer: Art 12(2) applies only to referrals by States Parties and proprio motu investigations; a Chapter VII referral by the Security Council bypasses it.
