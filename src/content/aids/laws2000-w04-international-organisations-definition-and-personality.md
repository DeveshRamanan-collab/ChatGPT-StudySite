plain: An international organisation is a body that states create under international law — usually by treaty — with a legal identity of its own, separate from its members.
concrete: The UN rents office buildings in New York and employs staff there; Charter art 104 gives it the domestic legal capacity to do that. But when it wanted to claim reparation from a state for injuries to its agent, domestic capacity was not enough — it needed international legal personality, which the Charter never mentions. In Reparation for Injuries (1949) the ICJ held that the members, by entrusting functions to the UN, had clothed it with the competence those functions required.
formal: "ARIO art 2(a): an IO is \"established by a treaty or other instrument governed by international law and possessing its own international legal personality\", and \"may include as members, in addition to States, other entities\". Personality is conferred expressly (WTO Agreement art VIII) or implicitly (Reparation for Injuries [1949] ICJ Rep 174, 179). Membership, withdrawal and expulsion follow the constitutive instrument (UN Charter arts 4, 6; Rome Statute art 127)."

terms:
  - term: constitutive instrument
    gloss: The treaty or other instrument creating an IO, such as the UN Charter. It sets membership, withdrawal, expulsion, personality and powers.
  - term: non-governmental organisation (NGO)
    gloss: A body such as Greenpeace or Amnesty International, constituted under domestic law rather than international law — so not an IO, despite its international focus.
  - term: implied personality
    gloss: International personality found from the functions the members intended an IO to exercise, where its instrument is silent (Reparation for Injuries).
  - term: domestic legal capacity (UN Charter art 104)
    gloss: The UN's capacity within members' territory to rent offices, employ staff and contract — distinct from international personality.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Article 104 of the UN Charter confers international legal personality on the UN.
    tempting: It is the Charter's only clause about the UN's legal capacity.
    correction: Art 104 gives domestic legal capacity in members' territory only. The Charter is silent on international personality, which the ICJ found implicitly in Reparation for Injuries.
  - claim: Any body working across many countries, with states involved, is an international organisation.
    tempting: International reach and state participation look like the marks of an IO.
    correction: "The test is the governing law: an IO is established by an instrument governed by international law. NGOs such as Greenpeace are constituted under domestic law, whatever their reach."
  - claim: An IO has international legal personality only if its founding treaty expressly confers it.
    tempting: Express conferral has been the norm for the last 30 years (e.g. WTO art VIII).
    correction: Personality can also be implied from the functions the members intended the organisation to exercise, as the ICJ held for the UN in Reparation for Injuries (1949).
