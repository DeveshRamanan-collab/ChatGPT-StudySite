plain: Conduct counts as the state's when done by its organs, by bodies the law empowers to exercise governmental authority, by organs lent to it, or by others it directs, controls or later adopts.
concrete: "Students stormed the US embassy in Tehran and took hostages. They were not Iran's organs, and the government itself had not attacked the embassy. But afterwards Iran issued a decree supporting what the students had done, endorsed it in public statements, and supported keeping the hostages. By acknowledging and adopting the students' conduct as its own, Iran made it its own: the occupation was attributed to Iran under art 11."
formal: ARSIWA chapter III (arts 4–11). Organs of any branch or level, per internal law (art 4) — exceptionally de facto organs in "complete dependence" (Genocide case, paras 392–393). Entities empowered by law to exercise governmental authority, acting in that capacity (art 5); lent organs (art 6); ultra vires conduct (art 7; Velásquez Rodríguez); instructions, direction or control (art 8); successful insurgents (art 10); adoption (art 11).

terms:
  - term: state organ (art 4)
    gloss: Any person or entity with that status under internal law — legislative, executive, judicial or other, central or territorial (e.g. an Australian state's police).
  - term: de facto organ
    gloss: An entity not an organ under internal law but acting in "complete dependence" on the state, merely its instrument. Exceptional (Genocide case, paras 392–393).
  - term: ultra vires (art 7)
    gloss: Conduct exceeding authority or contravening instructions. Still attributed if the organ or entity acted in that capacity (Velásquez Rodríguez).
  - term: acknowledgement and adoption (art 11)
    gloss: Conduct not otherwise attributable becomes the state's if and to the extent it acknowledges and adopts it as its own — e.g. Iran's decree in Tehran.
misconceptions:
  - claim: If soldiers disobey express orders, their conduct is private and not the state's.
    tempting: They defied the state, so they were not acting for it.
    correction: "Art 7: conduct of an organ acting in that capacity is attributed \"even if it exceeds its authority or contravenes instructions\" (Velásquez Rodríguez, para 170)."
  - claim: Once an entity is empowered under art 5, everything it does is the state's.
    tempting: Legal empowerment seems to be the whole link.
    correction: Art 5 also requires the entity to be acting in that capacity in the particular instance. An airline's border-check role does not make its commercial flights governmental.
  - claim: A militia the state funds or trains is automatically a de facto organ.
    tempting: Funding suggests a very close link.
    correction: De facto organ status needs "complete dependence", making the entity merely the state's instrument, and is exceptional (Genocide case, paras 392–393).
socratic:
  - ask: Police of an Australian state torture a foreign national. Whose conduct is it under international law?
    hint: Art 4 — territorial units.
    answer: Australia's. Art 4 attributes the conduct of any state organ, whether of the central government or a territorial unit, whatever its position.
  - ask: By statute a private company runs an immigration detention centre, and its guards mistreat detainees. Which article?
    hint: Not an organ, but empowered by law.
    answer: "Art 5: the company is empowered by the state's law to exercise elements of governmental authority, and the guards act in that capacity."
  - ask: The guards ignored company rules forbidding force. Does that break attribution?
    hint: Art 7.
    answer: No. Conduct is still attributed if the entity acts in that capacity, even if it exceeds its authority or contravenes instructions.
  - ask: Rebels commit abuses, then win the civil war and form the new government. Is the state responsible?
    hint: Art 10(1).
    answer: "Yes: the conduct of an insurrectional movement which becomes the new government is an act of that state. Had they lost, a good-faith state would not answer for them."
  - ask: Students with no link to the state seize an embassy; the government then issues a decree approving the occupation. Which route?
    hint: After the fact.
    answer: "Art 11: the state acknowledges and adopts the conduct as its own, as Iran did in Tehran."
