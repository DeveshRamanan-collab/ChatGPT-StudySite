plain: In tort, having been provoked, or the victim having been careless, generally does not reduce what an intentional wrongdoer must pay.
concrete: "Horkin had too much to drink and was asked to leave the North Melbourne Football Club. When he did not, club employees threw him out with more force than necessary, injuring him. The trial judge cut his damages by 30% for contributory negligence (his intoxication and failure to leave). The Victorian Supreme Court restored full liability: intentional battery and carelessness differ \"not merely in degree but in kind\"."
formal: "Provocation is a defence to a criminal charge but generally not to a tort: it cannot reduce compensatory damages, though it may help reduce or prevent exemplary damages (Fontin v Katapodis). Queensland and Western Australia are exceptions. Contributory negligence has been rejected for intentional torts: \"the difference is not merely in degree but in kind, and the social condemnation attached to the fault differs markedly\" (Horkin)."
terms:
  - term: Provocation
    gloss: Conduct by the plaintiff that incites the defendant's response. A defence to a criminal charge, but generally not to a tort action.
  - term: Contributory negligence
    gloss: Carelessness by the plaintiff that contributed to their own injury.
  - term: Compensatory vs exemplary damages
    gloss: Compensatory damages make good the plaintiff's loss; exemplary damages go beyond that to mark the defendant's conduct. Provocation can affect only exemplary damages.
