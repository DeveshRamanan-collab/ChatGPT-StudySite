plain: "Australia makes treaties through the executive in fixed steps: mandate, negotiation, ministerial and Executive Council approval, signature, parliamentary scrutiny by JSCOT, then any legislation and entry into force."
concrete: The 2021 AUKUS information-sharing agreement (ENNPIA) shows the documents at work. Its National Interest Analysis explained that US law restricts disclosing naval nuclear propulsion information unless such an agreement is in force, and that the UK was similarly restricted by its 1958 agreement with the US. Domestic implementation needed no change to Australian laws, and any party could terminate on six months' written notice.
formal: "DFAT's six steps: (1) the lead minister seeks a mandate to negotiate from the Minister for Foreign Affairs; (2) negotiation and finalisation of the text; (3) ministerial and Federal Executive Council approval of every treaty action; (4) signature — full powers unless the PM or Foreign Minister signs; (5) tabling for JSCOT for 15 or 20 joint sitting days; (6) implementing legislation, then entry into force."
terms:
  - term: National Interest Analysis (NIA)
    gloss: "Tabled with a treaty: its obligations, economic, social and political effects, reasons for Australia to act, implementation, costs and withdrawal."
  - term: JSCOT
    gloss: "Joint Standing Committee on Treaties: scrutinises tabled treaties and reports, within 15 or 20 joint sitting days, whether binding treaty action should be taken."
  - term: Federal Executive Council (ExCo)
    gloss: Must approve every treaty action — signing, ratifying, amending, terminating or withdrawing.
  - term: minor treaty action
    gloss: A treaty action with negligible national-interest effect, such as a technical amendment, given a streamlined parliamentary scrutiny process.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Parliament must approve every treaty before Australia can sign it.
    tempting: JSCOT and tabling give Parliament a visible role.
    correction: Signature follows ExCo approval (step 4); tabling for JSCOT comes after signature (step 5). Parliament scrutinises, and passes any implementing law, before binding treaty action.
  - claim: Any minister can start negotiating a treaty in their own portfolio.
    tempting: Ministers run their own policy areas.
    correction: The lead minister must first obtain a mandate to negotiate from the Minister for Foreign Affairs (or Cabinet), and the Prime Minister must be informed (step 1).
  - claim: An NIA is written after ratification to explain the obligations taken on.
    tempting: An NIA does set out the treaty's obligations.
    correction: It accompanies the treaty when tabled, so JSCOT can scrutinise it before binding treaty action is taken.
