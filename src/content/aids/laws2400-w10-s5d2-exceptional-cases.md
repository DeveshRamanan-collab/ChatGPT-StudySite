plain: If negligence cannot be shown to be a necessary condition of the harm, s 5D(2) lets a court, in an exceptional case, still accept factual causation, after asking whether and why responsibility should be imposed.
concrete: "In Adeels Palace, the club's lack of security was a breach, but the evidence could not show that security staff would have stopped the gunman re-entering. The High Court found no reason to treat it as an exceptional case under s 5D(2): it 'would be contrary to established principles' to hold the club responsible when its negligence was not a necessary condition of the harm but only 'might have' prevented it."
formal: "CLA s 5D(2): 'In determining in an exceptional case, in accordance with established principles, whether negligence that cannot be established as a necessary condition of the occurrence of harm should be accepted as establishing factual causation, the court is to consider (amongst other relevant things) whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed on the negligent party.' It is rarely applied."
terms:
  - term: Exceptional case
    gloss: The NSW wording in s 5D(2) for when negligence not shown to be a necessary condition may still establish factual causation. The textbook flowchart says 'an appropriate case'.
  - term: Evidentiary gap
    gloss: Where the evidence cannot establish the cause, eg because scientific evidence cannot identify which exposure caused the disease. The flowchart requires one for s 5D(2).
  - term: Section 3B(1) exclusion
    gloss: The CLA does not apply to dust disease and tobacco claims, which go under special statutes such as the Dust Diseases Tribunal Act 1989 (NSW).
misconceptions:
  - claim: Section 5D(2) applies whenever the breach is clear and the evidence leaves a gap.
    tempting: The flowchart lists a clear breach and an evidentiary gap as requirements.
    correction: "They are needed but not enough: s 5D(2) is for exceptional cases, and the court still weighs whether and why responsibility should be imposed. Adeels Palace refused it."
  - claim: Amaca v Booth used s 5D(2) to establish causation for mesothelioma.
    tempting: s 5D(2) was modelled on the dust disease cases.
    correction: "Booth never mentions the CLA: s 3B(1) excludes dust disease claims, which go under the Dust Diseases Tribunal Act 1989 (NSW). It was decided on the evidence."
  - claim: Because s 5D(2) and s 5D(4) use the same words, scope of liability can fill a factual causation gap.
    tempting: Both direct the court to consider whether and why responsibility should be imposed on the negligent party.
    correction: Same question, different stage. Section 5D(2) concerns factual causation in an exceptional case; s 5D(4) concerns scope of liability, which assumes the harm was caused.
