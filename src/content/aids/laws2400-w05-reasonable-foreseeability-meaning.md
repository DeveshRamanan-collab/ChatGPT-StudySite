plain: At the duty stage, the defendant need only have been able to foresee harm to someone like the plaintiff, not exactly how it would happen.
concrete: "Chapman's careless driving caused a collision on a dark, wet night near Adelaide, and he was thrown onto the road unconscious. Dr Cherry stopped to help him and was struck and killed by Hearse's car. Chapman argued he owed Cherry no duty because he could not foresee that chain of events. The High Court disagreed: injury to people coming to render aid at the scene was reasonably foreseeable."
formal: At the duty stage it is "not necessary for the plaintiff to show that the precise manner in which his injuries were sustained was reasonably foreseeable; it is sufficient if it appears that injury to a class of persons of which he was one might reasonably have been foreseen" (Chapman v Hearse). The duty, breach and remoteness inquiries "progressively decline from the general to the particular" (Glass JA, San Sebastian).

terms:
  - term: Class of persons
    gloss: A group the plaintiff belongs to, such as people who stop to render aid at an accident, to whom injury was foreseeable.
  - term: "\"From the general to the particular\""
    gloss: "Glass JA: foreseeability narrows from duty (class of persons) to breach (kind of carelessness) to remoteness (kind of damage)."
  - term: Remoteness
    gloss: Whether the kind of damage the plaintiff suffered was foreseeable as a possible outcome of the kind of carelessness charged.
misconceptions:
  - claim: No duty is owed unless the defendant could foresee exactly how the plaintiff would be hurt.
    tempting: "Chapman argued just that: he could not have foreseen such a drastic chain of events."
    correction: "Chapman v Hearse rejected it: it is enough that injury to a class of persons of which the plaintiff was one might reasonably have been foreseen."
  - claim: Reasonable foreseeability is one test, asked once, at the duty stage.
    tempting: The neighbour principle makes foreseeability look like a duty-only idea.
    correction: "It is asked three times: duty (class of persons), breach (kind of carelessness), remoteness (kind of damage), moving \"from the general to the particular\" (Glass JA)."
  - claim: Dr Cherry's being a doctor is what made his injury foreseeable.
    tempting: Doctors are exactly the people you expect to stop at an accident.
    correction: The High Court said it was without significance that Cherry was a medical practitioner. The class was persons fulfilling a moral and social duty to render aid.
socratic:
  - ask: Chapman crashes on a dark, wet highway and lies unconscious on the road. Who might foreseeably come to be on that road at risk?
    hint: Think about people who stop at accidents.
    answer: Persons fulfilling a moral and social duty to render aid to those injured, at some risk to themselves.
  - ask: Chapman says he could not have foreseen a helper being hit by another car. Does that defeat the duty?
    hint: Must the precise manner of injury be foreseeable?
    answer: No. The precise manner need not be foreseeable; foreseeable injury to a class of persons including the plaintiff is enough.
  - ask: At the breach stage, what must be foreseeable instead?
    hint: Shift the focus to the defendant's conduct.
    answer: That the kind of carelessness charged against the defendant might cause damage of some kind to the plaintiff.
  - ask: And at the remoteness stage?
    hint: Shift the focus to the damage itself.
    answer: That the kind of damage the plaintiff suffered was foreseeable as a possible outcome of that kind of carelessness.
  - ask: Can what is reasonably foreseeable change over time?
    hint: Think about asbestos.
    answer: Yes. It depends on the state of scientific knowledge about the risk, as with asbestos exposure or the impact of climate change.
elements:
  - name: "Duty: class of persons"
    test: Was injury to the plaintiff, or a class of persons to which they belonged, reasonably foreseeable as a possibility? The precise manner need not be.
    cases:
      - chapman-v-hearse
      - caterson-v-commissioner-for-railways
  - name: "Breach: kind of carelessness"
    test: Was it reasonably foreseeable that the kind of carelessness charged might cause damage of some kind to the plaintiff?
    cases:
      - wyong-shire-council-v-shirt
      - overseas-tankship-v-miller-steamship-the-wagon-mound-no-2
  - name: "Remoteness: kind of damage"
    test: Was the kind of damage the plaintiff suffered foreseeable as a possible outcome of that kind of carelessness?
    cases:
      - mount-isa-mines-ltd-v-pusey
