plain: A subject of international law is an entity that holds international rights and duties and can bring, and be made to answer, international claims. States hold the full set; other subjects hold only some.
concrete: "A UN mediator was killed, and UN officials injured, in clashes involving Israeli militants. Could the UN itself claim reparation from Israel? To bring an international claim it needed international legal personality — and the UN Charter never says it has any. The General Assembly asked the ICJ. In Reparation for Injuries (1949) the Court said yes: the UN was meant to exercise functions only an international person could perform, though it \"certainly is not\" a state."
formal: A subject is "an entity possessing international rights and obligations and having the capacity (1) to maintain its rights by bringing international claims; and (2) to be responsible for its breaches of obligation by being subjected to such claims" (Crawford, 2019). Only states have full capacity. Subjects "are not necessarily identical in their nature or in the extent of their rights" (Reparation for Injuries [1949] ICJ Rep 174, 178).

terms:
  - term: international legal personality
    gloss: Holding rights and obligations under international law, with capacity to bring international claims and to be held responsible. Asking who is a subject is asking who has personality.
  - term: full and partial capacity
    gloss: Only states have every capacity of subjecthood; international organisations and others have only some, limited by their functions.
    example: The UN can bring claims and make treaties, but it is not a state.
  - term: capacity to create international law
    gloss: The power to make the rules a subject is bound by — for example, concluding binding treaties.
  - term: immunity from jurisdiction
    gloss: A subject's freedom from other states' courts. Sovereign equals have no authority above them, so Australia cannot try New Zealand in its courts.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Once an entity has international legal personality, it has the same rights and duties as a state.
    tempting: Personality sounds all-or-nothing, and states are the model subject.
    correction: Subjects "are not necessarily identical in their nature or in the extent of their rights" (Reparation). The UN is an international person but "certainly" not a state; only states have full capacity.
  - claim: An entity must meet the statehood criteria, or be a UN member, to be a subject of international law.
    tempting: Statehood and UN membership are this week's headline tests of status.
    correction: Crawford's definition turns on rights, obligations and claims, not statehood. International organisations and individuals are subjects without being states or UN members.
  - claim: Individuals hold ICCPR rights, so they have the same legal personality as states.
    tempting: Holding rights under a binding treaty is one capacity of subjecthood.
    correction: Only states have full personality. Individuals hold some rights (and some obligations, under international criminal law), not the whole set of capacities.
socratic:
  - ask: The UN wants to claim reparation from a state for injuries to its agent. What must be true of the UN before it can bring that claim?
    hint: Crawford's definition, limb (1).
    answer: It must be a subject of international law — have international legal personality, including the capacity to maintain its rights by bringing international claims.
  - ask: The UN Charter never says the UN has international personality. Does that end the matter?
    hint: What did the ICJ look at in Reparation?
    answer: No. The Court looked at the functions and rights the UN was intended to exercise; they could only be explained if it had a large measure of international personality.
  - ask: Does that make the UN a state?
    hint: Read the passage at 178–179.
    answer: No. It is an international person — "not the same thing as saying that it is a State, which it certainly is not" — and its rights and duties are not the same as a state's.
  - ask: So how should an exam answer frame "Is X a subject?"
    hint: The five capacities on slide 9.
    answer: Ask which capacities X has — claiming rights, diplomatic relations, direct obligations, law-making, immunities — and where each comes from. Only states have all of them.
