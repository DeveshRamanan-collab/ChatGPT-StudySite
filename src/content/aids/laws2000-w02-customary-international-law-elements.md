plain: Custom is unwritten law formed when states generally and consistently act a certain way because they believe the law requires it; both the behaviour and the belief are needed.
concrete: "The lecturer's examples of practice that is not law: diplomatic protocol when a foreign delegation arrives, and sending humanitarian aid after a disaster abroad. Many states do both, often and consistently, yet neither is legally required — states act from courtesy, tradition or goodwill. Because the belief of legal obligation (opinio juris) is missing, no customary rule exists, however common the practice."
formal: Custom requires (a) a general practice — consistent repetition by states facing the same facts (objective element) — and (b) acceptance as law, opinio juris sive necessitatis (subjective element). Practice need not be in "absolutely rigorous conformity" (Nicaragua [186]) but must be "extensive and virtually uniform", including specially affected states, even over a short period (North Sea [74]); courtesy is not enough ([77]).

terms:
  - term: state practice
    gloss: "The objective element: what states do or omit to do — diplomatic acts, legislation, court decisions, conduct on the ground; no hierarchy among forms (ILC Conclusion 6)."
  - term: opinio juris sive necessitatis
    gloss: "The subjective element: states' belief that a practice is legally required, distinguishing law from courtesy, convenience or tradition."
  - term: specially affected states
    gloss: States whose interests a rule most concerns, such as coastal states for territorial sea delimitation; their practice weighs heavily (North Sea [74]).
  - term: failure to react
    gloss: Inaction over time may evidence opinio juris, if the state could react and the circumstances called for a reaction (ILC Conclusion 10(3)).
misconceptions:
  - claim: A practice must continue for a very long time, even centuries, before it becomes custom.
    tempting: Custom was traditionally pictured as practice repeated over hundreds of years.
    correction: "North Sea [74]: a short period is not of itself a bar, provided practice within it — including that of specially affected states — is extensive and virtually uniform."
  - claim: Any inconsistent state conduct defeats a customary rule.
    tempting: A rule seems unproven if states keep departing from it.
    correction: "Nicaragua [186]: practice need not be in absolutely rigorous conformity; inconsistent conduct generally treated as a breach does not create a new rule, and invoking an exception within the rule confirms it."
  - claim: If states do something often enough, it becomes custom.
    tempting: Frequency looks like the best evidence of a rule.
    correction: "North Sea [77]: frequency or habit \"is not in itself enough\". Ceremonial and protocol acts motivated by courtesy, convenience or tradition lack opinio juris."
socratic:
  - ask: Many states send aid after disasters abroad. Is there a customary duty to do so?
    hint: Is the practice there? Is the belief there?
    answer: "The practice is there, but states do not act from a sense of legal obligation, so opinio juris is missing: no custom."
  - ask: What is the objective element, and why is it the easier one to show?
    hint: You can observe it.
    answer: State practice — what states do or omit — visible in diplomatic acts, legislation, court decisions and conduct on the ground.
  - ask: How consistent must the practice be?
    hint: Compare Asylum with Nicaragua.
    answer: Generally consistent, not perfect (Nicaragua). But "so much uncertainty and contradiction", as in Asylum, means no constant and uniform usage.
  - ask: Must every state take part, over a long time?
    hint: Think of landlocked states and territorial sea rules.
    answer: No. Extensive, virtually uniform practice including specially affected states can suffice, even over a short period (North Sea [74]).
  - ask: How do you prove the belief if states rarely explain themselves?
    hint: ILC Conclusion 10 lists forms of evidence.
    answer: From public statements, official publications, government legal opinions, diplomatic correspondence, national court decisions, treaty provisions, conduct on resolutions — even failure to react.
