plain: Factual causation asks whether the harm would have happened without the defendant's negligence. If it would have happened anyway, the negligence did not cause it.
concrete: "Mr Barnett drank tea at work that had been poisoned with arsenic, and went to hospital. The duty doctor, feeling unwell himself, refused to see him; another doctor attended later, but it was too late, and he died. The duty doctor was negligent, yet the claim failed: even timely treatment would not have saved him. He would have died anyway, so the negligence was not a cause of his death."
formal: "Common law: would the plaintiff have suffered the damage but for the defendant's negligence? CLA s 5D(1)(a): was the negligence a necessary condition of the occurrence of the harm? Section 5D(1)(a) 'is a statutory statement of the \"but for\" test of causation' (Strong v Woolworths). The plaintiff proves it on the balance of probabilities (s 5E)."
analogy:
  text: Rewind the story, take out the defendant's careless act, and replay it. If the harm still happens, the act was not a necessary condition. If the harm disappears, it was.
  maps:

terms:
  - term: "'But for' test"
    gloss: "Common law: would the plaintiff have suffered the damage but for the defendant's negligence? 'No' means factual causation is satisfied."
  - term: Necessary condition
    gloss: "Section 5D(1)(a): was the negligence a necessary condition of the occurrence of the harm? 'Yes' means satisfied. The same test as 'but for'."
  - term: Balance of probabilities
    gloss: "The civil standard: more likely than not. On causation the plaintiff always bears the onus (s 5E)."
  - term: Subjective test (s 5D(3))
    gloss: What the injured person would have done is decided subjectively; their later statement about it is inadmissible unless against their interest.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Under the common law 'but for' question, answering 'Yes' means factual causation is satisfied.
    tempting: "'Yes' does satisfy the statutory necessary condition question."
    correction: The questions are mirror images. 'Would the injuries have been suffered but for the negligence?' — 'No' is satisfied. 'Was the fault a necessary condition?' — 'Yes' is satisfied.
  - claim: Section 5D(1)(a) replaced 'but for' with a different, looser statutory test.
    tempting: Statutes often change common law tests, and the wording is different.
    correction: Strong v Woolworths called s 5D(1)(a) 'a statutory statement of the "but for" test'. Courts still use 'but for' language when applying it.
  - claim: Factual causation asks whether the harm was a reasonably foreseeable result of the breach.
    tempting: Foreseeability runs through duty, breach and remoteness.
    correction: "Factual causation is not about reasonableness: it is what the evidence proves happened. Foreseeability returns at scope of liability (remoteness)."
socratic:
  - ask: In Barnett, the duty doctor negligently refused to see the patient. Why did the claim fail?
    hint: What would timely treatment have achieved?
    answer: Even timely treatment would not have saved him. He would have died anyway, so the negligence was not a 'but for' cause of the death.
  - ask: How does the CLA put the same question?
    hint: s 5D(1)(a).
    answer: Was the negligence a necessary condition of the occurrence of the harm? 'But for' and necessary condition are two ways of saying the same thing.
  - ask: In Strong v Woolworths, why did it matter when the chip was dropped?
    hint: A reasonable system inspected at intervals of not more than 20 minutes.
    answer: If it fell in the last 20 minutes, a reasonable inspection would not have removed it, so the missing system would not be a necessary condition of the fall.
  - ask: How did the High Court decide when the chip fell?
    hint: Which standard, and which period was longer?
    answer: "On the balance of probabilities (s 5E): it was more probably deposited in the longer period, 8.00am to 12.10pm, so causation was established."
  - ask: Suppose causation turns on whether the plaintiff would have heeded a warning. How is that decided?
    hint: s 5D(3).
    answer: Subjectively, in the light of all relevant circumstances. Her own statement after the harm about what she would have done is inadmissible unless it is against her interest.

what-if:
  - change: In Barnett, the evidence showed prompt treatment would probably have saved the deceased.
    effect: "The 'but for' question would be answered 'No': he would not have died but for the negligence. Factual causation would be satisfied, and the claim would move to scope of liability."
  - change: In Strong v Woolworths, the probabilities favoured the chip being dropped within 20 minutes before the fall.
    effect: A reasonable inspection system would not have found it, so the lack of a system would not be a necessary condition of the fall; factual causation would fail.
  - change: After her injury, the plaintiff tells the court she would certainly have obeyed a warning sign.
    effect: Under s 5D(3)(b) the statement is inadmissible unless against her interest. What she would have done is decided subjectively, in the light of all relevant circumstances.
