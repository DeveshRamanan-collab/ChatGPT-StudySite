plain: "Trespass needs direct interference: the harm must follow immediately from the defendant's act. If something else must happen first, the claim lies in action on the case instead."
concrete: "In Hutchins v Maughan [1947] VLR 131 the defendant laid poisonous baits on unfenced land. Later the complainant brought his dogs onto the land; they took the baits and died. Herring CJ held trespass did not lie: the baits were laid before the dogs arrived, and the complainant himself had to intervene by bringing them there. The injury was consequential, so the remedy lay in case."
formal: Where the injury is immediate on the act done, trespass lies; where it is only consequential, the remedy is in case (Reynolds v Clarke (1725); Scott v Shepherd (1773); Leame v Bray (1803), applied in Hutchins v Maughan [1947] VLR 131). A log that hits someone as it is thrown into the highway is trespass; someone later tripping over it is case.

  limits:
    - "Directness turns on time and intervention, not on how many physical steps occur: a crate shoved so it falls straight onto a foot is still direct."
    - Dominoes suggest a mechanical test, but each case is determined on its own facts (Herring CJ).
terms:
  - term: Direct (immediate) injury
    gloss: Injury that follows so immediately on the defendant's act that it can be called part of the act; trespass lies.
  - term: Consequential injury
    gloss: Injury that results only after an intervening event, e.g. the complainant bringing his dogs to the baits; the remedy is in case.
  - term: Trespass vi et armis
    gloss: The historical form of trespass named in Reynolds v Clarke (1725), available where the immediate act itself injures the plaintiff.
  - term: Intervening event
    gloss: A step by the plaintiff or someone else needed before the act causes harm; its presence makes the injury consequential.
    example: In Hutchins, the complainant coming to the land with his dogs.
misconceptions:
  - claim: If the defendant's object physically touches the plaintiff, the harm is direct.
    tempting: Contact feels like the essence of directness.
    correction: Timing is the test. A person who trips over a log already lying in the road has touched it, yet that is case, not trespass (Leame v Bray, quoted in Hutchins v Maughan).
  - claim: Directness depends on whether the defendant meant to cause harm.
    tempting: Deliberate conduct feels more "direct" than carelessness.
    correction: Directness asks whether the injury followed immediately from the act — causal sequence and time. Intention belongs to the separate fault element; Hutchins' baits were deliberately laid yet the injury was not direct.
  - claim: Because the boy in Williams v Milotin was hit directly, only trespass could be pleaded.
    tempting: Direct contact is the hallmark of trespass.
    correction: The High Court held two causes of action arose on the same facts, and the plaintiff could choose negligence with its six-year limitation period.
socratic:
  - ask: In Hutchins, were the baits laid before or after the dogs came onto the land?
    hint: Read Herring CJ on the order of events.
    answer: Before — perhaps even before the complainant arrived in the vicinity.
  - ask: Could laying the baits, by itself, have harmed the complainant?
    hint: What did he have to do first?
    answer: No. He had to intervene himself by coming to the land and bringing his dogs; the act of itself did him no mischief.
  - ask: So was the injury immediate or consequential?
    hint: Did it follow so closely as to be "part of the act"?
    answer: Consequential. It did not follow so immediately on the act as to be part of it, so trespass did not lie and the remedy was in case.
  - ask: What one change would have made it trespass?
    hint: Think of the log thrown into the highway.
    answer: If the defendant had thrown the baits to the dogs, the injury would be directly occasioned and trespass would lie. That is why directness is "an issue of time".
