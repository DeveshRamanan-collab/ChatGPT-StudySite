plain: "Before the ICJ can hear a case, a real dispute must already exist: one state's claim is positively opposed by the other, which knows it. A respondent's denial doesn't decide that."
concrete: "The lecturer explains why this matters: the ICJ has jurisdiction only if a dispute exists when proceedings begin, so a respondent expecting to lose may say there is no dispute at all. The Court checks objectively. It looks at the facts — public statements, diplomatic notes, how the parties behaved — for a specific issue on which one state's view was positively opposed by the other, and communicated. An official's press statement contradicting the other state can be evidence."
formal: A dispute is "a disagreement on a point of law or fact, a conflict of legal views or interests" (Mavrommatis, PCIJ 1924). Existence is "a matter for objective determination"; mere denial does not prove non-existence (Peace Treaties). A claim must be "positively opposed" by a respondent that "was aware, or could not have been unaware" of it (South West Africa), shown by "an examination of the facts" (Georgia v Russia).
terms:
  - term: legal dispute
    gloss: A disagreement on a point of law or fact, a conflict of legal views or interests between persons — here, states (Mavrommatis).
  - term: objective determination
    gloss: The Court decides for itself whether a dispute exists; a party's denial does not prove there is none (Peace Treaties, 1950).
  - term: positively opposed
    gloss: One party's claim is met by the other's clearly opposite view, and the respondent was aware, or could not have been unaware, of it (South West Africa).
misconceptions:
  - claim: If the respondent denies that any dispute exists, the Court lacks jurisdiction.
    tempting: A dispute needs two sides who disagree.
    correction: "Existence is a matter for objective determination: \"the mere denial of the existence of a dispute does not prove its non-existence\" (Peace Treaties, 1950)."
  - claim: Any sharp public disagreement between states is a dispute, because the threshold is low.
    tempting: The slides do call the threshold low.
    correction: "Low is not nil. It must be more than mere friction: clearly opposite views on an identifiable issue, communicated so the respondent knew (South West Africa; Marshall Islands v UK)."
  - claim: A dispute that crystallises while the case is pending is enough.
    tempting: The opposition does eventually appear.
    correction: The dispute must exist at the date proceedings are instituted; otherwise the Court has no jurisdiction to deal with the case.
socratic:
  - ask: State X files at the ICJ. State Y says "there is no dispute between us". Why would Y say that?
    hint: Jurisdiction.
    answer: If no dispute existed when proceedings were instituted, the Court has no jurisdiction — so a respondent expecting to lose may deny the dispute.
  - ask: Does Y's denial settle the point?
    hint: Peace Treaties (1950).
    answer: No. Whether a dispute exists is a matter for objective determination; mere denial does not prove its non-existence.
  - ask: What must X show?
    hint: South West Africa.
    answer: That its claim was positively opposed by Y, and that Y was aware, or could not have been unaware, of that opposition — clearly opposite views on the issue.
  - ask: Where would you look for that?
    hint: Georgia v Russia, para 30.
    answer: "At the facts: public statements, diplomatic notes and other exchanges, and the parties' conduct. Bilateral exchanges showing conflicting claims are usually the strongest evidence of a territorial dispute."
  - ask: X's claim was set out only in an internal report it never sent or published. Enough?
    hint: Communication.
    answer: No. There must be communication showing the opposed views (Marshall Islands v UK); Y could not have been aware of a claim it never saw.
