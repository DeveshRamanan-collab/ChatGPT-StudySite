plain: Dworkin accepts that judges use judgment, but denies that in hard cases they are free of legal standards. In Riggs v Palmer, principles of the law decided that the murderer could not inherit.
concrete: "Francis Palmer left most of his estate to his grandson Elmer, aged 16. Knowing his grandfather might revoke the will, Elmer poisoned him. The will was valid, and the will statutes, read literally, gave Elmer the property. The majority refused: all laws 'may be controlled in their operation and effect by general, fundamental maxims of the common law', including that no one may profit by his own wrong."
formal: "'Discretion, like the hole in a doughnut, does not exist except as an area left open by a surrounding belt of restriction' (TRS 31). Weak discretion: standards demand judgment, or a decision is final. Strong: the official 'is simply not bound by standards set by the authority in question' (TRS 32). Treat principles as law, and 'a legal obligation might be imposed by a constellation of principles' (TRS 44)."

  - term: Weak discretion
    gloss: Either the standards cannot be applied mechanically and demand judgment (TRS 31), or an official has final authority and cannot be reviewed (TRS 32). Dworkin accepts both.
  - term: Strong discretion
    gloss: "On some issue the official 'is simply not bound by standards set by the authority in question' (TRS 32): what Dworkin says positivists must accept in hard cases."
  - term: Constellation of principles
    gloss: "Dworkin's alternative source of legal obligation: it exists whenever the case for it, in terms of binding legal principles, is stronger than the case against (TRS 44)."

objections:
  - position: In hard cases judges are bound by legal principles; their discretion is weak only (Dworkin).
    evidence: In Riggs the majority applied maxims of the common law; judges cite principles as binding, as a sergeant's orders control him (TRS 34).
    objection: "Raz and the realists: when the law runs out, judges reach beyond it for extra-legal principles and make new law. Some commentators add that Dworkin cherry-picked cases like Riggs (lecture)."
