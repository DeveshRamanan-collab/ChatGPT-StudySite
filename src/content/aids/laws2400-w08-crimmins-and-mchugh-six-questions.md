plain: A regulator that directs a worker into danger it knows about, where the worker cannot protect himself, can owe him a duty even though it is not his employer.
concrete: "Crimmins was a casual waterside worker at Port Melbourne in the 1960s. The Australian Stevedoring Industry Authority, the regulator, not his employer, directed workers where to work. He unloaded asbestos cargoes and in 1997 was diagnosed with mesothelioma. The High Court held the Authority owed him a duty: it had an overarching supervisory and regulatory role regarding safety."
formal: "Crimmins v Stevedoring Industry Finance Committee (1999). McHugh J: the authority owed a duty because it directed the worker to places of risk of which it was, or ought to have been, aware, knowing he was specially vulnerable through casual employment and his obligation to obey its directions. McHugh J set six questions for an affirmative duty of a statutory authority in a novel case."
terms:
  - term: Affirmative duty
    gloss: A duty to act, for example to exercise a statutory power to protect someone, rather than a duty to take care in what one does.
  - term: Specific class
    gloss: A defined group the authority's powers can protect, such as waterside workers it directs, as opposed to the public at large.
  - term: Core policy making / quasi-legislative function
    gloss: High-level decisions, such as whether to make an order requiring respirators; a duty over these points against liability.
misconceptions:
  - claim: A duty was obvious in Crimmins because the Authority was his employer.
    tempting: Employers owe workers a duty of care, and the Authority told him where to work.
    correction: The Authority was the regulator, not his employer; that is why the case needed McHugh J's questions. A statutory authority employer would have made a duty much easier.
  - claim: All six questions must be answered "yes" for a duty.
    tempting: They are set out as a list of questions to tick.
    correction: Not all need an affirmative answer. A "yes" to question 5 (core policy or quasi-legislative functions) or question 6 (other policy reasons) points against a duty.
  - claim: Crimmins was vulnerable because he did not know asbestos was dangerous.
    tempting: Ignorance of a risk sounds like vulnerability.
    correction: "McHugh J located vulnerability in his casual employment and his obligation to obey the Authority's directions: refusing a dangerous job could cost him work."

    answer: It was, or ought to have been, aware of the risks at the places it sent him, and knew or ought to have known he was specially vulnerable.
  - ask: Why did Hayne J dissent?
    hint: What kind of power was ordering respirators?

  - ask: How should you use the six questions in an answer?

elements:
  - name: 1. Reasonable foreseeability
    test: Was it reasonably foreseeable that an act or omission of the defendant, including a failure to exercise its statutory powers, would injure the plaintiff?
    cases:
      - crimmins-v-stevedoring-industry-finance-committee
  - name: 2. Power to protect a specific class
    test: By its statutory obligations, did the defendant have power to protect a specific class including the plaintiff, rather than the public at large?
    cases:
      - crimmins-v-stevedoring-industry-finance-committee
      - graham-barclay-oysters-v-ryan
  - name: 3. Vulnerability
    test: "Was the plaintiff vulnerable: could she not reasonably be expected to adequately safeguard herself from harm?"
    cases:
      - crimmins-v-stevedoring-industry-finance-committee
  - name: 4. Knowledge
    test: Did the defendant know, or ought it to have known, of the risk of harm to the specific class if it did not exercise its powers?
    cases:
      - crimmins-v-stevedoring-industry-finance-committee
  - name: 5. Core policy or quasi-legislative function
    test: Would a duty impose liability for the defendant's core policy making or quasi-legislative functions? If yes, that points against a duty.
    cases:
      - crimmins-v-stevedoring-industry-finance-committee
  - name: 6. Other policy reasons
    test: Are there any other supervening reasons in policy, such as indeterminate liability, to deny a duty of care?
    cases:
      - crimmins-v-stevedoring-industry-finance-committee

transfer:
  - scenario: A statutory port authority allocates casual labourers to berths each day. It knows fumigation chemicals at one berth are hazardous but gives no direction about masks. A labourer who would lose work if he refused is injured there.
    answer: Walk McHugh J's questions. Injury is foreseeable; the authority's powers protect a specific class (labourers it allocates); the labourer is vulnerable, as he cannot refuse without losing work; and it knew of the hazard. A direction about masks at one berth is operational rather than core policy. As in Crimmins, a duty is likely.
