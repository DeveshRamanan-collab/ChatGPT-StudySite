plain: A state cannot normally be sued in another state's courts, or have a judgment enforced against it, without its consent — because states are equals. Immunity blocks the case; it does not excuse the conduct.
concrete: "The lecturer's example: New Zealand does something wrongful and is sued in an Australian court. Because states are sovereign equals, the Australian court must rule on immunity first and, if New Zealand is immune, refuse to hear the case — nor can New Zealand's property here be seized. But had New Zealand taken part in the proceedings, it would have waived immunity, and could not reclaim it once judgment went against it."
formal: "State immunity is customary international law, grounded in sovereign equality. The UN Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and their Property 2004 (not in force: 25 of 30 ratifications) mostly reflects custom: a state is immune \"in respect of itself and its property\" (art 5); courts refrain from jurisdiction (art 6); no immunity on express consent (art 7), participation (art 8) or counterclaim (art 9). \"State\": art 2(1)(b)."
terms:
  - term: immunity from adjudication
    gloss: A foreign state cannot be sued before the forum state's courts unless it consents.
  - term: immunity from enforcement
    gloss: A judgment given against a state cannot be enforced upon it, e.g. by confiscating its assets. The question arises only after jurisdiction and judgment.
  - term: procedural bar
    gloss: Immunity is decided first and only stops the court hearing the case; it says nothing about whether the state's conduct was lawful.
  - term: "\"State\" (art 2(1)(b))"
    gloss: Organs of government; federal units, subdivisions and agencies entitled to exercise sovereign authority; and representatives acting in that capacity.

socratic:
  - ask: A company sues State X in an Australian court over X's conduct. What must the court decide first?
    hint: A procedural bar.
    answer: Whether X is immune. Immunity is a preliminary, procedural question decided before the merits.
  - ask: Why should X be immune at all?
    hint: Sovereign equality.
    answer: States are juridically equal, so no state may exercise jurisdiction over another without its consent; one state's court judging another would infringe that equality.
  - ask: Does the 2004 Convention govern?
    hint: Count the ratifications.
    answer: No — it needs 30 and has 25, so it is not in force. Apply customary international law, using the Convention's articles as a guide because they mostly reflect custom.
  - ask: X files a defence, argues for a year, loses, then claims immunity. Result?
    hint: Arts 7–9.
    answer: Too late. Participating in the proceedings waived immunity (art 8), and a waived immunity cannot be reclaimed when judgment goes against the state.
  - ask: Suppose the defendant is X's export-licensing agency, not X itself. Is it 'the State'?
    hint: Art 2(1)(b).
    answer: Yes, to the extent it is entitled to perform acts in the exercise of X's sovereign authority.
