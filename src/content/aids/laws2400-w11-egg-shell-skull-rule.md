plain: Once the defendant causes harm of a foreseeable kind, they take the plaintiff as they find them. A hidden weakness, or a high earning capacity, that makes the loss bigger is the defendant's problem.
formal: "'The defendant must take the plaintiff with all his weaknesses, beliefs and reactions as well as his capacities and attributes, physical, social and economic' (Nader v Urban Transit Authority of NSW; see Kavanagh v Akhtar). In Tame, Gummow and Kirby JJ said that, if liability is established, the egg-shell skull rule applies to the assessment of damages."

terms:
  - term: Egg-shell skull rule
    gloss: "The defendant takes the plaintiff as found: with all his weaknesses, beliefs and reactions, and his capacities and attributes, physical, social and economic (Nader)."
  - term: Capacities and attributes
    gloss: The plaintiff's physical, social and economic characteristics, such as earning capacity, which can make the loss larger without making it too remote.
  - term: Normal fortitude
    gloss: An ordinarily robust person. Windeyer J saw a normal fortitude condition of liability as sitting badly with the egg-shell rule; NSW s 32 builds it into the mental harm duty.
misconceptions:
  - claim: The defendant is liable only for the harm a person of ordinary strength would have suffered.
    tempting: It seems fair to judge the defendant by what could be foreseen.
    correction: The defendant must take the plaintiff with all his weaknesses, beliefs and reactions (Nader); the extent of the harm need not be reasonably foreseeable.
  - claim: The rule applies only if the defendant knew of the plaintiff's weakness.
    tempting: Liability usually tracks what the defendant knew or ought to have known.
    correction: "The rule has no knowledge requirement: the defendant takes the plaintiff as found."
  - claim: The egg-shell skull rule makes a defendant liable for any reaction, however idiosyncratic.
    tempting: The rule expressly covers the plaintiff's beliefs and reactions.
    correction: It does not create liability. Where a response is so extreme or idiosyncratic that its risk is far-fetched, the defendant need not guard against it (Tame); the rule applies to damages once liability exists.
