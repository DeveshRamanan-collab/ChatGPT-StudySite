plain: Hart's rule of recognition and Kelsen's basic norm do the same job, explaining validity and the unity of a legal system, but one is a social fact and the other a presupposition.
concrete: Compare two countries. On Hart's view, what ultimately makes each country's law valid is a fact about what its officials accept as criteria, so the two rules of recognition may differ in content. On Kelsen's view, a jurist in each country presupposes the same basic norm, 'who laid down the first constitution ought to be obeyed'; only the first constitution differs.
formal: "Same function, different nature (slide 20). Hart: the rule of recognition's existence is 'a complex, empirical question of fact'; it is neither valid nor invalid; its content varies by jurisdiction. Kelsen: the basic norm is a 'juristic hypothesis', a 'postulated ultimate rule', a 'fiction'; its validity must be presupposed; its content is always the same. 'Rule of recognition' avoids 'Kelsen's view of the conflict between law and morals' (CL 293)."
terms:
  - term: Juristic hypothesis
    gloss: "Kelsen's description of the basic norm: presupposed by the jurist to make sense of validity, not a rule officials practise."
  - term: Membership vs binding force
    gloss: "Hart: a valid rule belongs to the system. Kelsen: a valid norm binds."
  - term: Conflict of law and morals
    gloss: For Kelsen a legal and a moral norm cannot both be valid at once, so no meaningful conflict arises; Hart can accept such conflicts.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Hart and Kelsen both say the ultimate rule is presupposed by legal theorists.
    tempting: Both ultimate rules sit at the top of the system and do the same job.
    correction: Only Kelsen. For Hart the rule of recognition is a social rule whose existence is a complex, empirical question of fact, shown in official practice.
  - claim: Each country's basic norm has a different content.
    tempting: Each country has its own historically first constitution.
    correction: For Kelsen the basic norm always has the same content, 'who laid down the first constitution ought to be obeyed'; only the constitution it points to differs. Hart's rules of recognition can differ in content.
  - claim: The difference between the two is only one of terminology.
    tempting: Hart knew Kelsen's work and gives his rule the same job.
    correction: Hart chose 'rule of recognition' partly 'to avoid any commitment to Kelsen's view of the conflict between law and morals' (CL 293); they also differ on fact versus presupposition, validity and content.
