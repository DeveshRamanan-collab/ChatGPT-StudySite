plain: Positivists split over Dworkin's challenge. Inclusive positivists (Hart) say a legal system may make morality a test of validity; exclusive positivists (Raz) say morality can never be such a test.
concrete: A constitution, enacted by a constituent assembly, protects equality. Parliament passes a statute by the correct procedure, but its content discriminates, and a court with the power to do so strikes it down. The statute had formal but not substantive validity. Hart says morality entered through a social source, the constitution, in that system only. Raz says the equality principle, once incorporated, is a legal criterion.
formal: "'The existence and content of the law can be identified by reference to the social sources of law (e.g. legislation, judicial decisions, social customs) without reference to morality except where the law thus identified has itself incorporated moral criteria for the identification of the law' (CL 269). Inclusive: particular systems may make moral criteria necessary or sufficient for validity. Exclusive (Raz): moral criteria can be neither."
terms:
  - term: Social sources thesis
    gloss: Law's existence and content can be identified by its social sources, such as legislation, judicial decisions and customs, without reference to morality, unless the law incorporates moral criteria (CL 269).
  - term: Inclusive (soft) positivism
    gloss: "Particular legal systems may make moral criteria necessary or sufficient for legal validity, in that system only: a contingent connection (Hart)."
  - term: Exclusive (hard) positivism
    gloss: Moral criteria can be neither sufficient nor necessary conditions for legal validity; once incorporated into law, they become legal criteria (Raz).
  - term: Substantive validity
    gloss: Validity depending on a law's content, as against formal validity from correct procedure. A constitutional equality principle can make a statute substantively invalid.
