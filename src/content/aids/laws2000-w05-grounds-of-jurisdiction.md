plain: "With a foreign element, a state needs a recognised link: where the crime happened, who committed it, who suffered, a threat to the state, or a crime so grave any state may act."
concrete: In 1988 Pan Am Flight 103, from Heathrow to New York, exploded over Lockerbie in Scotland, killing 259 people aboard and 11 on the ground; many passengers were American. The bomb was dispatched from Malta by Libyan nationals, passed through Frankfurt and was loaded at Heathrow. The UK claimed objective territorial jurisdiction because the bomb exploded in UK airspace; the US and UK sought extradition; Libya asserted jurisdiction over its nationals.
formal: "To assert prescriptive jurisdiction over a \"foreign element\", a state must justify a ground: territoriality (fundamental; objective and subjective); active nationality (nationality: \"a genuine connection of existence, interests and sentiments\", Nottebohm); passive personality (US v Yunis — controversial, subsidiary); protection (genuine threat to a vital security interest); universality (piracy; genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity, torture: Geneva Convention IV art 146)."
terms:
  - term: objective territoriality
    gloss: Jurisdiction over an offence completed on the territory even though some elements took place abroad — the UK's basis at Lockerbie.
  - term: subjective territoriality
    gloss: Jurisdiction over offences initiated or planned on the territory; important against transnational crime such as cybercrime.
  - term: passive personality
    gloss: Jurisdiction because the victim is a national (US v Yunis). Controversial — better as a subsidiary ground.
  - term: concurring jurisdiction
    gloss: Several states with grounds over one matter. No universally binding rule resolves it; treaties may require consultation (Cybercrime Convention art 22(5)).
misconceptions:
  - claim: Active nationality lets a state prosecute anyone who harms its nationals abroad.
    tempting: Both principles turn on a national.
    correction: Active nationality turns on the offender's nationality. Jurisdiction based on the victim's nationality is passive personality — controversial and better as a subsidiary ground.
  - claim: A state's territorial jurisdiction covers only its own citizens.
    tempting: States are closely tied to their nationals.
    correction: Territoriality covers all acts committed on the territory and everyone located there; citizenship is not required (the lecturer's Greek domestic violence example).
  - claim: Universal jurisdiction lets any state try any serious crime, wherever the accused is.
    tempting: "\"Universal\" sounds unlimited."
    correction: It covers listed grave offences — piracy, genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity, torture. Without a treaty it is controversial, so states usually require the accused's presence (e.g. Criminal Code Div 268).

    hint: Where was the crime completed? Who was the victim?
    answer: Objective territoriality, as the offence was completed in the UK; and passive personality, as the victim was a UK national — though that is a weaker, subsidiary ground.
  - ask: What grounds does Greece have?
    hint: Where was it initiated? Who is the offender?
    answer: Subjective territoriality, as the offence was initiated in Greece; and active nationality, as she is a Greek national.
  - ask: Mr Brawel, an Australian, seriously injures two Americans holidaying in Bali. Who can act?
    hint: Place, offender, victims.
    answer: Indonesia on territoriality; Australia on active nationality; the US only on passive personality, which is controversial and better as a subsidiary ground.
  - ask: An XWZ member who tortured civilians and committed crimes against humanity in State A flees to Germany. Can Germany act without a territorial or nationality link?
    hint: Which crimes need no link?
    answer: "Yes, on universal jurisdiction: crimes against humanity and torture are offences any state may prosecute. His presence in Germany supplies the territorial link states usually require."
  - ask: Several states can claim the same case. Which goes first?
    hint: Slide 49.
    answer: No universally binding rule decides. Treaties may require consultation (Cybercrime Convention art 22(5)); usually the state with the closest link is best placed to investigate and try.

    test: "All acts on the territory and everyone located there, citizen or not. Cross-border: objective (completed there) or subjective (initiated or planned there)."
  - name: Active nationality
    test: The offender is a national, acting abroad. States largely decide who their nationals are; dual nationals can face both states.
    cases:
