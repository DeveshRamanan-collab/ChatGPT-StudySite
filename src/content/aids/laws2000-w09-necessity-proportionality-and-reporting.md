plain: Even after an armed attack, defensive force must be a last resort, kept in proportion, and reported to the Security Council at once.
concrete: Uganda claimed self-defence for its operations in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, pointing to earlier serious cross-border incidents. But its forces had taken airports and towns many hundreds of kilometres from Uganda's border. The ICJ said that would not seem proportionate to the series of transborder attacks Uganda relied on, nor necessary to end them (Armed Activities, para 147). Geographical scope is a proportionality indicator.
formal: "Necessity and proportionality are not stated in art 51 but are customary (Nicaragua). Necessity: no other means of halting the attack — a last resort; measures taking effect months later were not necessary (Nicaragua [237]). Proportionality: commensurate with the attack, or balanced against halting or preventing further attack; the Court \"cannot close its eyes to the scale of the whole operation\" (Oil Platforms [77]). Art 51: report immediately."
terms:
  - term: necessity
    gloss: "No other means of halting the armed attack than force; self-defence is a last resort. Indicator: the temporal link."
  - term: proportionality
    gloss: "Two approaches: commensurate with the attack, or measured against its broader effects and the aim of halting or preventing further attack."
  - term: temporal link
    gloss: The time between attack and response. Measures that took effect months later were not necessary (Nicaragua [237]).
  - term: reporting (art 51)
    gloss: Measures taken in self-defence "shall be immediately reported to the Security Council".
misconceptions:
  - claim: A proportionate response must use the same weapon and number of strikes as the attack.
    tempting: The first approach says "commensurate".
    correction: "Commensurate means proportionate in scale, not identical in means: a missile strike need not be answered by a missile."
  - claim: Once an armed attack has occurred, any defensive response is lawful.
    tempting: The attack opens the right of self-defence.
    correction: Necessity and proportionality are separate customary conditions (Nicaragua) that limit every response.
  - claim: Failing to report to the Security Council automatically makes self-defence unlawful.
    tempting: Art 51 says measures "shall be" reported, and the slides list reporting among the conditions.
    correction: "The lecturer: reporting is not in itself a condition like necessity and proportionality, but in Nicaragua a failure to report suggested the state did not believe it was acting in self-defence."

    hint: Last resort; timing.
    answer: "Necessity: talks were on offer and the attack had stopped. A long delay suggests other means existed (Nicaragua [237])."
  - ask: What does necessity require in general?
    hint: Last resort.
    answer: "That the state had no other means of halting the armed attack than force: if it could achieve the same result without force, self-defence is not justified."
  - ask: After repeated missile attacks, A destroys B's launch sites, causing more damage than the attacks did. Proportionate?
    hint: Two approaches.
    answer: On the first approach it may look excessive; on the second it is balanced against the aim of halting or preventing further attacks. It depends on the facts.
  - ask: Which indicators would you cite?
    hint: Oil Platforms [77]; Armed Activities [147].
    answer: Destructive scope — the scale of the whole operation (Oil Platforms); geographical scope — taking towns hundreds of kilometres away was not proportionate (Armed Activities).
  - ask: What must A do with its defensive measures, and until when may it act?
    hint: Art 51's last sentence.
    answer: Report them immediately to the Security Council; and act only until the Council has taken the measures necessary to maintain international peace and security.
