plain: "\"Land\" covers the soil, things fixed to it, and the airspace up to the height needed for ordinary use. To sue for trespass you need exclusive possession of the land, not ownership."
concrete: "In Newington v Windeyer (1985) 3 NSWLR 555 the plaintiff had occupied a vacant area of land next to their property, The Grove, for 50 years without being its registered owner. They hosted parties there, maintained the garden and trees, and paid rates. The court held they could sue someone trying to use the land: their conduct showed exclusive possession, which — not ownership — is what trespass requires."
formal: Land includes the soil, fixtures such as a house, factory or crops before harvest, and airspace — but only to the height necessary for the ordinary use and enjoyment of the land (Bernstein v Skyviews). Exclusive possession at the time of the trespass, not ownership, is needed to sue; possession is factual control plus an intention to exclude all others (Newington v Windeyer).

terms:
  - term: Airspace
    gloss: Protected as land only up to the height necessary for the ordinary use and enjoyment of the land and its structures (Bernstein v Skyviews).
  - term: Usque ad coelum
    gloss: The maxim that land runs up to the heavens; Griffiths J in Bernstein called its literal application a fanciful notion and rejected it.
  - term: Exclusive possession
    gloss: Factual control plus an intention to exclude all others, at the time of the trespass. Needed to sue; ownership is not.
  - term: Fixtures
    gloss: Things forming part of the land, such as a house, a factory or crops before harvesting.

  - ask: In Bernstein v Skyviews, what did the aircraft do, and how high was it?
    hint: Several hundred feet; a photograph.
    answer: It flew several hundred feet above Lord Bernstein's estate and took a photograph of his house.
  - ask: Did it interfere with any use of the land?
    hint: Griffiths J's balance.
    answer: No. Above the height needed for ordinary use and enjoyment the owner has no greater rights than the public, so there was no trespass.
  - ask: Why, then, was a 24 cm sign a trespass in Kelsen?
    hint: Where was the intrusion?
    answer: It was low, inside the airspace needed for ordinary use of the land, so it invaded protected airspace.
  - ask: Who may sue for trespass to land?
    hint: Ownership or possession?
    answer: Whoever has exclusive possession at the time — a tenant, or a non-owner occupier like the plaintiff in Newington v Windeyer.
  - ask: How did that plaintiff prove possession?
    hint: Two elements, shown by conduct.
    answer: Factual control and an intention to exclude others, shown by hosting parties, maintaining the garden and trees, and paying rates.
