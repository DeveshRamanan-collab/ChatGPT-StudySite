terms:
  - term: Act vs omission
    gloss: Doing something carelessly, versus failing to do something. For physical harm, an omission needs more than reasonable foreseeability to found a duty.
  - term: Special relationship
    gloss: A recognised relationship that attracts a duty more readily, such as occupier–entrant, employer–employee or manufacturer–consumer.
  - term: Public authority
    gloss: A defendant exercising statutory powers, whose duty may be shaped by those powers and its limited resources.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Once harm was foreseeable, every kind of harm is analysed the same way.
    tempting: Foreseeability is the common starting point for every duty question.
    correction: Not all harms are legally recognised in the same way. Courts ask how the harm was caused, who the defendant is and the nature of the harm, and each answer brings its own extra requirements.
  - claim: Classifying the facts, e.g. "an omission causing physical harm", establishes the duty.
    tempting: The classification names the relevant rules, so it feels like an answer.
    correction: Classification only tells you which rules apply next. Duty, breach and causation must still be analysed separately.
  - claim: A failure to act is treated just like a careless act.
    tempting: Either way the plaintiff is harmed by the defendant's carelessness.
    correction: Physical harm caused by an act is the typical case. Where physical harm is caused by an omission, more than reasonable foreseeability is required (Hargrave v Goldman).
