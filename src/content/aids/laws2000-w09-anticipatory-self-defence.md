plain: May a state strike when an attack is coming but hasn't happened? Art 51 says "if an armed attack occurs"; acting against a manifestly imminent attack has more support, but it remains a grey area.
concrete: After 9/11, the US National Security Strategy of 2002 claimed the option of acting pre-emptively "to counter a sufficient threat to our national security … even if uncertainty remains as to the time and place of the enemy's attack". This Bush doctrine, the lecturer says, underpinned the 2003 invasion of Iraq but won very limited support. States are readier to accept a strike against a manifestly imminent attack — such as intercepting a missile already on its way.
formal: Art 51 is triggered "if an armed attack occurs". There is serious controversy between those arguing anticipatory, unilateral self-defence is customary international law and those rejecting it as incompatible with art 51's strict wording. The US National Security Strategy 2002's expanded definition had little support; there is more support if a specific attack is manifestly imminent, and all other restrictions apply. Modern high-speed weapons keep the debate open.

  - term: manifestly imminent
    gloss: A specific attack that is clearly about to happen — where support for anticipatory self-defence is strongest.
  - term: Bush doctrine
    gloss: The US National Security Strategy 2002 claim to act pre-emptively against a sufficient threat, even if the time and place of attack are uncertain.
