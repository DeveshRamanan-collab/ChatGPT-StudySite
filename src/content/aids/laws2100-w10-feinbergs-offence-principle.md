plain: "Feinberg: preventing serious offence to others can justify a criminal prohibition, but only if almost anyone would be offended and the offence cannot reasonably be avoided."
concrete: Mill's state may not protect people from being offended. Feinberg answers critics who say harm alone does not reach far enough. Because offence reaches further than harm, he narrows it with two tests. The reaction must be one almost anyone chosen at random would have, regardless of sex, age or race. And the offended person must be unable to avoid the behaviour reasonably, or only at real inconvenience. Both must be met.
formal: "Feinberg (1926–2004): 'It is always a good reason in support of a proposed criminal prohibition that it would probably be an effective way of preventing serious offense...to persons other than the actor, and that it is probably a necessary means to that end.' A supplement to the harm principle; two tests must both be met: universality and reasonable avoidability (slide 13)."
terms:
  - term: Offence principle
    gloss: Preventing serious offence to persons other than the actor is a good reason for a criminal prohibition, if probably effective and necessary (Feinberg); a supplement to the harm principle.
  - term: Standard of universality
    gloss: For offensiveness to justify coercion, it must be a reaction expected from almost anyone chosen at random, regardless of sex, age, race, etc.
  - term: Standard of reasonable avoidability
    gloss: You can expect a response from the state only if you cannot reasonably avoid the behaviour, or avoiding it would be inconvenient for you.
