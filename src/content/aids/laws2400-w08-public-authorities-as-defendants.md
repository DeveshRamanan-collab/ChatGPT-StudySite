plain: "Government bodies can be sued in negligence, but it is harder than suing a private person: courts need more than foreseeability, and will not second-guess high-level policy or how limited public money is spent."
concrete: "Police responding to a call shot an agitated man in the course of the operation. For police, the starting point is that no duty is owed to individual members of the public in investigating and suppressing crime (Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire). But that is not an immunity: in Zalewski v Turcarolo the operational response attracted a duty of care."
formal: Public authorities are bodies created by statutes to perform public functions or act in the public interest. With no Crown immunity now, they may be sued in negligence. They differ from private defendants in their nature and responsibilities, in exercising statutory functions, and in the public policy considerations courts weigh. Sutherland Shire Council v Heyman (1985) considered the policy versus operation distinction and general reliance.

terms:
  - term: Public authority
    gloss: A body created by statute to perform public functions or act in the public interest, such as the State, councils, statutory authorities and the police.
  - term: Policy vs operation
    gloss: Policy is the high-level approach (largely off limits to courts); operations is how the authority carries it out (more arguable).
  - term: Crown immunity
    gloss: The old rule protecting government from tort claims. It no longer applies, so public authorities can be sued in negligence.
misconceptions:
  - claim: A public authority can only be challenged through administrative law, not sued in negligence.
    tempting: Administrative law remedies exist for authorities that abuse their discretion.
    correction: There is no Crown immunity now from tortious liability, so public authorities may also be sued in negligence; a duty is simply harder to establish.
  - claim: Hill v Chief Constable makes police immune from negligence claims.
    tempting: Hill starts from no duty to members of the public in investigating crime.
    correction: "It is not an immunity. Negligent operational conduct can attract a duty: Zalewski v Turcarolo (police shooting during an operational response)."
  - claim: If an authority had a statutory power to intervene, it owed a duty to use it.
    tempting: The power would have prevented the harm, so failing to use it looks careless.
    correction: A statutory power to intervene is not of itself a duty to exercise it (Stuart v Kirkland-Veenstra).
socratic:

    answer: "It is the paradigm: an act causing physical injury, with nothing special about the parties. Reasonable foreseeability is typically enough."
  - ask: Now the defendant is a council. Why might foreseeability no longer be enough?
    hint: Think about money, and who decides how to spend it.
    answer: Resources are limited. A court saying the council should have spent money on this would intrude on the executive (separation of powers). Indeterminacy is also a concern.
  - ask: A State decides to let an industry largely regulate itself. Can a court review that choice in negligence?
    hint: Is this policy or operations?
    answer: "It is high-level policy, which is pretty much off limits: setting policy is the executive's job."
  - ask: Then an inspector the State does send carelessly misreads a test. Is that different?
    hint: Is this setting policy or carrying it out?
    answer: It is operational, carrying the policy out, so a duty is more arguable, though hurdles remain.
  - ask: Do police owe a duty to each member of the public to catch criminals?
    hint: Hill, then Zalewski.
    answer: "No: no duty in investigating and suppressing crime (Hill), because of conflicting responsibilities, resource allocation and defensive policing. But negligent operational conduct can attract a duty (Zalewski)."
