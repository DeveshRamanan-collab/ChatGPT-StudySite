plain: Makers and distributors of goods can owe consumers a duty of care for defective products, but a foreseeable risk does not mean they must eliminate it at any cost.
concrete: "Consumers caught Hepatitis A after eating oysters grown in Wallis Lake, NSW, where faecal contamination had washed in after a storm. The grower's \"depuration\" process only reduced the viral load. The High Court held the NSW government owed no duty, and the grower owed a duty but did not breach it: the risk's probability did not justify the \"difficult, expensive and inconvenient alleviating action\" the consumers argued for."
formal: Before Donoghue v Stevenson, owners of "dangerous things" had a duty to keep them from third parties; there was none for non-dangerous things. After Donoghue and Grant, ordinary negligence principles can ground liability for defective products, alongside contract, sale of goods law and the Australian Consumer Law. A foreseeable risk does not alone establish breach (Graham Barclay Oysters v Ryan, Gummow and Hayne JJ).
