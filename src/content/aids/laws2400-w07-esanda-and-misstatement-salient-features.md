plain: Foreseeing that someone might lose money relying on your words is not enough for a duty. Courts weigh several features of the relationship, especially reliance and assumption of responsibility.
concrete: "Esanda lent money to companies associated with Excel, relying on Excel's accounts, which PMH had audited and certified. Excel went into receivership and Esanda lost money. Esanda argued it belonged to a class who might reasonably rely on audited accounts. The High Court found no duty: there was no request, no intention to induce Esanda to act, and no assumption of responsibility; an audit informs the company and its shareholders."
formal: "Esanda Finance Corp v PMH (1997): no duty, as no circumstances established a relationship of proximity. Dawson J: mere foreseeability does not, where the only harm is pure economic loss, give rise to a duty; the law requires \"a special relationship ... described as a relationship of proximity\". \"Proximity\" is the historical label; the modern approach treats the factors as salient features (Sullivan v Moody)."
analogy:
  text: An audit report is like a letter addressed to the company and its shareholders. Others may read it over their shoulder, and the writer may foresee that, but it was not written for them.

terms:
  - term: Proximity (historical)
    gloss: In pre-2000 cases like Esanda, an overarching principle covering the special relationship; not the much narrower "proximity" in the salient features list.
  - term: Salient features
    gloss: The factors courts weigh, beyond reasonable foreseeability, to decide whether a duty arises outside the paradigm (Sullivan v Moody).
  - term: Assumption of responsibility
    gloss: The defendant's undertaking of a task from which acceptance of reasonable care can be inferred; one of the most important features.

    correction: "Not a rigid checklist: the factors cross over and courts look at the circumstances as a whole. Foreseeability must always be ticked; the rest are weighed."

socratic:
  - ask: Was it foreseeable that a lender might rely on Excel's audited accounts and lose money?
    hint: Who reads audited accounts?
    answer: Yes. Foreseeability was present, but for pure economic loss it is not enough on its own.
  - ask: Why not, according to Dawson J?
    hint: Think about the bounds of liability.
    answer: A duty based only on foreseeability of financial loss would extend liability in negligence beyond acceptable bounds. A special relationship of proximity is also needed.
  - ask: Esanda said it belonged to a class who might reasonably rely on audited accounts. What was missing?
    hint: Look at PMH's side of the relationship.
    answer: There was no request, no intention by PMH to induce Esanda to act, and no assumption of responsibility towards it.
  - ask: Who is an audit for?
    hint: The lecturer, a former auditor, answers this.
    answer: It informs the company and its shareholders about the company's accounts; it is not for investors or financiers to rely on.
  - ask: What policy reasons did McHugh J give against auditors owing third parties a duty?
    hint: Four reasons on the slide.
    answer: Higher auditing costs, more complex litigation, the risk of indeterminate liability, and difficulties proving reliance.
elements:
  - name: Reasonable foreseeability
    test: Was loss to someone in the plaintiff's position reasonably foreseeable? Always required, but never enough for pure economic loss.
    cases:
      - esanda-finance-corp-v-pmh
  - name: Indeterminacy of liability
    test: Would a duty expose the defendant to a class that cannot be narrowed or defined, such as everyone who reads public accounts?
    cases:
      - esanda-finance-corp-v-pmh
  - name: Category of relationship
    test: "What is the relationship: friends at a social gathering, or a commercial or financial advisory one?"
    cases:
      - hedley-byrne-v-heller
  - name: Defendant's knowledge
    test: Did the defendant know, or ought it to have known, of matters affecting the plaintiff, including its reliance?
    cases:
      - tepko-pty-ltd-v-water-board
  - name: Reliance by the plaintiff
    test: Did the plaintiff rely, and was that reliance reasonable in the circumstances?
    cases:
      - san-sebastian-pty-ltd-v-minister
      - tepko-pty-ltd-v-water-board
  - name: Assumption of responsibility
    test: Did the defendant undertake a task from which acceptance of reasonable care can be inferred?
    cases:
      - esanda-finance-corp-v-pmh
      - mallonland-pty-ltd-v-advanta-seeds-pty-ltd
  - name: Special skill of the defendant
    test: Did the defendant have, or profess, special skill? Relevant, but not a separate requirement (Shaddock).
    cases:
      - hedley-byrne-v-heller
      - shaddock-and-associates-pty-ltd-v-parramatta-city-council
  - name: Vulnerability of the plaintiff
    test: Could the plaintiff have protected itself, by contract or its own inquiries? Sophisticated commercial parties usually can.
    cases:
      - anchorage-capital-master-offshore-ltd-v-sparkes

transfer:
  - scenario: A credit-rating agency publishes a free online rating of a listed company. Thousands of investors read it. One investor loses money after the rating proves careless and sues the agency.
    answer: Start with Esanda. Loss is foreseeable but not enough. The class of readers is open-ended (indeterminacy); the agency made no statement to this investor, had no request from it and did not assume responsibility towards it. Despite the agency's special skill, the features weigh against a duty, much as in Esanda.
