plain: An entity is a state if it has a permanent population, a defined territory, an effective government and the capacity to deal with other states. Size and form of government do not matter.
concrete: "Scotland has a permanent population, a defined territory and its own government, with a lot of independence in running its affairs. But decisions about its borders, and negotiations with other states, are made at the level of the United Kingdom. So Scotland meets three Montevideo criteria and fails the fourth: it cannot enter into international relations on its own. The lecturer adds Greenland — its own territory, population and government, but part of Denmark."
formal: "Montevideo Convention on Rights and Duties of States 1933 art 1: the state \"should possess the following qualifications: a. a permanent population; b. a defined territory; c. government; and d. capacity to enter into relations with other states\" — widely regarded as codifying customary international law. Boundaries need not be fully delimited (North Sea Continental Shelf [1969] [46]). Established statehood carries a presumption of continuity."
analogy:
  text: A four-legged stool stands only on all four legs. A leg may be short or thin, but it must be there.
  maps:
    - from: the four legs
      to: population, territory, government and capacity for relations
    - from: a short leg
      to: no minimum population or territory (Nauru, the Vatican)
    - from: the stool standing
      to: the entity being a state
  limits:
    - An established state keeps 'standing' when its government leg cracks (presumption of continuity); a real stool would fall.
    - A stool on four legs always stands, but an entity meeting the criteria through illegal force may be refused recognition (TRNC).
terms:
  - term: permanent population
    gloss: A core population living in the territory permanently. No minimum number (Nauru has about 12,000 people) and no nationality requirement.
  - term: defined territory
    gloss: Some territory of any size (the Vatican is about 0.5 sq km), with reasonably defined boundaries; border disputes do not defeat it.
  - term: effective government
    gloss: A coherent, effective exercise of public power over the territory and population — "a stable political community supporting a legal order to the exclusion of others" (Crawford).
  - term: presumption of continuity
    gloss: Once statehood is established, ineffective government does not necessarily end it (Somalia, Syria, Yemen); new entities face the stricter test.
misconceptions:
  - claim: A state must have fully delimited, undisputed land borders.
    tempting: "'Defined territory' sounds like fixed lines on a map."
    correction: There is "no rule that the land frontiers of a State must be fully delimited and defined" (North Sea Continental Shelf [46]); border disputes are common among states.
  - claim: The population must be large, or consist mainly of the entity's own nationals.
    tempting: Statehood and nationality are linked in everyday thinking, and tiny populations seem fragile.
    correction: No fixed or minimum number is prescribed (Nauru about 12,000; China about 1.4 billion), and the criterion does not relate to nationality — only a core population living there permanently.
  - claim: A state whose government loses control of much of its territory stops being a state.
    tempting: Effective government is one of the four criteria.
    correction: "Once statehood is established it carries a presumption of continuity: Somalia, Syria and Yemen remain states though described as fragile or failed. Effectiveness matters mainly for new entities."

what-if:
  - change: The entity is ruled by an unelected military council.
    effect: No difference. No specific form of government is prescribed — dictatorships and democracies alike can be states, because each state chooses its own system as a matter of sovereignty.
  - change: An established state loses control of half its territory in a civil war.
    effect: It remains a state. The presumption of continuity means ineffective government does not necessarily affect established statehood, as with Somalia, Syria and Yemen.

    effect: "Meeting the criteria may not be enough: non-recognition may prevent statehood where the means used were illegal (Friendly Relations Declaration; TRNC). Move on to recognition."
