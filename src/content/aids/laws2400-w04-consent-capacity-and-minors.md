plain: Consent only counts if the person could understand what they agreed to. Adults are assumed able; a child can be too once mature enough, but a court can still step in.

formal: "Consent must come from a competent person: one with sufficient understanding to comprehend the act and its consequences. Adults are presumed competent (rebuttable). A minor can consent once she has \"sufficient understanding and intelligence to enable her to understand fully what is proposed\" (Gillick, approved in Marion's Case). Parents cannot consent to non-therapeutic treatment not in the child's interests (Marion's Case); parens patriae reaches anyone under 18 (X v SCHN)."

terms:
  - term: Capacity (competence)
    gloss: Sufficient understanding to comprehend the act and its consequences.
  - term: Gillick competence
    gloss: A minor's capacity to consent once she has sufficient understanding and intelligence to understand fully what is proposed.
    example: A 15-year-old consenting to contraceptive advice in Gillick.
  - term: Parens patriae jurisdiction
    gloss: The Supreme Court's protective jurisdiction over people under 18, which can override even a competent minor's decision.
    example: X v Sydney Children's Hospital Network.
  - term: Non-therapeutic treatment
    gloss: Treatment not for the child's illness, such as sterilisation, which parents cannot authorise when it is not in the child's interests.
    example: Marion's Case.

what-if:
  - change: The 15-year-old in Gillick had not yet reached sufficient understanding of what was proposed.
    effect: She could not give valid consent herself. As Lord Scarman put it, until the child achieves capacity, the parental right to decide continues, save in exceptional circumstances.
  - change: X had already turned 18 when he refused the transfusion.
    effect: The parens patriae jurisdiction over minors would not reach him (the orders were varied to expire on his 18th birthday); as a competent adult his refusal would be respected, as in Rossiter.
  - change: The patient in Rossiter had been unconscious rather than competent.
    effect: The presumption of capacity would be displaced, so he could neither consent nor refuse; whether treatment was justified would turn on necessity (In re F) instead.
