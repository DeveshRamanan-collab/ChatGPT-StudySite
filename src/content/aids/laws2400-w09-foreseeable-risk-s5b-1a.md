plain: A risk is foreseeable if the defendant knew, or should have known, about it at the time. Even an unlikely risk counts, as long as it is not far-fetched.
concrete: "Shirt was water skiing on a lake when he fell and struck his head on the bottom, where the water was only about 3.5–4 feet deep; he became quadriplegic. He said he was misled by a council \"deep water\" sign, put up to warn swimmers of deeper channels by a jetty. Mason J: a reasonable council might see the sign was ambiguous and could induce a skier to ski there believing the water deep."
formal: "Section 5B(1)(a): the risk must be foreseeable, \"a risk of which the person knew or ought to have known\". Wyong: a risk which is not far-fetched or fanciful is real and therefore foreseeable; foreseeability and likelihood are two different things. At breach the test is more specific than at duty: the actual conduct and actual risk. Even extremely foolhardy plaintiff conduct must be foreseen (Nagle)."
terms:
  - term: Constructive knowledge
    gloss: What a reasonable person in the defendant's position ought to have known, even if the defendant did not actually know it.
  - term: Not far-fetched or fanciful
    gloss: "Mason J's test in Wyong: such a risk is real and therefore foreseeable, however unlikely."
  - term: Fact-value complex
    gloss: "McHugh J in Swain: reasonable foreseeability carries inherent questions of fairness, policy, practicality, proportion, expense and justice."
misconceptions:
  - claim: A risk that was very unlikely to happen cannot be foreseeable.
    tempting: Foreseeing something sounds like expecting it to happen.
    correction: "Mason J in Wyong: foreseeability and likelihood are two different things. A risk quite unlikely to occur may nevertheless be plainly foreseeable; likelihood is weighed later, in (b) and (c)."
  - claim: The plaintiff must show the exact way the accident happened was foreseeable.
    tempting: Breach-stage foreseeability is described as more specific.
    correction: Neither the precise manner nor the particular injury need be foreseeable, only a reasonably foreseeable risk of injury of some kind to someone such as the plaintiff (as in Chapman v Hearse).
  - claim: A defendant need not foresee a plaintiff behaving foolishly.
    tempting: It seems unfair to make defendants guard against recklessness.
    correction: The defendant must foresee and guard against inadvertent, negligent or even extremely foolhardy conduct by the plaintiff, even though it is unlikely (Nagle v Rottnest Island Board; McLean v Tedman).

    answer: Perhaps not, but it was not far-fetched or fanciful. An unlikely risk can still be plainly foreseeable; its likelihood matters later.
  - ask: Do we judge what the council knew with today's hindsight?
    hint: When is foreseeability tested?
    answer: No. It is what the defendant knew or ought to have known at the time of the conduct, not with hindsight.
