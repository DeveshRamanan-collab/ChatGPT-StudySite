plain: If the plaintiff's own carelessness helped cause their injury, their damages are cut by what is fair, given each side's share of the responsibility.
concrete: Early one morning the defendant parked a truck in the middle of a six-lane road to unload produce. The plaintiff, drunk and speeding, drove into it. The trial judge held the defendant liable in negligence but found 70% contributory negligence against the plaintiff, so his damages were reduced rather than refused. The Full Court of South Australia instead put the entire responsibility on the plaintiff; the High Court held the defendant's negligence was a cause.
formal: Contributory negligence results in a partial reduction based on what is 'just and equitable having regard to [their] share in the responsibility for the damage' (Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1965 (NSW) s 9(1)). The standard is a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position, on what they knew or ought to have known (CLA s 5R). A causal link is required (Caswell v Powell Duffryn).
terms:
  - term: Just and equitable reduction
    gloss: The reduction fitting the plaintiff's share in the responsibility for the damage, found by weighing each party's whole conduct and relative blameworthiness (Pennington v Norris).
  - term: Standard of contributory negligence (s 5R)
    gloss: "The principles for negligence apply: a reasonable person in the position of the plaintiff, judged on what they knew or ought to have known at the time."
  - term: Causal requirement
    gloss: The plaintiff's lack of care for their own safety must be causally linked to the injury (Caswell v Powell Duffryn); ordinary principles of causation apply.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Where both parties were careless, the damages are simply halved.
    tempting: Equal shares seem fair when both are at fault.
    correction: The court examines the whole conduct of each negligent party and their relative blameworthiness (Pennington v Norris), then reduces by what is just and equitable.
  - claim: Any careless conduct by the plaintiff reduces their damages.
    tempting: The plaintiff was at fault, so some reduction seems deserved.
    correction: There must be a causal link between the plaintiff's lack of care for their own safety and their injury (Caswell v Powell Duffryn); ordinary causation principles apply.
  - claim: A plaintiff is judged only on what they actually knew, so an unknown hazard cannot count against them.
    tempting: Blaming someone for what they did not know seems unfair.
    correction: "Section 5R(2): the standard is a reasonable person in their position, decided on what the plaintiff knew or ought to have known at the time."
socratic:
  - ask: In March v Stramare the drunk, speeding plaintiff hit a truck parked mid-road. What did the trial judge do with his carelessness?
    hint: Liable, but...
    answer: Held the defendant liable but found 70% contributory negligence against the plaintiff, reducing his damages rather than defeating the claim.
  - ask: What standard is the plaintiff's own care measured against?
    hint: s 5R.
    answer: "The same principles as for negligence: a reasonable person in the position of the plaintiff, on what the plaintiff knew or ought to have known at the time."
  - ask: A cyclist without a helmet suffers only a broken foot. Can the missing helmet reduce her damages for the foot?
    hint: Caswell.
    answer: No. Without a causal link between that lack of care and the foot injury, there is no contributory negligence for it.
  - ask: How does the court fix the size of the reduction?
    hint: Pennington v Norris.
    answer: By examining the whole conduct of each negligent party and considering their relative blameworthiness, then setting what is just and equitable.
  - ask: Damages are $200,000 and a 30% reduction is just and equitable. What does the plaintiff recover?
    hint: Reduction, not recovery.
    answer: "$140,000: the $60,000 reduction comes off the damages otherwise recoverable."
elements:
  - name: Lack of care (s 5R)
    test: Did the plaintiff fail to take precautions a reasonable person in their position would have taken, on what they knew or ought to have known?
    cases:
      - davies-v-swan-motor-co-swansea-ltd
      - jones-v-livox-quarries-ltd
    authorities:
      - civil-liability-act-2002-nsw-s5r
  - name: Causal link
    test: Did that lack of care for the plaintiff's own safety contribute to the injury? Ordinary principles of causation apply.
    cases:
      - caswell-v-powell-duffryn-associated-collieries
  - name: Just and equitable reduction
    test: Weigh the whole conduct and relative blameworthiness of each party; reduce the damages by the plaintiff's share in the responsibility.
    cases:
      - pennington-v-norris
    authorities:
      - law-reform-miscellaneous-provisions-act-1965-nsw-s9
what-if:
  - change: The plaintiff's lack of care played no part in causing the injury.
    effect: "No reduction: contributory negligence requires a causal link between the plaintiff's lack of care and the injury (Caswell v Powell Duffryn)."
  - change: The court finds the plaintiff's share of responsibility is total.
    effect: In NSW, CLA s 5S allows a reduction of 100% if the court thinks it just and equitable, with the result that the claim is defeated.
  - change: A reasonable person in the plaintiff's position could not have known of the hazard.
    effect: "The argument weakens: contributory negligence is judged on what the plaintiff knew or ought to have known at the time (s 5R(2)(b))."
boundary:

    why: Still contributory negligence territory, but the employment context — familiarity, repetition, urgency, preoccupation — may be taken into account (McLean v Tedman; Staveley Iron v Jones).
transfer:
  - scenario: A pedestrian, looking at her phone, steps onto a crossing without checking for traffic. A driver who is speeding heavily cannot stop and hits her.
    answer: Her inattention is a failure to take a precaution a reasonable pedestrian would take (s 5R), and it is causally linked to her injury (Caswell). The court weighs the whole conduct and relative blameworthiness of each — heavy speeding against a moment's inattention (Pennington v Norris) — and reduces her damages by what is just and equitable (1965 Act s 9(1)).
