plain: "Since 1932, the starting point for duty is: take reasonable care not to harm people you can foresee your actions might affect. Courts later added limits for harder cases."
concrete: "In August 1928, Mrs Donoghue's friend bought her a ginger beer at a café in Scotland. It came in an opaque bottle bearing the manufacturer's name, David Stevenson. As it was poured, a decomposed snail floated out. Donoghue, who had no contract with Stevenson, sued him for shock and severe gastro-enteritis. The House of Lords, by 3:2, allowed her appeal: a manufacturer can owe the consumer a duty of care."
formal: "Lord Atkin in Donoghue v Stevenson: \"You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour\": persons so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation. Australia rejected Anns (Sutherland Shire Council v Heyman) and never incorporated Caparo; since the late 1990s the High Court uses salient factors."
terms:
  - term: Neighbour principle
    gloss: "Lord Atkin's test: take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour."
  - term: "\"Neighbour\""
    gloss: Persons so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation.
  - term: Proximity (as touchstone)
    gloss: "Deane J in Heyman: the parties' relationship as a \"touchstone and control\" of when a duty is owed; indicators include reliance, assumption of responsibility and control."
  - term: Salient factors approach
    gloss: "The High Court's contextual method since the late 1990s: begin with reasonable foreseeability, then weigh factors about the parties' relationship."
misconceptions:
  - claim: Australian courts decide duty using the Caparo three-stage test.
    tempting: Caparo is a well-known House of Lords test, and English cases have shaped Australian tort law.
    correction: Caparo has never been incorporated into Australian law, and Anns was rejected in Sutherland Shire Council v Heyman. Australia uses established categories and the salient factors approach.
  - claim: If harm was reasonably foreseeable, the neighbour principle settles that a duty is owed.
    tempting: Lord Atkin's test is framed around foreseeability.
    correction: The neighbour principle is a starting position only. Omissions, non-physical initial harm and special parties usually need more than reasonable foreseeability.
  - claim: Mrs Donoghue could not sue because her friend, not she, bought the drink.
    tempting: Only the purchaser had a contract with the seller.
    correction: A duty of care exists independently of contract. The House of Lords (3:2) allowed Donoghue's appeal against the manufacturer.
socratic:
  - ask: Mrs Donoghue had no contract with the manufacturer. Why could she still sue him?
    hint: Does negligence depend on contract?
    answer: "Negligence is a tort in itself: the manufacturer could owe the consumer a duty of care independently of any contract."
  - ask: Who, in law, is my neighbour?
    hint: Lord Atkin's answer.
    answer: Persons so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation when directing my mind to the acts or omissions in question.
  - ask: Dr Grant's underpants contained free sulphites. Why did Donoghue v Stevenson apply?
    hint: Could anyone have found the defect before he wore them?
    answer: The defect was hidden and unknown to the consumer, and the garments reached him with no reasonable possibility of intermediate examination.
  - ask: What did English courts do with negligence from the 1960s?
    hint: Hedley Byrne, Dorset Yacht, Anns, Caparo.
    answer: "They expanded it into pure economic loss and public authority liability, and proposed general tests: Anns (two stages), then Caparo (three stages)."
  - ask: Did Australia follow those general tests?
    hint: Heyman, then the late 1990s.
    answer: No. Anns was rejected in Sutherland Shire Council v Heyman and Caparo never adopted. Australia used proximity as a touchstone in the 1980s, then the contextual salient factors approach.
what-if:
  - change: In Grant, a reasonable inspection before wearing would have revealed the sulphites.
    effect: The Donoghue principle applies only where the defect is "hidden and unknown to the consumer, otherwise the directness of cause and effect is absent", so the duty would not follow in the same way.
  - change: Mrs Donoghue had bought the ginger beer herself.
    effect: "She would then also have a contract claim against the seller, but nothing changes against the manufacturer: the duty arises independently of contract, just as Dr Grant's contract with the retailer did not matter."
boundary:

  - text: Omissions, initial harm that is not physical, or special parties.
    why: "Outside the paradigm: more than reasonable foreseeability is usually required, drawn from decided cases."
