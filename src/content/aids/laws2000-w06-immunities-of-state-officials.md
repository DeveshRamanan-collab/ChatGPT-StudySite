plain: State officials are protected abroad because they act for their state. Top leaders are fully immune while in office; every official stays immune for official acts, even after leaving office.
concrete: "The lecturer's example: the sitting president of Greece, on holiday in Australia, drives drunk and kills someone. An Australian court cannot try the president — a head of state in office has personal immunity, covering even private acts. Greek courts could. And if the former president visits Australia after leaving office, the immunity is gone: a drunken holiday drive was never an official act, so functional immunity does not cover it."
formal: "Officials' immunity is customary and belongs to the state: foreign ministers' immunities are \"not granted for their personal benefit, but to ensure the effective performance of their functions\" (Arrest Warrant [2002] ICJ Rep 3). Functional immunity (ratione materiae): official acts, \"under the colour of authority\"; survives office. Personal immunity (ratione personae): heads of state, heads of government, foreign ministers; covers private acts; ends with office (Djibouti v France)."

terms:
  - term: functional immunity (ratione materiae)
    gloss: "Attaches to the act: official acts done with official authority. Continues after the official leaves office; waivable only with the state's consent."
  - term: personal immunity (ratione personae)
    gloss: "Attaches to the office: complete, even for private acts, but only for very high-ranking officials and only while in office."
  - term: "\"under the colour of authority\""
    gloss: "The test of an official act: done by the individual with official authority."
