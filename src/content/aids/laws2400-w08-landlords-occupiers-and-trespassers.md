plain: Landlords and occupiers usually owe people on their premises a duty of care. The real argument is about how far it goes and what reasonable steps were needed.
concrete: "In Jones v Bartlett, the tenant's son walked through a glass door and was injured. The door met the Australian standards when the house was built, but not when it was leased, and the landlord did not know. The High Court held the landlord owed a duty of care but had not breached it: the glass door was not held to be a dangerous defect."
formal: "Cavalier v Pope (1906), no landlord duty for defective premises, was rejected in Northern Sandblasting v Harris (1997). Jones v Bartlett (Gummow and Hayne JJ): premises reasonably fit for habitation as a domestic residence; do not let premises with defects known or knowable to make them unsafe; the duty is discharged by reasonable steps to ascertain defects and remove them or make the premises safe."
terms:
  - term: Occupier
    gloss: A person or entity who controls the relevant land; not necessarily the owner.
  - term: Lawful entrant
    gloss: Someone entitled to be on the land, such as a customer or invited guest; owed a duty whose extent depends on the relationship, purpose of entry and type of premises.
  - term: Trespasser
    gloss: Someone on land without permission. Under Cardy, they trespass at their own risk; the occupier must only refrain from intentional or wanton harm.
misconceptions:
  - claim: A landlord owes no duty for defects, because the tenant is in control.
    tempting: "That was the old reasoning: the tenant is there and can see the defects."
    correction: The Cavalier v Pope rule was rejected in Northern Sandblasting v Harris. Landlords owe a duty; Jones v Bartlett is about its content.
  - claim: A landlord must guarantee that the premises meet current building standards.
    tempting: The door in Jones v Bartlett failed the standards at the time of letting.
    correction: The duty is to take reasonable steps to ascertain defects and to remove them or make the premises safe. The door was not a dangerous defect, so there was no breach.
  - claim: Trespassers are owed the same duty as lawful entrants.
    tempting: Everyone on land can be injured by the same hazards.
    correction: "Cardy: a trespasser enters at their own risk; the occupier must only refrain from intentional or wanton harm. The position is unresolved, but the trend places more responsibility on the trespasser."

  - ask: Why did the law once give landlords no duty for defective premises?
    hint: Who lives there and sees the defects?
    answer: The tenant, in the premises and in control, was thought better placed to see defects (Cavalier v Pope). The High Court rejected that rule in Northern Sandblasting.
  - ask: In Jones v Bartlett, did the landlord owe the tenant's son a duty?
    hint: Gummow and Hayne JJ moved past this question quickly.
    answer: Yes. The key issue was not whether a duty existed but its nature and extent.
  - ask: What does the landlord's duty require?
    hint: Fit for what? And what steps?
    answer: "Premises reasonably fit for habitation as a domestic residence: reasonable steps to ascertain defects that make them unsafe, and reasonable steps to remove them or make them safe."

transfer:
  - scenario: A holiday-flat owner lets a flat for a week. Before letting, she has a licensed electrician check the wiring. A hidden fault that a reasonable inspection could not detect later injures the guest.
    answer: Apply Jones v Bartlett. A duty is owed; the question is its content. The electrician's check is likely a reasonable step, so there is probably no breach.
