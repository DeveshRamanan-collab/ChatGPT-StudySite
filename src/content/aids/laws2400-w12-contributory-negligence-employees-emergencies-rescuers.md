plain: "Some careless-looking conduct is judged more gently: a worker's inattention at a familiar, urgent task, a split-second choice in an emergency, and a good-faith rescue."
formal: "Employment: inattention bred of familiarity and repetition, the urgency of the task, preoccupation with the work and other prevailing conditions may be taken into account (McLean v Tedman; Staveley Iron v Jones). Agony of the moment: conduct justified in a perceived emergency may not be contributory negligence (Avram v Gusakoski; Caterson). Rescuers: most jurisdictions protect those acting in good faith and without recklessness (eg CLA s 56)."
terms:
  - term: Momentary thoughtlessness
    gloss: An employee's inattention bred of familiarity and repetition, the urgency of the task or preoccupation with the work, which may be taken into account.
  - term: Agony of the moment
    gloss: A perceived emergency in which action that looks careless for one's own safety may be justified, and so not contributory negligence.
  - term: Good samaritan (s 56)
    gloss: A person who, in good faith and without expectation of payment or other reward, assists a person apparently injured or at risk of being injured.
misconceptions:
  - claim: An employee's inattention at work can never be contributory negligence.
    tempting: The flowchart treats workers' momentary thoughtlessness sympathetically.
    correction: The employment conditions 'may be taken into account' in deciding whether the employee was contributorily negligent; they do not rule it out.
  - claim: Anyone who reacts badly in an emergency is contributorily negligent.
    tempting: In hindsight, a safer choice was available.
    correction: Action that looks like a lack of care may not be contributory negligence where it can be justified in a situation of perceived emergency (Avram v Gusakoski; Caterson).
  - claim: CLA s 57 reduces a good samaritan's own damages for carelessness during a rescue.
    tempting: The flowchart lists rescuers under contributory negligence.
    correction: Section 57(1) protects a good samaritan from personal civil liability for acts or omissions in an emergency. The materials do not say how a rescuer's own carelessness is weighed.

    answer: Inattention bred of familiarity and repetition, the urgency of the task, her preoccupation with the work and other prevailing conditions (McLean v Tedman; Staveley Iron).
  - ask: A bus passenger jumps from a bus she sees is about to crash, and breaks her leg. Is she contributorily negligent?
    hint: Agony of the moment.
    answer: "Perhaps not: action that looks like a lack of care for her own safety may be justified in a situation of perceived emergency (Avram v Gusakoski; Caterson)."
  - ask: Who is a good samaritan in NSW?
    hint: s 56.
    answer: A person who, in good faith and without expectation of payment or other reward, comes to the assistance of a person who is apparently injured or at risk of being injured.
  - ask: What does s 57 give a good samaritan?
    hint: Liability, not damages.
    answer: No personal civil liability for acts or omissions in an emergency when assisting such a person.
  - ask: When does the good samaritan's protection not apply?
    hint: s 58.
    answer: Where their own intentional or negligent act caused the injury or risk; where alcohol or drugs voluntarily taken significantly impaired their care and they failed to take reasonable care; or where they impersonate a health, emergency or police worker.
