plain: False imprisonment is directly and totally restraining someone's freedom to move, deliberately or carelessly, without lawful justification. No walls or locks are needed.
concrete: "In Balmain New Ferry v Robertson (1906) 4 CLR 379, a passenger who missed his ferry wanted to leave the wharf, but was not allowed out without paying a further penny. The High Court held there was no false imprisonment: he had entered the wharf on that condition, so being kept until he paid had a lawful excuse."
formal: "False imprisonment is the direct total restraint of the liberty of the plaintiff by an intentional or negligent act of the defendant without lawful justification. Restraint may be without physical boundaries (Symes v Mahon) and without the plaintiff's knowledge (Meering v Graham-White Aviation). It must be total: no reasonable means of escape (Bird v Jones). Directing another to detain is direct (Coles Myer v Webster)."
terms:
  - term: Total restraint
    gloss: Restraint with no reasonable means of escape (Bird v Jones). Danger, distance, time and the legality of an escape route bear on reasonableness.
  - term: Mental coercion
    gloss: Restraint by an assertion of authority or a threat rather than physical barriers; it can imprison as effectively as a locked door.
  - term: Lawful justification
    gloss: A legal basis for the detention — statutory or common law authority, or an accepted condition of entry — which defeats the claim.
  - term: Conditional entry
    gloss: Entry on terms, such as paying to leave; enforcing that condition is a lawful excuse (Balmain New Ferry v Robertson).

socratic:
  - ask: In Balmain, was there any way for the passenger to leave the wharf?
    hint: On what terms?
    answer: Yes — by paying the penny. So the question became whether insisting on that condition was lawful.
  - ask: Why was the condition a lawful excuse?
    hint: On what terms did he enter?
    answer: He entered on the condition of paying to leave. A conditional entry is a lawful excuse, so there was no false imprisonment.
  - ask: If he could have left by another safe route, would the restraint be total?
    hint: Bird v Jones.
    answer: No. Restraint must be total, with no reasonable means of escape; a partial obstruction is not false imprisonment.
  - ask: A store manager tells a guard to lock a suspect in a room. Is the manager's act direct?
    hint: Coles Myer v Webster.
    answer: Yes. Directing, promoting or causing another person to detain the plaintiff is a direct act.
  - ask: The guard reasonably but wrongly believed the detention lawful. Does that help him?
    hint: Slide 24.
    answer: No. An intentional imprisonment can attract liability despite a reasonable but mistaken belief in its lawfulness.
elements:
  - name: Positive act
    test: The defendant must do a positive act.
  - name: Direct interference
    test: The imprisonment must be the direct result of the act; directing, promoting or causing another to detain the plaintiff counts (Coles Myer v Webster).
  - name: Intentional or negligent act
    test: Intention or negligence. A reasonable but mistaken belief in a right to detain does not excuse an intentional imprisonment.
  - name: Total restraint of liberty
    test: No reasonable means of escape (Bird v Jones). Restraint may be by mental coercion, without barriers (Symes v Mahon), even without the plaintiff knowing (Meering).
  - name: Without lawful justification
    test: Lawful authority, by statute or common law, is fatal to the claim (Ruddock v Taylor), as is an accepted condition of entry (Balmain New Ferry v Robertson).
