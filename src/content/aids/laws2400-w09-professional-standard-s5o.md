plain: Professionals are judged by the skill they profess. In NSW a professional has a defence if respected peers widely accepted what they did as competent practice.
concrete: "In Rogers v Whitaker, an eye surgeon failed to warn of a complication arising in about one in 14,000 cases. The High Court held the standard of care is that of the ordinary skilled person exercising and professing to have that special skill, and rejected the Bolam principle: the standard is not simply set by what the profession itself accepts as practice."
formal: "Section 5O(1): a professional is not liable for a professional service done as, at the time, was widely accepted in Australia by peer professional opinion as competent practice; not if the court finds that opinion irrational (s 5O(2)); acceptance need not be universal (s 5O(4)). Peterkin v Tadros [2026] NSWSC 1185: a defence; defendant's onus; if made out, it supplants s 5B."
terms:
  - term: Bolam principle
    gloss: The standard of care is set by accepted professional (medical) practice. Rejected in Rogers v Whitaker; a modified version is now in s 5O.
  - term: Peer professional opinion
    gloss: What other practitioners in Australia widely accept as competent practice; it may be one of several differing views (s 5O(3)).
  - term: Defence
    gloss: Something the defendant must establish, as opposed to an element the plaintiff proves; Peterkin treats s 5O this way.
misconceptions:
  - claim: The plaintiff must disprove s 5O before s 5B is reached.
    tempting: The plaintiff usually proves breach, and s 5O comes first.
    correction: "Peterkin v Tadros: s 5O is a defence. The defendant bears the onus of its preconditions: practising a profession, in a manner widely accepted in Australia by peers as competent practice."
  - claim: If the parties' experts disagree, the s 5O defence must fail.
    tempting: Disagreement seems to show the practice was not accepted.
    correction: Peer opinion need only be widely, not universally, accepted (s 5O(4)); differing widely accepted opinions may each be relied on (s 5O(3)).
  - claim: If the s 5O defence fails, the professional is liable.
    tempting: The defence was the professional's protection.
    correction: If s 5O fails, the plaintiff must still satisfy ss 5B and 5C to establish breach.

  - ask: An eye surgeon is sued. Against whom is the surgeon's conduct measured?
    hint: Rogers v Whitaker.
    answer: "The ordinary skilled person exercising and professing to have that special skill: an ordinary eye surgeon, not a layperson or the best surgeon."
  - ask: What did s 5O bring back?
    hint: The principle Rogers rejected.
    answer: "A modified Bolam principle: no liability if the practice was widely accepted in Australia by peer professional opinion as competent, unless that opinion is irrational."
  - ask: The defendant pleads s 5O. Which do you apply first, s 5O or s 5B?
    hint: Peterkin v Tadros.
    answer: Section 5O first. If made out, it supplants s 5B and there is no breach; there is no reason to judge the conduct against two standards.
  - ask: The court finds the peer opinion relied on irrational. What next?
    hint: Section 5O(2), then where?
    answer: The opinion cannot be relied on, so s 5O fails, and the plaintiff must establish breach under ss 5B and 5C.
