plain: Force against another state is lawful only if the Security Council authorises it or the state acts in self-defence against an armed attack. A government's invitation means there is no prohibited force at all.
concrete: "The lecturer's example: Australia uses force in New Zealand because New Zealand, under attack, asks for help. That is not even an exception to art 2(4) — New Zealand consented, so there is no violation. Cold War UK guidelines doubted a government could still consent once it had lost control of part of its territory to rebels; today states intervene at a government's request regardless, as with Iraq's request to the US against ISIS, or France in Mali."
formal: "Art 42: if art 41 measures \"would be inadequate or have proved to be inadequate\", the Council \"may take such action by air, sea, or land forces as may be necessary to maintain or restore international peace and security\". Art 51: \"the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs\", \"until the Security Council has taken measures necessary\". Invitation: government consent, not an opposition request (Nicaragua)."
terms:
  - term: Security Council authorisation (art 42)
    gloss: Force decided by the Council where measures not involving armed force (art 41) would be or have proved inadequate.
  - term: self-defence (art 51)
    gloss: The inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs, until the Security Council has taken the measures necessary.
  - term: intervention by invitation
    gloss: Force used in a state at its government's request; the consent means art 2(4) is not breached.
  - term: status quo ante bellum
    gloss: The position before the attack. Self-defence aims to fend off current or further attacks and restore it.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Intervention by invitation is a third exception to art 2(4).
    tempting: It is another way force becomes lawful.
    correction: Consent means there is no violation of art 2(4) at all, so nothing needs excusing. The Charter's exceptions are only arts 42 and 51.
  - claim: An opposition group's request can justify intervention just as a government's can.
    tempting: The opposition may speak for the people.
    correction: "Nicaragua: non-intervention would lose its effectiveness if intervention could be justified \"by a mere request for assistance made by an opposition group\"."
  - claim: Self-defence lasts as long as the victim state chooses.
    tempting: Art 51 calls it an "inherent" right.
    correction: Only "until the Security Council has taken measures necessary to maintain international peace and security"; the Council's authority to act is unaffected.

  - ask: With no invitation, what are the Charter's two exceptions?
    hint: Arts 42 and 51.
    answer: Security Council authorisation of force (art 42), and individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs (art 51).
  - ask: When may the Council act under art 42?
    hint: Art 41 first.
    answer: When measures not involving armed force under art 41 would be inadequate or have proved inadequate; it may then take action by air, sea or land forces.
  - ask: What is self-defence for, and when must it stop?
    hint: Purpose; the end of art 51.
    answer: To fend off current attacks or prevent further ones and restore the status quo ante bellum — and only until the Security Council has taken the measures necessary.

what-if:
  - change: The inviting government has lost control of much of its territory to rebels.
    effect: Cold War UK guidelines doubted its consent; but today states commonly intervene at an existing government's request regardless (e.g. the US in Iraq against ISIS, France in Mali).
  - change: The Security Council has already taken the measures necessary to restore peace.
    effect: The right of self-defence lasts only until then; continuing unilateral force would need another basis.
  - change: A coalition uses force without Council authorisation and claims no armed attack, as NATO did in Kosovo (1999).
    effect: Neither exception fits. The UK relied instead on overwhelming humanitarian necessity — an argument with no Charter basis.
