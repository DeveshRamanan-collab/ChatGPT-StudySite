plain: The law asks for reasonable care, not perfect safety. Conduct is compared with what a reasonable person would have done, not with the defendant's own best effort.
concrete: "In Romeo, a young woman fell from an unfenced cliff in a reserve managed by the Conservation Commission and was seriously injured. A fence would have prevented this fall, but the claim effectively demanded fencing a long natural coastline. Kirby J found no breach: the perceived magnitude of risk, the remote possibility of an accident, the expense, difficulty and inconvenience of fencing, and the Commission's other priorities."
formal: "Conduct is measured against a standard of care, on the balance of probabilities; falling far short or just short is equally a breach. The duty is to take reasonable care, not to avoid all injury: reasonableness, not elimination of risk (Swain). Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co (1856), Alderson B: negligence is omitting what a reasonable man would do, or doing what a prudent and reasonable man would not."
terms:
  - term: Objective standard
    gloss: Conduct is judged against a reasonable person's response to a foreseeable risk, not the defendant's own beliefs or personal best.
  - term: No degrees of carelessness
    gloss: Marginally careless or completely reckless conduct is equally a breach; breach is not graded.
  - term: Balance of probabilities
    gloss: The civil standard of proof on which breach must be established.
misconceptions:
  - claim: A defendant who genuinely did their personal best cannot be in breach.
    tempting: It seems unfair to blame someone who tried hard.
    correction: "The standard is objective: a defendant may be in breach despite doing their personal best, if that was not reasonable."
  - claim: If some risk remained after the defendant's precautions, the duty was breached.
    tempting: The plaintiff was still injured.
    correction: "The duty is to take reasonable care, not to eliminate risk (Swain). Gummow J in Dederer: reasonable care contrasts with an obligation to prevent harm."
  - claim: Conduct only just below the standard is a lesser breach, or no breach.
    tempting: "\"Only just\" sounds trivial."
    correction: "There are no degrees of carelessness: falling just short is as much a breach as falling far short."
socratic:
  - ask: Romeo fell from an unfenced cliff. A fence would have stopped her. Is that enough for breach?
    hint: Prevent harm, or take reasonable care?
    answer: No. The duty is reasonable care, not elimination of risk. The question is whether a reasonable authority would have fenced, not whether a fence would have helped.
  - ask: How did Alderson B define negligence in Blyth?
    hint: "Two limbs: doing and not doing."
    answer: Omitting what a reasonable man would do, or doing what a prudent and reasonable man would not do; negligence can lie in an act or an omission.
  - ask: If Blyth defined negligence in 1856, what did Donoghue v Stevenson add in 1932?
    hint: Think about causes of action.
    answer: It established negligence as a separate, standalone cause of action.
  - ask: A first-time organiser did her best but fell short of a reasonable organiser. In breach?
    hint: Objective or subjective?
    answer: "Yes, she may be: the test is objective, so personal best is no answer if it was not reasonable."
