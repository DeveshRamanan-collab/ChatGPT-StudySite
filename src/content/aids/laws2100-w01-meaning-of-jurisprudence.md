plain: "'Jurisprudence' has several meanings; in this unit it means legal theory, a general theory of law. General jurisprudence asks puzzles every legal system raises; particular jurisprudence asks puzzles only some raise."
concrete: Take two questions. 'Is there a general obligation to obey the law?' arises wherever there is law, so it belongs to general jurisprudence. 'In what sense is Parliament sovereign?' only makes sense in a system with a doctrine of parliamentary sovereignty. Where the constitutional architecture is different, the question does not really make sense, so it belongs to particular jurisprudence.
formal: "Iurisprudentia = iuris (of right, of law) + prudentia (wisdom, foresight, knowledge). The word can mean a body of judicial decisions, jurisprudence in relation to religious law, or medical jurisprudence; here it is a general theory of law. Bix uses 'jurisprudence', 'legal theory' and 'legal philosophy' interchangeably (Preface, p vii). General jurisprudence: puzzles thrown up by all legal systems. Particular jurisprudence: puzzles in some systems but not necessarily all."
terms:
  - term: General jurisprudence
    gloss: Philosophical puzzles thrown up by all legal systems. Its object is law in general, not the law of one jurisdiction.
    example: What is law? What is legal reasoning? Is there a general obligation to obey the law?
  - term: Particular jurisprudence
    gloss: Puzzles that emerge in some legal systems or traditions but not necessarily all.
    example: What is common about the common law? In what sense is Parliament sovereign?
  - term: Iurisprudentia
    gloss: "The Latin root: iuris (of right, of law) plus prudentia (wisdom, foresight, knowledge)."
