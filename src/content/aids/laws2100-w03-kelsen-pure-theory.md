plain: For Kelsen, law is a coercive order of norms. A norm is valid, which means binding, because a higher norm authorised it, all the way back to a basic norm that must be presupposed.
concrete: "Why is a council regulation valid? Because a higher norm authorised the council to make it. Why is that norm valid? Because a still higher norm authorised its maker, and so on up to the constitution, then back in time through earlier constitutions to the historically first one. Why obey that? Here we must presuppose a norm: you ought to obey the historically first constitution."
formal: Law is 'a definite type of norm'; 'the law is a coercive order' (p 1); it 'regulates its own creation, application and execution' (p 5). The tax officer's command is 'authorized by an objectively valid legal norm', the gangster's is not (p 6). Validity is binding force, not a fact; only a competent authority creates a valid norm; the chain ends in a presupposed basic norm.

terms:

    gloss: "The presupposed norm 'you ought to obey the historically first constitution': a transcendental-logical presupposition, not part of the legal order."
  - term: Chain of validity
    gloss: Each norm is valid because a higher norm authorised its creation, up to the constitution and back to the historically first constitution.
  - term: Stufenbau
    gloss: The Vienna school's term for the hierarchical structure of a legal order, built on authorisation.
  - term: Pure theory
    gloss: "A theory of positive law that 'answers the question of what the law is, not what it ought to be': the latter is politics; the pure theory is science (1941)."

objections:
  - position: Coercion marks law off from other normative orders, and validity comes from authorisation (Kelsen).
    evidence: The tax officer and the gangster share a subjective meaning; only the tax officer's command is authorised by an objectively valid norm (p 6); law regulates its own creation (p 5).
    objection: The chain ends in a presupposed basic norm, a 'juristic hypothesis' or 'fiction'. Hart says the ultimate rule is a social fact, neither valid nor invalid, shown in official practice.
  - position: "A theory of law can be pure: about what the law is, not what it ought to be (Kelsen, 1941)."
    evidence: From a comparison of all the phenomena called law, it seeks law's structure and typical forms, independent of their changing content (1941, 44).
    objection: For some legal philosophers the sharp is/ought distinction is intellectually misleading and/or practically dangerous, for example faced with morally evil laws such as the Nazi regime's (slide 29).
