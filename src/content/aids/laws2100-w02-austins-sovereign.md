plain: For Austin, positive laws are a sovereign's commands. The sovereign is a determinate superior whom the bulk of a society habitually obeys, and who habitually obeys no one else.
concrete: "Picture a small community that habitually obeys a council of elders, which obeys no one else. No territory and no modern state institutions are needed: for Austin that is enough for an independent political society, and the council's general commands are its positive laws. The same test fits a city state, an empire, an absolute monarchy or a constitutional democracy: find the highest political power."
formal: "'Every positive law is set by a sovereign person, or a sovereign body of persons, to a member or members of the independent political society wherein that person or body is sovereign or supreme' (PJD 165). A society is political iff the generality of members habitually obey a determinate and common superior; independent iff that superior does not habitually obey another (PJD 169)."
terms:
  - term: Sovereign
    gloss: A determinate person or body of persons whom the bulk of a society habitually obeys, and who does not habitually obey anyone else.
  - term: Independent political society
    gloss: A society whose members generally obey a determinate and common superior, who in turn habitually obeys no other person or body.
  - term: Habit of obedience
    gloss: "The fact that the bulk of a society regularly obeys one and the same superior: the pillar on which Austin's sovereignty rests."
  - term: Austin's legal positivism
    gloss: "'The existence of law is one thing; its merit or demerit is another' (Lecture V): law as it is, kept apart from law as it ought to be."

objections:
  - position: "Austin offers a neat, general explanation of law: identify the highest power and its general commands."
    evidence: It fits a city state, an absolute monarchy or a constitutional democracy; it needs no territory, only habitual obedience to a determinate superior (PJD 165–169; lecture).
    objection: "Hart: 'the whole conception of a supreme and independent sovereign habitually obeyed ... is misleading, since there is little in any actual legal system which corresponds to it' (CL 27)."
  - position: A theory of law can be general, with principles 'abstracted from every particular system' (Austin, Lecture XI).
    evidence: Austin illustrates his principles from Roman law and the law of England, the two systems he had studied with some accuracy.
    objection: Some argue we are historically situated and can say something meaningful only about our own legal system, perhaps compared with others; the essence of law cannot be captured (lecture).
    reply: "Austin, Kelsen and Hart argue it is possible, necessary and useful to work at that level, building from knowledge of positive law: theory does not 'come from the sky'."
