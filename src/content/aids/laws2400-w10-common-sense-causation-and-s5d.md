plain: Courts once settled hard causation cases by 'common sense'. The CLA now separates the factual question (did the negligence cause the harm?) from the policy question (should the defendant answer for it?).
concrete: Early one morning, the defendant's truck was parked in the middle of a six-lane road to unload fruit and vegetables, as had long been the practice. The plaintiff, drunk and speeding, drove into it and was injured. The trial judge held the defendant liable, with 70% contributory negligence; the Full Court of South Australia put all responsibility on the plaintiff. The High Court held the defendant's negligence was a cause, though the judges reasoned differently.
formal: "March v Stramare: Mason CJ — 'but for' was never the exclusive test; causation 'must be determined by applying common sense to the facts of each particular case'. McHugh J — 'but for' should be exclusive, with a 'scope of the risk' test bringing policy in as part of remoteness. Adeels Palace: s 5D(1) 'treats factual causation and scope of liability as separate and distinct issues'."

    gloss: "Mason CJ's approach in March v Stramare: causation is a question of fact determined by applying common sense to the facts of each case."
  - term: "'Scope of the risk' test"
    gloss: "McHugh J's proposal in March: keep 'but for' as the exclusive factual test and deal with policy factors as part of remoteness."

    gloss: The Negligence Review Panel's report behind the CLA. It criticised 'common sense' causation for hiding policy choices inside a factual question.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Section 5D(1) codified Mason CJ's common sense test.
    tempting: March v Stramare is the leading common law case, and the CLA codified the common law.
    correction: The Ipp Report criticised 'common sense' for hiding policy choices. Section 5D(1) treats factual causation and scope of liability as separate and distinct issues (Adeels Palace).
  - claim: In March v Stramare, McHugh J held that causation is a matter of common sense.
    tempting: March is famous for common sense, and McHugh J sat in the case.
    correction: That was Mason CJ. McHugh J said 'but for' should be the exclusive test, with a 'scope of the risk' test bringing policy in as part of remoteness.
  - claim: If security might have prevented the harm, factual causation is established.
    tempting: The occupier had no security at all, so excusing it seems unfair.
    correction: "Adeels Palace: that changing any of the circumstances 'might have made a difference does not prove factual causation'."
socratic:
  - ask: In March v Stramare, would the plaintiff have been injured without the truck parked mid-road?
    hint: Was the truck needed for this collision?
    answer: "No: without the truck there was nothing to hit. The truck and the plaintiff's own drunk, speeding driving were both necessary conditions of the injury."
  - ask: Why did Mason CJ say 'but for' could not be the only test?
    hint: Two events, or an intervening one.
    answer: It does not work where two or more acts or events are involved, or where an intervening event breaks the chain. Causation must be determined by common sense.
  - ask: What did the Ipp Report find wrong with 'common sense'?
    hint: What hides beneath a simple factual question?
    answer: It hid value judgments about who should be responsible and how far liability should extend, making the law less transparent and less predictable.
  - ask: How does s 5D(1) deal with that?
    hint: Count its paragraphs.
    answer: It splits causation into (a) factual causation, a necessary condition, and (b) scope of liability, so policy is dealt with openly at (b).
  - ask: Why did Moubarak lose in Adeels Palace?
    hint: What did the evidence show about security staff?
    answer: It did not show security staff would have deterred or prevented the gunman's re-entry. That a change 'might have made a difference' does not prove factual causation.

    answer: Under s 5D(1) the court does not pick 'the' cause by common sense.
