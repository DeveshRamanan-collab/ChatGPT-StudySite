plain: Trespass to goods is directly interfering with a chattel that someone possesses at the time. What counts is possession, not ownership, and no damage need be proved.
concrete: "Penfolds sold wine in bottles embossed \"This bottle is the property of Penfolds Wines Ltd\". Elliott, who sold bulk wine to customers bringing their own bottles, filled Penfolds bottles that his brother had left with him. Penfolds sued in trespass. The High Court held there was no trespass to goods: Penfolds did not possess the bottles when Elliott used them — Elliott did (Penfolds Wines v Elliott (1946) 74 CLR 204)."
formal: "Trespass to chattels requires a direct interference with chattels in the possession of the plaintiff at the time of the interference; being trespass, no proof of actual damage is generally required. Possession differs from ownership and has two elements: intention to exercise control and exclude others, and actual control. A bailee's possession bars the bailor's trespass claim, save exceptions such as destruction (Penfolds Wines v Elliott)."

terms:
  - term: Chattel
    gloss: Goods that are tangible, movable, visible, physically possessable and transferable by delivery — pets, cars, phones, artwork — but not fixtures attached to land.
  - term: Possession
    gloss: Intention to exercise control and exclude others, plus actual control. Different from ownership, and essential to trespass to goods.
  - term: Bailment
    gloss: Goods held by a bailee for a bailor. The bailee has possession, so the bailor generally cannot sue in trespass (Dixon J, Penfolds).
  - term: Finder
    gloss: A finder or occupier can have possession in law of chattels on the premises, good against everyone except the true owner (NCA v Flack).

socratic:
  - ask: In Penfolds, who owned the bottles?
    hint: Read the embossing.
    answer: Penfolds. The bottles said so, and customers bought only the wine.
  - ask: Who had the bottles when Elliott filled them?
    hint: "Follow the chain: Penfolds, the brother, Elliott."
    answer: Elliott, who was left them by his brother. Dixon J treated Penfolds as bailor and the brother as bailee; bailment can continue in chains.
  - ask: So why did the trespass claim fail?
    hint: What does trespass to goods protect?
    answer: Possession. Penfolds was not in possession at the time of the interference, so it had no title to sue in trespass.
  - ask: Can a bailor ever sue a bailee in trespass?
    hint: Dixon J's exception.
    answer: Yes, for example where the bailee completely destroys the goods.
  - ask: If trespass fails for lack of possession, is the owner left without a remedy?
    hint: The next tort on the slides.
    answer: No. Conversion can be brought on a right to immediate possession, and the majority in Penfolds found conversion.
elements:
  - name: Chattel
    test: Tangible, movable, visible goods, physically possessable and transferable by delivery; not fixtures permanently attached to land.
  - name: Plaintiff's possession at the time
    test: Intention to control and exclude others, plus actual control, at the moment of interference. A bailee has it (Penfolds); so may a finder or occupier (NCA v Flack).
    cases:
      - penfolds-wines-pty-ltd-v-elliott

  - name: Direct interference
    test: A direct interference with the chattel. No proof of actual damage is generally required.
  - name: Defences
    test: The usual defences, such as consent and lawful authority, apply (Topic 4).
