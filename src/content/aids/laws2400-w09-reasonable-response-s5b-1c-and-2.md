plain: Once a risk matters, ask what a reasonable person would have done about it, weighing how likely and how serious the harm was against the cost and value of preventing it.
concrete: "Paris worked in a garage and had only one good eye. His employer knew this but did not give him safety goggles, which was not ordinary industry practice at the time. A piece of metal struck his good eye. The House of Lords held the employer in breach: the known chance that a particular worker would suffer a graver injury than his fellows had to be considered in setting the employer's obligation."
formal: "Section 5B(2): deciding whether a reasonable person would have taken precautions, the court considers, amongst other relevant things, (a) the probability of harm if care were not taken, (b) its likely seriousness, (c) the burden of precautions, and (d) the social utility of the activity. Section 5C: the burden includes similar risks; avoidability by other means, or later action, does not of itself affect liability."
terms:
  - term: Calculus of negligence
    gloss: The weighing of probability, seriousness, burden and social utility to decide what a reasonable person would have done; not a checklist or algorithm.
  - term: Burden of taking precautions
    gloss: "Not only cost: also practical matters such as the availability of suitable equipment, convenience and safety (Woods)."
  - term: Social utility
    gloss: The public value of the activity creating the risk, such as an emergency vehicle's run or the blood supply (E v Red Cross).
  - term: Inherent risk
    gloss: A risk that cannot be avoided by reasonable care and skill; no liability for its materialisation (s 5I), though a duty to warn remains.
misconceptions:
  - claim: A low probability of harm settles s 5B(1)(c) for the defendant.
    tempting: In Dederer, low probability helped the RTA.
    correction: Probability is only s 5B(2)(a). The court weighs seriousness, burden, social utility and other relevant things together; no single factor decides it.
  - claim: Taking a precaution after the accident admits the defendant was negligent.
    tempting: Fixing the hazard looks like conceding it was a hazard.
    correction: "Section 5C(c): later action that would have avoided the risk does not of itself give rise to or affect liability, and is not of itself an admission."
  - claim: The burden of precautions is just their financial cost.
    tempting: "\"Burden\" sounds like money."
    correction: "Woods v Multi-Sport Holdings: it extends to practical considerations such as the availability of suitable equipment, convenience and safety."
socratic:
  - ask: In Dederer the harm was grave. Why was there no breach?
    hint: Judge probability prospectively.
    answer: "Judged at the time, the probability was low: years of jumping, no injuries. Given the expense and difficulty of the plaintiff's measures, the existing signs were a reasonable response."
  - ask: Why was Paris's employer in breach when goggles were not usual practice?
    hint: What did the employer know about Paris?
    answer: It knew he had only one good eye, so an injury would be graver for him. Seriousness raises the standard, especially with known vulnerability.
  - ask: Woods lost an eye at indoor cricket. Why was no helmet required?
    hint: Is cost the only burden?
    answer: No suitable helmet existed for indoor cricket, none were worn or required by the rules, and they raised their own convenience and safety concerns; serious eye injuries were rare.
  - ask: Why was the Red Cross not in breach for not testing earlier?
    hint: What would testing have cost the community?
    answer: "Testing would likely have lost about 5% of all blood donations, and might not have reduced the risk: the social utility of the blood supply weighed against it."
  - ask: How do you put the factors together?
    hint: Kirby J in Romeo.
    answer: They are a framework, weighed as a whole on the facts, to decide which risks to guard against and which can safely be ignored.

what-if:
  - change: Paris's employer did not know he had only one good eye.
    effect: The known vulnerability that raised the standard disappears. The question becomes whether goggles were needed for workers generally, which was not ordinary practice then.
  - change: In Woods, purpose-made indoor cricket helmets had been cheap, available and worn elsewhere.
    effect: The burden of the precaution falls sharply, so a reasonable provider might be expected to supply them, and breach becomes more arguable.
  - change: A patron injured at a venue was heavily intoxicated.
    effect: "Section 49(1)(c): intoxication does not of itself increase or otherwise affect the standard of care owed to him."
