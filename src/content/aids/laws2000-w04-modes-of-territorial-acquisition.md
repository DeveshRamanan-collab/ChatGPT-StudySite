plain: "A state can gain sovereign title to land in five ways: transfer from another state, new land formed by nature, occupying land no state ever held, uncontested long possession — and, no longer, conquest."

formal: "Five modes: cession (the acquirer gets no more rights than the ceding state had); accretion (an island is \"a naturally formed area of land, surrounded by water, which is above water at high tide\": UNCLOS art 121(1); artificial islands are not islands: art 60(8)); occupation of terra nullius (effective control plus intention); prescription (peaceful control, implied consent; sustained protest prevents it); conquest — prohibited since 1945 (UN Charter art 2(4))."

terms:
  - term: title
    gloss: The source of a state's right to territory (and sometimes the proof of it). The five modes are sources of title.
  - term: terra nullius
    gloss: Land never under any state's sovereignty, acquired by effective control plus an intention to claim title. Barely applicable now, but central to colonial expansion.
  - term: low-tide elevation
    gloss: A naturally formed area of land submerged at high tide. Not an island under UNCLOS art 121(1), so it cannot ground a sovereignty claim.
  - term: belligerent occupation
    gloss: "The regime triggered when one state's military occupies another's territory: temporary, with no transfer of sovereignty however effective the control."
misconceptions:
  - claim: A state that conquers territory acquires title once its control is effective and stable.
    tempting: Effective control is what matters for terra nullius, and conquest once gave title.
    correction: Since the UN Charter (art 2(4)) territory cannot be acquired by force. Occupation triggers the belligerent occupation regime — temporary, with no transfer of sovereignty (Kuwait 1990; Crimea 2014).
  - claim: Long, peaceful administration of another state's land gives title by prescription, whatever the owner says.
    tempting: Peaceful control acting as sovereign is a requirement of prescription.
    correction: "Prescription rests on implied consent, so sustained protests and objections by the 'old' state prevent it. Land never under any sovereignty is a different mode: terra nullius."
  - claim: Building an artificial island, or building up a reef, creates new territory with its own territorial sea.
    tempting: New land is added, and accretion recognises new land.
    correction: Accretion must be natural. Artificial islands "do not possess the status of islands" and have no territorial sea (UNCLOS art 60(8)); low-tide elevations are not islands (art 121(1); South China Sea arbitration).

elements:
  - name: Cession
    test: Transfer by agreement, often a purchase treaty (Alaska 1867). The acquirer gets no more rights than the ceding state had and must respect third states' and indigenous rights.
  - name: Accretion
    test: New land formed gradually by nature (Japan's 1986 volcanic island). An island must be naturally formed and above water at high tide; artificial islands and low-tide elevations do not count.

    test: Land never under any state's sovereignty, plus effective control and an intention to obtain title.
  - name: Prescription

  - name: Conquest (prohibited)
