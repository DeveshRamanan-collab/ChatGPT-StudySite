plain: A good first interview lets the client tell their story before the lawyer narrows in, and avoids re-traumatising them. Skipping the interview can cost the client their claim.
concrete: The client's banana chair broke and she was injured; Depo Medrol injections then made things worse. She joined a class action against Depo Medrol and filled in the firm's questionnaire, but was never interviewed or asked about the original injury. When she found out she should have sued the chair's manufacturer, that claim was time-barred. In Roberts v Cashman the NSW Supreme Court held the firm's duty of care was breached.
formal: "Roberts v Cashman [2000] NSWSC 770 [64]: there is 'a grave danger' that a solicitor breaches the implied duty in the retainer by not interviewing the client in litigation matters. A questionnaire 'is not of itself a sufficient discharge'; a solicitor who does not interview 'does so at his own peril'. ASCR r 7.1: give clear and timely advice so the client can make informed choices."
terms:
  - term: Client-focused interviewing
    gloss: The lawyer controls the process, not the content. The client is an actor and agent who makes their own decisions (ASCR rr 7 and 8).
  - term: LARS
    gloss: "Active listening: Listening, Acknowledging (door openers, minimal encouragers), Reflecting (and reframing), Summarising facts and concerns."
  - term: T-funnel questioning
    gloss: Broad open questions, then general open, then detailed, then nitty-gritty questions, with clarification throughout.
  - term: Trauma-informed practice
    gloss: Avoiding re-traumatisation, not solving trauma. Ask 'What happened to you?' rather than 'What is wrong with you?'.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Clients mainly want legal advice, so share your view as soon as the problem looks clear.
    tempting: Advice is what the client is paying for.
    correction: Often what clients really need is to be heard; lawyers who jump in before understanding leave clients feeling unheard. Hear the client before trying to solve the problem.
  - claim: Open with detailed, pointed questions to identify the legal issue quickly.
    tempting: Boxing the problem early seems efficient.
    correction: Listen to the narrative first, then start broad and narrow down. Don't start questioning too soon, don't do too much of it, and don't assign legal boxes too early.
  - claim: Being trauma-informed means helping the client work through their trauma.
    tempting: A caring lawyer wants to help.
    correction: Trauma-informed practice is about avoiding re-traumatisation, not solving trauma. Your job is to know where the client can find appropriate professional help.

    answer: Broad open questions first, then general open, detailed and nitty-gritty questions, clarifying along the way. No leading, compound or double-negative questions.
