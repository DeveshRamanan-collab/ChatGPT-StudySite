plain: "Legal positivists separate law as it is from law as it ought to be: whether something is law depends on social facts, not on its moral merits, even though law and morality often overlap."
concrete: A parliament passes a plainly unjust statute, and nothing in the constitution makes justice a test of validity. On Bentham and Austin's 'two simple things', its injustice alone does not show it is not law. Equally, a rule that would be morally excellent is not law just because it is desirable. Whether each is law depends on the system's own criteria.
formal: "Green and Adams: positivism is 'the thesis that the existence and content of law depends on social facts and not on its merits'. Austin: 'The existence of law is one thing; its merit and demerit another' (PJD 157). Hart: 'it is in no sense a necessary truth that laws reproduce or satisfy certain demands of morality, though in fact they have often done so' (CL 185–186)."

socratic:
  - ask: A statute is grossly unjust. Does that alone show it is not law?
    hint: The first of the 'two simple things'.
    answer: No. Without an express constitutional or legal provision, the mere fact that a rule violates morality does not show it is not a rule of law.
  - ask: A rule would be morally excellent. Is it therefore law?
    hint: The second simple thing.
    answer: No. It does not follow from the mere fact that a rule is morally desirable that it is a rule of law.
  - ask: Do positivists deny that laws often match morality?
    hint: Bentham and Austin, as Hart reads them.
    answer: No. Many legal rules mirror moral rules as a matter of historical fact; the claim is only that this is not a necessary truth.
  - ask: What if the constitution itself says no law may be grossly unjust?
    hint: Bentham's concession.
    answer: Then morality enters through an explicit legal provision, which Bentham never denied. The statute fails because of that provision, not because immorality as such defeats law.
  - ask: Why do some find the sharp is/ought separation worrying?
    hint: Hart names three lines of criticism.
    answer: "As intellectually misleading or practically dangerous: on law and morality, on formalism in adjudication, and on morally evil laws such as those of the Nazi regime."
