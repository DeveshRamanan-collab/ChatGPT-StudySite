plain: Tort deals with private civil wrongs and compensation; criminal law with public wrongs and punishment; contract with duties the parties agreed. The same conduct can fall into more than one.
concrete: Assault is the lecture's example of one wrong that is both a tort and a crime. If A punches B, the State may prosecute A for the offence, aiming at punishment and needing proof beyond reasonable doubt. B can also sue A in tort herself, seeking compensation and proving her case on the balance of probabilities.
formal: "Tort v crime: private v public wrong; compensation v punishment; redress/correction v retribution/deterrence; started by the individual v the State; balance of probabilities v beyond reasonable doubt. Tort v contract: duties from law v from parties and law; general (neighbour principle) v specific parties; motive relevant in some torts v never; unliquidated v liquidated damages; privity irrelevant v generally required."
terms:
  - term: Balance of probabilities
    gloss: The standard of proof in a tort action, lower than the criminal standard of proof beyond reasonable doubt.
  - term: Unliquidated damages
    gloss: Damages that are not a fixed sum; the lecture's table marks tort damages unliquidated and contract damages liquidated.
  - term: Privity
    gloss: Being a party to the contract. Generally required to sue in contract; not relevant in tort, where duties come from the general law.
  - term: Neighbour principle
    gloss: "The lecture's label for the general scope of tort duties: owed broadly to others, not only to specific contracting parties."
misconceptions:

    tempting: "The comparison table sets them up as opposites: private v public, compensation v punishment."
    correction: "The distinction is not always sharp: a wrong such as assault may be both a tort and a crime, and compensation may be obtained for injuries from crimes (Lecture 1, slide 18)."
  - claim: A tort claim must be proved beyond reasonable doubt.
    tempting: It is the best-known standard of proof, and the same conduct may also be a crime.
    correction: Tort uses the balance of probabilities; beyond reasonable doubt is the criminal standard (Lecture 1, slide 19).
  - claim: You can only sue in tort if you had a contract with the defendant.
    tempting: Both branches deal with civil wrongs, and many disputes, such as product liability, involve a contract.
    correction: "Privity is not relevant in tort: tort duties arise from the law and are general (neighbour principle); privity is generally required only in contract (Lecture 1, slide 21)."
