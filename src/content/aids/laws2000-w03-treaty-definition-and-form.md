plain: A treaty is a written agreement between states that is meant to be governed by international law — whatever it is called and however informal it looks.
concrete: "The lecturer's example: Australia's Prime Minister meets the UK's Prime Minister over coffee. They agree Australia will import certain UK cars at a 2% tariff, then leave. Most of the class said this was not a treaty, and they were right — at least under the VCLT, which covers only agreements in written form. Had the deal been recorded in minutes of the meeting, the answer could change (Qatar v Bahrain)."
formal: "VCLT art 2(1)(a): \"treaty\" means \"an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or in two or more related instruments and whatever its particular designation\". The VCLT applies only to treaties between states (art 1); treaties involving international organisations fall under the 1986 Vienna Convention."
terms:
  - term: Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT)
    gloss: The 'treaty on treaties' (adopted 1969, in force 1980). Many provisions reflect customary international law, so they bind non-parties too.
  - term: memorandum of understanding (MoU)
    gloss: Can be a treaty under international law (Somalia v Kenya), but DFAT practice treats Australian MoUs as having 'less than treaty' status.
  - term: governed by international law
    gloss: Disputes about the agreement are resolved under international law, not a party's domestic law; an agreement choosing Australian law is not a treaty.
  - term: related instruments
    gloss: A treaty can be one document or several — for example a treaty plus a protocol, which itself has treaty status.
misconceptions:
  - claim: A document must be called a 'treaty' or 'convention' to be a treaty.
    tempting: The word 'treaty' appears in the definition itself.
    correction: "Art 2(1)(a) applies \"whatever its particular designation\": a press communiqué (Aegean Sea), meeting minutes (Qatar v Bahrain) and an MoU (Somalia v Kenya) were all capable of being treaties."
  - claim: Because DFAT treats MoUs as less than treaties, an MoU can never be a treaty at international law.
    tempting: The label matters in Australian practice, and DFAT says so publicly.
    correction: That is Australia's domestic practice. In Somalia v Kenya a signed, dated MoU registered with the UN was held to be a treaty under international law.
  - claim: Only states that ratified the VCLT are bound by its rules.
    tempting: The VCLT is itself a treaty, with about 116 parties.
    correction: Many VCLT provisions reflect customary international law, binding non-parties too; commentary and ICJ case law show whether a given article is customary.
socratic:
  - ask: Two prime ministers agree a 2% car tariff over coffee and leave. Is there a VCLT treaty?
    hint: "Run through art 2(1)(a): which element is missing?"
    answer: No — the agreement is not in written form, so it falls outside the VCLT definition.
  - ask: Suppose the deal is recorded in the minutes of their meeting. Does the informal format matter?
    hint: Think of Qatar v Bahrain.
    answer: No. Minutes recording commitments the parties consented to were held to be a treaty (Qatar v Bahrain); format and label do not matter.
  - ask: What if the minutes say disputes will be resolved under Australian law?
    hint: The third element.
    answer: Then it is not governed by international law, so it is not a treaty — even though it is between states.
  - ask: What if one party is an international organisation?
    hint: Art 1 VCLT.
    answer: The 1969 VCLT does not apply; the 1986 Vienna Convention covers treaties involving international organisations.
  - ask: Once art 2(1)(a) is met, is the state bound?
    hint: "What comes next: authority, then consent."
    answer: Not necessarily. A document meeting the definition may still need proper authority and consent to be bound, by signature or ratification.

what-if:
  - change: The coffee-table deal is written up and signed by both prime ministers.
    effect: It is now in written form between states; if intended to be governed by international law it meets art 2(1)(a). Prime ministers need no full powers (art 7), but consent to be bound still needs checking.
  - change: The signed document says disputes will be decided under English law.
    effect: It is not governed by international law, so it is an agreement between states but not a treaty under art 2(1)(a).
  - change: The document is an Australian MoU drafted under DFAT's 'less than treaty' practice.
    effect: Australia does not treat it as a treaty. But if it records binding commitments, international law may still classify it as one, as in Somalia v Kenya; Australia's label does not settle its international status.
