plain: Pure economic loss is money lost when nobody was hurt and no property was damaged first. Courts allow it only in certain situations, because of three policy worries.
concrete: "A council crew digging on a footpath negligently cuts the electricity cable a cake shop needs. The chef cannot bake and loses profits, but no one is hurt and nothing the shop owns is damaged: that is pure economic loss. Change one fact, so the surge short-circuits the fridge and the stock spoils, and there is property damage; the losses flowing from it become consequential."
formal: "Pure economic loss (PEL) is economic loss which does not result from any physical injury to the person or property. It was not recoverable until recently, and even now is recoverable only in certain situations if additional conditions are satisfied. Policy reasons for the reluctance: indeterminate liability, the relationship with the law of contract, and loss linked to a lawful commercial activity. CLA s 5 includes economic loss in \"harm\"."

terms:
  - term: Pure economic loss (PEL)
    gloss: Economic loss which does not result from any physical injury to the person or property.
    example: Profits lost when a negligently cut cable shuts a business.
  - term: Consequential economic loss
    gloss: Economic loss that follows a physical injury or property damage, such as lost wages or medical expenses; reasonable foreseeability is typically enough.
  - term: Indeterminate liability
    gloss: "Liability with no boundaries: the class of people who might sue cannot be narrowed or defined."
    example: A tip on the radio or a podcast relied on by anyone listening.
misconceptions:
  - claim: Profits lost after a council cuts a cable are consequential loss, because something physical was damaged.
    tempting: A cable was physically cut, so the loss seems to follow property damage.
    correction: The cable was not the plaintiff's property and the plaintiff was not injured, so the lost profit is pure economic loss. It becomes consequential only if the plaintiff's own property (the fridge, the stock) is damaged.
  - claim: Indeterminacy means no duty for PEL can be owed to a large class of plaintiffs.
    tempting: Large numbers sound like the floodgates the courts fear.
    correction: A large class is not necessarily a problem. The concern is a class that cannot be narrowed or defined, such as everyone who might hear a radio tip.
  - claim: Because PEL is a recognised form of harm, foreseeable PEL means a duty is owed.
    tempting: That is how the physical-injury paradigm works under Donoghue v Stevenson.
    correction: Recognition means a duty can arise, not that it always will. Reasonable foreseeability is always needed but, away from the paradigm, more is required (Esanda).
socratic:
  - ask: A car accident leaves you unable to work for 20 years. Is your lost income pure economic loss?
    hint: "What came first: an injury, or only the money loss?"
    answer: No. It follows a physical injury, so it is consequential economic loss, which courts have never had difficulty compensating.
  - ask: A council crew cuts a cable and your cake shop cannot bake. Nothing you own is damaged. What kind of loss is your lost profit?
    hint: Was your person or your property harmed?
    answer: "Pure economic loss: no physical injury and no damage to your property; only the money loss."
  - ask: Why should it matter to the court which kind of loss it is?
    hint: Think about what foreseeability does in each case.
    answer: For consequential loss, foreseeability is typically enough. PEL was not recoverable until recently and now needs additional conditions.
  - ask: A podcaster tells listeners to buy a cryptocurrency that later collapses. What worries a court about a duty here?
    hint: Who could sue?
    answer: "Indeterminate liability: there is no way to define who heard and relied on the statement."
  - ask: A new supermarket takes a rival's profits. Is a duty owed for that loss?
    hint: Is anything wrongful about competing?
    answer: No. It is loss linked to a lawful commercial activity; competition is the nature of commerce, so no duty arises.
what-if:

    effect: There is now property damage to the plaintiff. The losses that flow from it are consequential, and reasonable foreseeability is typically enough for a duty.

    why: There was property damage, but Caltex did not claim for it, so the case was dealt with as pure economic loss. How a claim is framed decides how it is treated.

transfer:

    answer: "Classify first. Café A suffered property damage, so its lost trade is consequential and reasonable foreseeability is typically enough. Café B suffered pure economic loss: no injury, no damage to its property. For Café B, foreseeability is necessary but not sufficient; the court will weigh the salient features and the policy concerns, such as whether the class of affected businesses can be defined."
