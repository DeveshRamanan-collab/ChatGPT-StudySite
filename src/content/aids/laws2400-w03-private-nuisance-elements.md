plain: Private nuisance is a substantial, unreasonable interference with someone's use and enjoyment of their land — by noise, smell, dust and the like. Only someone with a proprietary interest can sue.
concrete: "In Victoria Park Racing v Taylor (1937) 58 CLR 479 a neighbour across the road built a platform about sixteen feet high, from which a broadcaster described each race live on radio. Patrons stayed home to listen. The High Court majority held there was no nuisance: the racecourse was as usable as ever, the loss was diverted custom from lawful competition, and freedom from being overlooked is not a protected right."
formal: "Private nuisance, an action on the case, protects against tangible and intangible interferences with use and enjoyment of land. The plaintiff must have title to sue (a proprietary interest: Oldham v Lawson (No 1); Hunter v Canary Wharf); the interference must be substantial and unreasonable (an ordinary person's standard: Munro v Southern Dairies); and the defendant must have created, authorised or continued it."

terms:
  - term: Title to sue (nuisance)
    gloss: "A proprietary interest: an owner or tenant in actual possession. A licensee — a tenant's daughter, a guest, a short-term Airbnb occupant — cannot sue."
  - term: Substantial and unreasonable
    gloss: Judged objectively, by an ordinary person in the plaintiff's position, weighing frequency, duration, time, locality and distance.
  - term: Continuing a nuisance
    gloss: Not creating it, but neglecting to remedy it after becoming, or when one should have become, aware of it (Lord Wright).
  - term: Tangible and intangible interference
    gloss: "Private nuisance covers both: fire, water and dust, as well as noise and smell."
misconceptions:
  - claim: Anyone living in the house can sue for nuisance.
    tempting: Khorasandjian v Bush let a licensee daughter sue, and Deasy Investments v Monreast followed it.
    correction: "Hunter v Canary Wharf overruled Bush: title to sue requires a proprietary interest. In Oldham v Lawson (No 1) the husband, a licensee only, could not sue (Lecture 2, slides 4–5)."
  - claim: Being overlooked by a neighbour is a nuisance.
    tempting: It feels like an invasion of one's enjoyment of the land.
    correction: "Victoria Park Racing v Taylor: freedom from view is not a legally protected interest, and loss from lawful competition is not interference with enjoyment — though hostility might change things (slide 8)."
  - claim: Only the person who created the nuisance can be sued.
    tempting: The creator is the obvious wrongdoer.
    correction: An occupier who authorises it, or who continues it by neglecting to remedy it once aware of it, can also be liable (Lecture 2, slides 10–12).
socratic:
  - ask: In Victoria Park Racing, could the racecourse still be used as a racecourse?
    hint: "Latham CJ: \"as suitable as ever\"."
    answer: Yes. The defendants had not interfered with the use and enjoyment of the land itself.
  - ask: Then what was the plaintiff really complaining about?
    hint: Where did its patrons go?
    answer: "Lost profits: patrons stayed home to listen. Diverted custom, Dixon J said, can be a head of damage for a wrong, but it is not the wrong itself."
  - ask: Isn't being overlooked a wrong?
    hint: Dixon J on freedom from view.
    answer: No. Freedom from view is not a legally protected interest; the occupier's remedy was to build a higher fence.
  - ask: When was the noise, smell and flies from horses a nuisance in Munro v Southern Dairies?
    hint: Whose standard of comfort?
    answer: When, considering all the factors, it was substantial and unreasonable to an ordinary person in the plaintiff's position — not someone abnormally sensitive.
  - ask: Can firing guns on your own land be a nuisance?
    hint: Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett.
    answer: "Yes, where it is done deliberately to disturb a neighbour's foxes in the breeding season: motive or malice can make an interference unreasonable."
elements:
  - name: Title to sue
    test: A proprietary interest — owner or tenant in actual possession; a licensee cannot sue (Oldham v Lawson; Hunter v Canary Wharf, overruling Khorasandjian v Bush).
    cases:
      - oldham-v-lawson-no-1
      - hunter-v-canary-wharf
      - khorasandjian-v-bush
  - name: Substantial and unreasonable interference
    test: Weigh frequency, duration, time, locality and distance against an ordinary person's standard (Munro). Malice may tip the balance; being overlooked or lawful competition is not enough (Victoria Park).
    cases:
      - munro-v-southern-dairies
      - hollywood-silver-fox-farm-v-emmett
      - victoria-park-racing-and-recreation-grounds-co-v-taylor
  - name: Created, authorised or continued
    test: The creator (strictly liable, even if not the occupier); an occupier who authorises the activity; or one who continues it by neglecting to remedy it once aware.
what-if:
  - change: In Oldham v Lawson, the husband had also held a legal interest in the house.
    effect: He would then have a proprietary interest, and so title to sue, like his wife.
  - change: The Victoria Park broadcaster's overlooking had been hostile, akin to harassment.
    effect: "The lecture leaves this open: motive or malice can make an interference unreasonable (Hollywood Silver Fox Farm), so hostile overlooking might be treated differently."
  - change: A new occupier learns of a nuisance created by a previous occupier and does nothing.
    effect: "The new occupier may be liable for continuing it: neglecting to remedy a nuisance once aware of it is continuing it (Lord Wright)."
boundary:
  - text: Khorasandjian v Bush and Deasy Investments v Monreast.
    why: Both let licensees sue, but Hunter v Canary Wharf (UK, 1997) overruled Bush and restored the proprietary-interest requirement.
  - text: Rich and Evatt JJ's dissent in Victoria Park Racing.
    why: They thought the categories of nuisance are not closed and that systematic overlooking for profit could be actionable; the majority disagreed.
transfer:
  - scenario: A tenant's neighbour installs a commercial-grade air conditioner that runs loudly all night beside the tenant's bedroom window. The tenant's adult son, staying with her for a month, also wants to sue.
    answer: The tenant has title to sue as a tenant in possession; her son, a licensee, does not (Oldham; Hunter v Canary Wharf). Loud, all-night noise beside a bedroom is likely substantial and unreasonable to an ordinary person, given time, duration and distance (Munro). The neighbour created the state of affairs, so is the right defendant.
