plain: The ICJ decides disputes between states, binding only those states, and gives advisory opinions to UN bodies, which bind no one but carry great weight. Judgments are final, without appeal.
concrete: "The UN General Assembly asked the ICJ two framed questions: was Mauritius's decolonisation lawfully completed at independence, and what follows from the UK's continued administration of the Chagos Archipelago? The Court found decolonisation had not been lawfully completed and that the UK had to end its administration. As an advisory opinion it formally bound no one — yet, coming from the UN's principal judicial organ, it carried considerable weight in a sensitive political dispute."
formal: "The ICJ, the UN's principal judicial organ, has 15 judges elected by the Security Council and General Assembly for nine years. Contentious cases: only states are parties (art 34); judgments bind the parties only (art 59) and are \"final and without appeal\" (art 60). Advisory opinions: requested by UN organs and specialised agencies (art 96 UN Charter); non-binding. Art 94 UN Charter: comply, or the Security Council may act."
terms:
  - term: contentious case
    gloss: A legal dispute between states submitted by them; the judgment binds the parties only.
  - term: advisory opinion
    gloss: The Court's answer to a legal question from a UN organ or authorised specialised agency (art 96 UN Charter); non-binding but weighty.
  - term: ad hoc judge
    gloss: A judge a disputing party may in principle appoint to sit alongside the 15 regular judges.
  - term: art 94 UN Charter
    gloss: Members must comply with ICJ decisions; if a party does not, the other may go to the Security Council, which may recommend or decide on measures.
misconceptions:
  - claim: An ICJ judgment binds every UN member, because the Court is the UN's principal judicial organ.
    tempting: The Court is so authoritative.
    correction: A judgment binds only the parties and cannot oblige third parties (arts 59, 60). A judgment between Australia and New Zealand does not bind Indonesia.
  - claim: Advisory opinions bind the states they concern.
    tempting: They come from the same Court as judgments.
    correction: Advisory opinions are non-binding — though, as with Chagos, they carry considerable weight.
  - claim: ICJ decisions bind later cases, as precedent does in the common law.
    tempting: Its "case law" is cited constantly.
    correction: The doctrine of precedent does not exist in international law. Judges and scholars cite ICJ case law as if it were common law, but a decision binds only its parties.
socratic:
  - ask: Who requested the Chagos opinion, and could Mauritius have requested it instead?
    hint: Art 96 UN Charter.
    answer: The General Assembly. Advisory opinions are requested by UN organs and authorised specialised agencies; a state brings contentious cases, not advisory requests.
  - ask: Did the Chagos opinion bind the UK?
    hint: Advisory.
    answer: No — advisory opinions are non-binding. But they carry considerable weight, coming from the UN's principal judicial organ.
  - ask: In a contentious case State X loses. Can it appeal?
    hint: Art 60.
    answer: No. The judgment is final and without appeal; if its meaning or scope is disputed, the Court construes it at a party's request.
  - ask: X refuses to comply. What can the winning state do?
    hint: Art 94 UN Charter.
    answer: Bring the matter before the Security Council, which may make recommendations or decide on measures to give effect to the judgment.
  - ask: Could an NGO ask the Court to find a former minister guilty of war crimes?
    hint: Art 34.
    answer: No. Only states may be parties, and the ICJ does not decide individual criminal responsibility.
