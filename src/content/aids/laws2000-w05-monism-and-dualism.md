plain: Monism treats international and national law as one legal system, so courts can apply international law directly. Dualism treats them as separate, so international law must first be turned into national law.
concrete: "Germany's Basic Law art 25 makes the general rules of international law \"an integral part of federal law\" that take precedence over the laws and directly create rights and duties — no Act needed: monism. The Netherlands blends the two: Parliament must approve treaties (art 91), but once published, treaty provisions binding on all persons apply (art 93) and override conflicting statutory regulations (art 94). Australia, like the UK and Canada, leans dualist."
formal: "Monism: one normative legal order; international law has direct effect (incorporation), the same authority as national law, and conflicts are resolved \"frequently in favour of international law\"; often civil law. Dualism: distinct orders; only national law has domestic force, so international law must be transformed; inconsistencies do not arise; often common law. \"In reality, blends of the two theories characterise\" most domestic legal orders (slide 8)."

terms:
  - term: incorporation (direct effect)
    gloss: International law applying in the domestic legal order without any transforming step — the monist mechanism.
  - term: transformation
    gloss: Turning international law into national law, usually by legislation, before it has domestic effect — the dualist mechanism.
  - term: ideal types
    gloss: "Abstract models: no state applies either theory perfectly; most systems blend them while leaning one way."
