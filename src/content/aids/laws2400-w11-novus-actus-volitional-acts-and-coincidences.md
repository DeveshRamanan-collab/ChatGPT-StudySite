plain: "A later event breaks the chain only if it is a genuinely new cause: ordinarily a free, deliberate human choice, or an extremely unlikely coincidence unconnected with the defendant's negligence."
concrete: "McKew's leg, injured by the defendant's negligence, was prone to give way. He went down a steep staircase without a handrail, unaided; the leg gave way and he suffered a severe ankle fracture. The original injury set up the fall, but his own unreasonable conduct was a novus actus interveniens: the chain was broken, and the defendant was not liable for the second injury."
formal: "The Oropesa: is the subsequent event 'ultroneous, something unwarrantable, a new cause which disturbs the sequence of events, something which can be described as either unreasonable or extraneous or extrinsic'? To sever the connection, the intervening act must ordinarily be voluntary human action (conduct which is volitional) or a causally independent act which is an extremely unlikely coincidence (Haber v Walker)."
terms:
  - term: Volitional conduct
    gloss: "Voluntary human action: a deliberate choice. The flowchart's examples that severed the chain: Rickards v Lothian, Yates v Jones, CES v Superclinics (Aust) Pty Ltd."
  - term: Non-volitional conduct
    gloss: "Conduct that is not a free choice. The flowchart's examples did not sever the chain: Urbanski v Patel, Haynes v G Harwood & Son, Medlin, Hirst."
  - term: Ultroneous
    gloss: "The Oropesa's word for a subsequent event that is unwarrantable: a new cause which disturbs the sequence of events."
  - term: Causally independent coincidence
    gloss: A later event unconnected with the defendant's negligence and extremely unlikely; it can sever the chain (Haber v Walker).
misconceptions:

    tempting: A new person acting after the breach looks like a fresh cause.
    correction: To sever, it must ordinarily be voluntary, volitional human action. The flowchart lists non-volitional conduct that did not sever the chain (eg Haynes v G Harwood & Son).
  - claim: Only voluntary human action can ever sever the chain.
    tempting: Voluntariness is the first limb on the flowchart.
    correction: "There is a second route: a causally independent act which is a coincidence which is extremely unlikely (Haber v Walker)."
  - claim: Under Haber v Walker, any unlikely later event breaks the chain.
    tempting: Haber v Walker is cited for extremely unlikely coincidences.
    correction: The event must also be causally independent. A reaction to the collision the defendant caused, such as another driver's reflex swerve, is not.
