plain: Volenti defeats a claim only where the plaintiff knew the very risk that hurt them, fully understood it and freely accepted it. Knowing an activity is dangerous in general is not enough.
concrete: "In McNamara v Duncan, a footballer was struck on the head by an opponent's raised elbow moments after kicking the ball, fracturing his skull. The defendant relied on volenti, arguing the blow was contact a footballer must expect. Fox J disagreed: a player does not consent to deliberate injury, even though it is known such acts may and probably will occur. That was a trespass claim, but it shows general awareness is not acceptance."
formal: Volenti non fit injuria operates as a complete defence to negligence. The defendant must show the plaintiff not only perceived the existence of the risk but fully appreciated its nature and extent and voluntarily accepted that specific risk (Banovic v Perkovic; Carey v Lake Macquarie City Council). Effective knowledge extends to an acceptance of materialisation of the risk (Carey; ICI v Shatwell).
terms:
  - term: Volenti non fit injuria
    gloss: Voluntary assumption of risk. Its essence is the plaintiff's willingness to assume a risk the plaintiff knew existed in a particular activity.
  - term: Specific risk
    gloss: The particular risk that materialised. The defence is confined to it and does not extend to an awareness of the general nature of the risk.
  - term: Effective knowledge
    gloss: "More than knowing a risk exists: an acceptance of that risk materialising (Carey; ICI v Shatwell)."
misconceptions:
  - claim: Knowing that an activity is dangerous is enough for volenti.
    tempting: The plaintiff chose to take part knowing it was risky.
    correction: The defence is narrowly confined to the specific risk; it does not extend to an awareness of the general nature of the risk.
  - claim: A plaintiff who carelessly takes a risk has voluntarily assumed it.
    tempting: She chose to act as she did.
    correction: Carelessness for her own safety is contributory negligence territory. Volenti needs proof that she fully appreciated and voluntarily accepted the specific risk.
  - claim: Volenti, like contributory negligence, reduces damages in proportion to the plaintiff's fault.
    tempting: Both defences turn on the plaintiff's own choices.
    correction: Volenti, if proved, is a complete defence that defeats the claim. Contributory negligence results in a partial, just and equitable reduction.

elements:
  - name: Perceived the risk
    test: The defendant must show the plaintiff perceived the existence of the risk that materialised.
  - name: Fully appreciated it
    test: Did the plaintiff fully appreciate the nature, extent and likely manifestation of that specific risk, not just its general nature?
    cases:
      - banovic-v-perkovic
      - carey-v-lake-macquarie-city-council
  - name: Voluntarily accepted it
    test: Did the plaintiff voluntarily accept that specific risk, including its materialisation?
    cases:
      - carey-v-lake-macquarie-city-council
      - ici-v-shatwell
what-if:
  - change: The plaintiff knew only that the activity was risky in a general way.
    effect: "Volenti fails: the defence does not extend to an awareness of the general nature of the risk. Consider contributory negligence instead."
  - change: The plaintiff appreciated the specific risk but had no real choice about facing it.
    effect: The acceptance must be voluntary. Without a voluntary acceptance of that specific risk, the defence is not made out.
boundary:
  - text: "Rootes v Shelton: a waterskier hurt partly because the towing boat's observer failed to warn of objects."
    why: Taking part in a pastime may mean accepting risks inherent in it, but that does not eliminate all duty of care (Barwick CJ); the boat driver owed and breached a duty.
  - text: A passenger who relied on a driver known to be drunk.
    why: Courts are generally reluctant to allow volenti against such passengers, and legislation such as Motor Accidents Act 1988 (NSW) s 76 excludes it.
