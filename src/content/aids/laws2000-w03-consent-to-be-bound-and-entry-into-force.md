plain: Agreeing to a treaty, by signing or ratifying, is different from the treaty taking effect; in between, a state must not undermine the treaty, and states can agree to apply it early.
concrete: The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea was finalised and opened for ratification in 1982 and widely signed. But it entered into force only in 1994, once the 60th instrument of ratification had been deposited. For about twelve years, states that had signed or even ratified were not bound by its detailed provisions; art 18 VCLT asked only that they not defeat its object and purpose.
formal: "Consent may be expressed \"by signature, exchange of instruments constituting a treaty, ratification, acceptance, approval or accession, or by any other means if so agreed\" (VCLT art 11). Ratification is required if the treaty says so, the negotiators agreed, signature was subject to ratification, or full powers or negotiations showed that intention (art 14). Entry into force: as the treaty provides or negotiating states agree (art 24)."

terms:
  - term: signature
    gloss: Signals a state's intention to become a party; sometimes it also expresses consent to be bound, as with the Somalia–Kenya MoU.
  - term: ratification
    gloss: The state's formal acceptance of a treaty's obligations, making it legally binding on the state; required in the four art 14 situations.
  - term: entry into force
    gloss: "When the treaty starts operating: as it provides or as the negotiating states agree (art 24), often after a set number of ratifications."
  - term: provisional application
    gloss: Applying a treaty, or part of it, before entry into force where the treaty provides or the negotiating states agree (art 25).
misconceptions:
  - claim: Once a state signs or ratifies, the treaty applies to it in full immediately.
    tempting: "'Consent to be bound' sounds like being bound."
    correction: Consent and entry into force are distinct (art 24). UNCLOS, opened for ratification in 1982, entered into force only in 1994; meanwhile states had only the limited art 18 duty.
  - claim: Art 18 requires a signatory to comply with every provision before entry into force.
    tempting: It is the rule governing the interim period.
    correction: Art 18 only obliges a state to refrain from acts that would defeat the treaty's object and purpose; detailed compliance is not required.
  - claim: Art 18 and art 25 are the same interim rule.
    tempting: Both operate before entry into force.
    correction: Art 18 is an automatic, minimal duty not to defeat object and purpose. Art 25 is opt-in provisional application of the treaty, in part or in full, where the treaty or negotiating states so agree.

    hint: Signing shows authority; does it show consent?
    answer: Not necessarily. Under art 11 consent may come by signature or ratification, and many treaties, especially multilateral ones, require ratification.
  - ask: When is ratification required?
    hint: Art 14 has four triggers.
    answer: If the treaty says so, the negotiators agreed, the representative signed subject to ratification, or full powers or negotiations showed that intention.
  - ask: Why does Australia usually ratify rather than rely on signature?
    hint: Dualism.
    answer: As a dualist common law country, Australia generally transforms treaties into domestic law before ratifying, after JSCOT scrutiny.
  - ask: Australia has ratified, but the treaty is not yet in force. What must it do?
    hint: Art 18(b).
    answer: Refrain from acts that would defeat the treaty's object and purpose, provided entry into force is not unduly delayed.
  - ask: Could the parties apply some provisions straight away?
    hint: Art 25.
    answer: Yes — provisional application, in part or in full, if the treaty provides for it or the negotiating states otherwise agree.

what-if:
  - change: The treaty itself provides that its fisheries quotas apply provisionally.
    effect: Under art 25 those provisions apply before entry into force — a fuller obligation than art 18's duty — because the treaty so provides.
  - change: Australia signs subject to ratification, then makes clear it will not become a party.
    effect: Art 18(a) applies only until a state makes its intention not to become a party clear; after that, even the object-and-purpose duty ends.
  - change: Entry into force is unduly delayed for decades.
    effect: The art 18(b) duty of states that have consented applies only provided entry into force is not unduly delayed, so the interim duty may lapse.
boundary:
  - text: The Somalia–Kenya MoU, which expressly entered into force on signature.
    why: Signature both expressed consent and brought the instrument into force, so there was no ratification step and no art 18 interim period.
  - text: Provisional application agreed by only some negotiating states.
    why: Art 25 can rest on a separate agreement among some states; it need not bind every party, unlike entry into force under the treaty's own terms.
transfer:
  - scenario: State X ratifies a new treaty protecting a marine reserve from deep-sea mining. The treaty needs 40 ratifications and has 25. Meanwhile X licenses a company to strip-mine the reserve.
    answer: The treaty is not in force (art 24), so X is not bound by its detailed provisions. But having consented pending entry into force, X must refrain from acts defeating the treaty's object and purpose (art 18(b)), provided entry into force is not unduly delayed. Strip-mining the very reserve the treaty protects is likely to defeat that purpose.
