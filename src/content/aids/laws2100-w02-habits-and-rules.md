plain: Habits and rules both show people behaving alike, but only rules attract criticism of deviation, supply a reason for it, and are seen from the inside as standards. Habits cannot confer authority.
concrete: "Most people go to the cinema at the weekend; men remove their hats on entering a church. From outside, both look the same: convergence of behaviour. But a man who keeps his hat on in church is criticised, and the rule is cited as the reason; skipping the cinema draws no criticism at all. The first is a social rule, the second a mere habit."
formal: "Chapter IV: 'the whole conception of a supreme and independent sovereign habitually obeyed ... is misleading' (CL 27). Can a habit of obedience explain the continuity and persistence of law, the sovereign's legally unlimited power, its position above the law? Rules differ from habits: deviation is criticised, the rule is a good reason for criticism, rules have an internal aspect. 'Habits are not \"normative\"' (CL 60)."
