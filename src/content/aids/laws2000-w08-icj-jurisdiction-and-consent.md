plain: The ICJ can hear a contentious case only between states, and only if both have consented — by special agreement, treaty clause, matching optional-clause declarations, or acceptance after filing.
concrete: In the tutorial, State A, a small West African country, sues State B, a Pacific nation, alleging conduct contrary to the Genocide Convention. The Court has no compulsory jurisdiction, so A must point to B's consent. Art IX of the Convention sends disputes about its interpretation, application or fulfilment to the ICJ at the request of any party. If both are parties, that treaty clause supplies consent — provided a dispute existed when A filed.
formal: "Only states may be parties (art 34 ICJ Statute); international organisations may only seek advisory opinions. Jurisdiction rests on consent, to the specific case or a category of disputes. Art 36: special agreement (compromis); a treaty clause; declarations under art 36(2) accepting jurisdiction over future disputes with states accepting the same obligation (optional clause); forum prorogatum. The Court decides its own jurisdiction (art 36(6))."
terms:
  - term: compromis
    gloss: A special agreement by which the parties submit a particular dispute to the Court.
  - term: optional clause (art 36(2))
    gloss: A declaration accepting the Court's jurisdiction over future legal disputes with any other state accepting the same obligation.
  - term: forum prorogatum
    gloss: Consent given by a state after an application has been filed against it.
  - term: compétence de la compétence
    gloss: The Court's power to rule on its own jurisdiction when the parties disagree (art 36(6)).
misconceptions:
  - claim: UN membership gives the ICJ jurisdiction over disputes between any members.
    tempting: The Court can hear disputes between the 193 members.
    correction: "That is access, not jurisdiction. The ICJ has no compulsory jurisdiction: it needs the parties' consent to the case, or to a category of disputes it falls within."
  - claim: One state's optional-clause declaration lets it sue any other state.
    tempting: The declaration is general.
    correction: It operates only in relation to another state accepting the same obligation. A state with no declaration, treaty clause or compromis has not consented.
  - claim: Once both parties have consented, jurisdiction cannot be contested.
    tempting: Consent is the core of jurisdiction.
    correction: The case must fall within the consent given, and a dispute must have existed when proceedings were instituted. If the parties disagree, the Court decides (art 36(6)).
socratic:
  - ask: An NGO and the UN each want to bring a case against State X. Can they?
    hint: Art 34.
    answer: No. Only states may be parties. The UN may request advisory opinions but cannot be a party; the NGO has no access at all.
  - ask: State A wants to sue State B. What must A point to?
    hint: No compulsory jurisdiction.
    answer: B's consent — to this case, or to a category of disputes that includes it.
  - ask: What are the ways consent can be given?
    hint: Art 36.
    answer: A compromis for the particular dispute; a treaty clause such as art IX of the Genocide Convention; declarations by both states under art 36(2); or forum prorogatum.
  - ask: B had not consented when A filed, but afterwards accepts the Court's jurisdiction over the case. Effect?
    hint: Later consent.
    answer: "Forum prorogatum: consent given after the application was filed founds jurisdiction."
  - ask: A and B disagree whether B's consent covers this dispute. Who decides?
    hint: Art 36(6).
    answer: The Court itself — compétence de la compétence.

  - change: Neither A nor B is party to the Genocide Convention or any treaty with an ICJ clause, and B refuses to take part.
    effect: No jurisdiction unless consent appears another way — matching art 36(2) declarations, a compromis, or later acceptance. The Court has no compulsory jurisdiction.
