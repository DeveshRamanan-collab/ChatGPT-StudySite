plain: "To win in negligence, the plaintiff must prove three things: the defendant owed them a duty of care, breached it, and the breach caused them compensable harm."
concrete: "Mrs Donoghue drank ginger beer from an opaque bottle; a decomposed snail floated out, and she sued the manufacturer, Stevenson, for shock and severe gastro-enteritis. Her claim needed all three elements: a duty owed to her as a consumer, a failure to take reasonable care, and harm caused by that failure. Without her illness there would be no claim, because negligence is an action on the case."
formal: "Negligence is an action on the case, so damage is vital. The plaintiff must prove (1) a duty of care: reasonable foreseeability (the neighbour principle) is always required but not always sufficient; (2) breach; (3) that the breach caused compensable harm (factual causation and scope of liability). CLA Part 1A applies to any claim for damages for harm resulting from negligence (s 5A), except liability excluded by s 3B."
analogy:
  text: "Three gates in a row: the plaintiff must open duty, then breach, then causation of harm. Failing any one gate ends the claim, however wide the others stand open."
  maps:
    - from: Gate one
      to: Duty of care
    - from: Gate two
      to: Breach of that duty
    - from: Gate three
      to: Breach caused compensable harm (factual causation, scope of liability)
    - from: Who must open them
      to: The plaintiff bears the burden of proof on all issues, on the balance of probabilities
  limits:
    - "The gates are not identical: reasonable foreseeability is asked at each, but it asks a different question each time."
    - "Passing all three gates is not the end: defences (e.g. contributory negligence, obvious risk) can still reduce or defeat the claim."
terms:
  - term: Action on the case
    gloss: A claim in which damage is an essential element, unlike trespass, which is actionable per se.
  - term: Compensable harm
    gloss: Physical, psychiatric or economic harm the law compensates. CLA s 5 defines harm to include personal injury or death, property damage and economic loss.
  - term: Scope of liability
    gloss: Whether it is appropriate for liability to extend to the harm caused (remoteness of harm).
  - term: Section 3B exclusions
    gloss: "Liability outside the CLA: intentional acts with intent to cause injury or death, sexual assault or misconduct, workers' compensation and dust diseases claims."
misconceptions:
  - claim: Proving that the defendant caused the harm is enough to win in negligence.
    tempting: Causation feels like the heart of "you hurt me, so you pay".
    correction: "Negligence has three elements: duty, breach, and causation of compensable harm. Duty and breach must be established before causation matters."
  - claim: Like battery, negligence is actionable without proof of damage.
    tempting: Both are torts arising from the defendant's conduct towards the plaintiff.
    correction: Negligence is an action on the case, so damage is vital to initiate an action. Battery is actionable per se.
  - claim: The Civil Liability Act has replaced the common law of duty of care.
    tempting: The CLA codified breach (ss 5B–5C) and causation (ss 5D–5E).
    correction: "Duty remains common law, modified only in specific contexts: public authorities, mental harm, recreational activities, professional negligence, intoxication and criminal enterprise."

    hint: Is negligence actionable per se?
    answer: "No. Negligence is an action on the case: damage is vital to initiate an action."
  - ask: Now suppose you are hurt. What must you prove?
    hint: A relationship, some conduct, a consequence.
    answer: That the defendant owed you a duty of care, breached that duty, and that the breach caused you compensable harm.
  - ask: Which element is usually uncontroversial in practice?
    hint: Think of drivers and other road users.
    answer: Duty of care. Reasonable foreseeability is always required, but most cases fall within an established category.
  - ask: Does the Civil Liability Act govern your claim?
    hint: Check s 5A, then s 3B.
    answer: Part 1A applies to any claim for damages for harm resulting from negligence (s 5A), unless s 3B excludes it, e.g. an intentional act with intent to injure, or dust diseases.
  - ask: Which elements has the CLA codified?
    hint: Look at ss 5B–5E.
    answer: Breach (ss 5B, 5C) and causation (ss 5D, 5E). Duty stays largely common law, and common law principles still help interpret those sections.
elements:
  - name: Duty of care
    test: Did the defendant owe the plaintiff a duty? Reasonable foreseeability is always required, not always sufficient; then established category or salient features.
    cases:
      - donoghue-v-stevenson
  - name: Breach
    test: Did the defendant's conduct fall below that of a reasonable person, given the degree of foreseeable risk and the reasonableness of the response?
    authorities:
      - civil-liability-act-2002-nsw-s5b
  - name: Causation of compensable harm
    test: Did the breach cause physical, psychiatric or economic harm (factual causation), and is it appropriate for liability to extend to that harm (scope of liability)?
    authorities:
      - civil-liability-act-2002-nsw-s5d
      - civil-liability-act-2002-nsw-s5
what-if:
  - change: The defendant's careless act caused no damage at all.
    effect: "There is no action in negligence: as an action on the case it requires damage. Contrast trespass, which is actionable per se."
  - change: The defendant acted with intent to injure the plaintiff.
    effect: Section 3B excludes civil liability for an intentional act done with intent to cause injury or death from most of the CLA, so the common law and intentional torts govern instead.
  - change: The harm is a dust disease contracted at work.
    effect: Section 3B excludes dust diseases claims from most of the CLA; they are covered by the common law and/or other legislative regimes.
