plain: For intentional torts to the person the usual remedy is damages — nominal, compensatory, aggravated or exemplary — and sometimes an injunction to stop a threatened tort from happening.
formal: Damages are the most common remedy for intentional interference with the person; in suitable cases an injunction may prevent an anticipated tort (e.g. battery). Damages may be nominal (no harm or damage caused), compensatory (the position had the wrong not occurred), aggravated (deliberate conduct resulting in an affront to the plaintiff) and/or exemplary (to punish particularly egregious conduct).

terms:
  - term: Nominal damages
    gloss: A token award where the tort is made out but no harm or damage was caused — possible because trespass to the person is actionable per se.
  - term: Aggravated damages
    gloss: Awarded for the defendant's deliberate conduct that resulted in an affront to the plaintiff, beyond the physical or economic loss.
  - term: Exemplary damages
    gloss: Awarded to punish the defendant where the conduct was particularly egregious, rather than to compensate the plaintiff.
  - term: Injunction
    gloss: A court order that, in suitable cases, prevents an anticipated tort — such as a threatened battery — from taking place.
misconceptions:
  - claim: If no harm was caused, no damages can be awarded.
    tempting: Damages usually compensate a loss.
    correction: Trespass to the person is actionable per se. Where no harm or damage was caused, nominal damages are likely (Lecture 1, slide 32).
  - claim: Aggravated and exemplary damages are the same thing.
    tempting: Both respond to bad conduct by the defendant.
    correction: Aggravated damages respond to deliberate conduct that results in an affront to the plaintiff; exemplary damages "punish" particularly egregious conduct (slide 32).
  - claim: Only one head of damages can be awarded for a tort.
    tempting: One wrong seems to deserve one award.
    correction: "Damages may be nominal, compensatory and aggravated and/or exemplary: different heads address different aspects of the wrong and can be combined (slide 32)."
