plain: A treaty can be properly made yet invalid, if a state's consent came from error, fraud, corruption or coercion, or if the treaty breaches a peremptory norm.
concrete: "The lecturer's examples: a treaty is concluded on the basis of a map that turns out to be wrong, and the map was material to a state's decision to join — the treaty may be invalid for error (art 48). Make the map wrong on purpose, to lure the other state into signing, and it is fraud (art 49). Bribe the negotiator to sign when they otherwise would not, and it is corruption (art 50)."

terms:
  - term: relative invalidity
    gloss: "Grounds going to defective consent (arts 46–50): internal law, error, fraud and corruption; the lecture calls such treaties voidable."
  - term: absolute invalidity
    gloss: "Grounds making a treaty void (arts 51–53): coercion of a representative, threat or use of force against the state, conflict with jus cogens."
  - term: void ab initio
    gloss: Invalid from the very beginning, as if it never had legal effect — for example, a treaty procured by force against the state (art 52).
  - term: peremptory norm (jus cogens)
    gloss: A norm accepted by the community of states as a whole as permitting no derogation, e.g. prohibitions of torture, genocide, aggression and piracy; self-determination.
