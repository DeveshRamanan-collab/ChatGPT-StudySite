plain: Vicarious liability makes one person liable for someone else's tort — typically an employer for a tort its employee commits in the course of employment.
concrete: "Fleming's \"man of straw\": an employee who commits a tort at work is apt to have no insurance and little money, so as a rule is not worth suing. The employer is a more promising source of recompense, and can pass the loss on through liability insurance and higher prices. Holding it liable also gives it an incentive to supervise and discipline staff."
formal: "Vicarious liability is one person or entity's liability for the conduct of others; the typical case is an employer's liability for a tort committed by an employee within the course of employment (tests in Topic 12). Fleming's policy reasons (Sappideen, Vines and Eldridge [13.45]): fairness to those harmed by the enterprise, a better source of recompense, wide distribution of losses, and admonitory value."

misconceptions:
  - claim: An employer can be vicariously liable even if the employee committed no tort.
    tempting: The focus is on the employer paying, so the employee's wrong seems beside the point.
    correction: "Vicarious liability is about who pays, not whether a tort was committed: the typical case is liability for a tort committed by an employee (Lecture 2, slide 14)."
  - claim: Vicarious liability means the employer was itself at fault.
    tempting: Normally people pay for their own wrongs.
    correction: It is liability for the conduct of others. Fleming's reasons are fairness, recompense, loss-spreading and deterrence, not the employer's own fault (Lecture 2, slides 14–15).
  - claim: Once the employer is liable, the employee cannot be sued.
    tempting: Fleming says employees are not worth suing.
    correction: Fleming says employees are, as a rule, not worth suing because they are rarely financially responsible — a practical point, not a legal bar. The employee who committed the tort remains a wrongdoer.
