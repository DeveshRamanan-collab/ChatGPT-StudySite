plain: "After factual causation, the court asks whether the defendant ought to answer for this harm. Two things can take it outside: a later event that breaks the chain, or harm of an unforeseeable kind."
concrete: "In the Topic 10 tutorial problem, Nancy's careless driving pushes Fred's car into Lucy, breaking both her legs. Later events follow. A youth snatches her handbag at the scene; Dr Sun's untried technique leaves her with a permanent ankle disability; she gives up kickboxing, drinks heavily and loses her job. Each later loss raises scope of liability: did a later event break the chain, or is the harm too remote?"
formal: "CLA s 5D(1)(b): 'that it is appropriate for the scope of the negligent person's liability to extend to the harm so caused'. Section 5D(4): the court is to consider whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed. This embraces policy-based considerations the courts are required to articulate (Wallace v Kam) and raises normative questions (Ruddock v Taylor)."
terms:
  - term: Scope of liability
    gloss: "Whether it is appropriate for the negligent person's liability to extend to the harm so caused: the second element of causation, s 5D(1)(b)."
  - term: Policy-based considerations
    gloss: Reasons for or against imposing responsibility, which the courts are required to articulate when deciding scope of liability (Wallace v Kam).
  - term: New intervening act
    gloss: "Novus actus interveniens: a later event that may break the chain of causation between the negligence and the harm."
  - term: Remoteness of harm
    gloss: "Concerned with the extent of the defendant's liability for losses: was the damage of a reasonably foreseeable kind?"
misconceptions:
  - claim: Once factual causation is shown, the defendant is liable for all the harm that follows.
    tempting: Factual causation is often the hard part of the argument.
    correction: Section 5D(1) makes scope of liability a separate element, and s 5D(4) asks whether and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed.
  - claim: Policy considerations belong only at the duty of care stage.
    tempting: Policy is familiar from the salient features approach to duty.
    correction: Scope of liability itself embraces policy-based considerations which the courts are required to articulate (Wallace v Kam).
  - claim: New intervening acts and remoteness are the two elements of s 5D(1).
    tempting: They are the two common law concepts on the scope of liability flowchart.
    correction: The two elements are factual causation (s 5D(1)(a)) and scope of liability (s 5D(1)(b)). Novus actus and remoteness both sit inside element (b).

  - ask: What does s 5D(4) direct the court to consider?
    hint: Amongst other relevant things...
    answer: Whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed on the negligent party.
  - ask: What must the court do with the policy behind its answer?
    hint: Wallace v Kam.
    answer: "Articulate it: scope of liability embraces policy-based considerations which the courts are required to state."
  - ask: A youth snatches Lucy's handbag at the scene. Which branch does that raise?
    hint: A later event, or an unexpected kind of harm?
    answer: "New intervening acts: whether the youth's deliberate later act breaks the chain between Nancy's negligence and the loss of the bag."
  - ask: The harm is of the expected kind but much worse than anyone would predict. Which branch?
    hint: No later event; think extent.
    answer: Remoteness of harm. The extent of the harm need not be reasonably foreseeable, provided harm of that kind was.

what-if:
  - change: No later event occurs; the only dispute is how badly the plaintiff was hurt.
    effect: "Only the remoteness branch is engaged: was harm of that kind reasonably foreseeable? The extent and precise manner of the harm need not be."
  - change: A stranger, for his own reasons, steals the plaintiff's bag after the crash.
    effect: "The novus actus branch is engaged: a third party's deliberate, volitional act may break the chain, taking the bag's loss outside the scope of liability."
  - change: The plaintiff's own careless conduct follows the defendant's negligence.
    effect: "Use the negligent-acts branch: whether it severs the chain is a matter of fact and degree (Mahony), and the plaintiff's negligence does not necessarily sever it."
boundary:
  - text: A later careless act that is the very risk the defendant's negligence created.
    why: "Stays within scope: where the defendant generated the very risk and the injury occurs in the ordinary course of things, later negligence does not sever the chain (March v Stramare)."

    answer: "After factual causation, apply s 5D(1)(b) and s 5D(4) to each later harm. The court must articulate why responsibility should or should not extend (Wallace v Kam)."
