plain: "To decide trespass to land, pass five gates in order: exclusive possession, of land, direct invasion, fault, and no lawful authority. Stop at the first gate the facts fail."
concrete: "In Barker v The Queen (1983) 153 CLR 338, a man authorised by the occupier to keep an eye on the house while they were away, and told where the key was, entered to steal. He was a trespasser: his entry was for a purpose outside the authority he had been given, so it was without lawful authority, justification or excuse — the last gate in the flowchart's definition."
formal: "Trespass to land involves the direct, intentional or negligent invasion or interference with land in possession of the plaintiff without lawful authority, justification or excuse (Barker v The Queen; Roy v O'Neill). General principles, in order: exclusive possession; of land; direct invasion or interference; fault; without lawful authority, justification or excuse. Then remedies."
terms:
  - term: Title to sue
    gloss: In the flowchart, possession of the land at the time of the interference — by title with actual possession, by consent under a lease, or by adverse possession.
  - term: Adverse possession
    gloss: Possession without title or consent; one of the ways the flowchart says exclusive possession can arise.
  - term: Exceeding a licence
    gloss: Going beyond a licence's terms, or staying after it expires or is revoked; the flowchart treats this as a direct invasion.
misconceptions:
  - claim: An owner always has title to sue in trespass.
    tempting: Ownership usually comes with possession.
    correction: Title to sue means possession at the time of the interference. Where a tenant stays on after the lease ends, the owner may need to retake possession to sue (flowchart p 142).
  - claim: An indirect interference with land, like noise, is trespass.
    tempting: It still interferes with the land.
    correction: Trespass needs a direct invasion or interference; indirect interference may be another tort, such as private nuisance (flowchart p 142).
  - claim: Trespass to land requires an intention to trespass.
    tempting: It is grouped with the intentional torts.
    correction: The act must be intentional or negligent, but the defendant need not intend to trespass and mistake is generally no defence; only involuntary conduct utterly without fault is not actionable (flowchart p 142).

elements:
  - name: Exclusive possession
    test: "Title to sue: possession at the time of the interference — by title with actual possession, by lease, or by adverse possession. Licences typically do not confer it."
    cases:
      - newington-v-windeyer
  - name: Of land
    test: Extends to invasion of or direct interference with airspace, fixtures and subsoil.
    cases:
      - bernstein-v-skyviews
      - kelsen-v-imperial-tobacco-co
  - name: Direct invasion or interference
    test: Includes exceeding a licence's terms, or staying beyond its expiry or revocation. Indirect interference may be private nuisance.
  - name: Fault
    test: An intentional or negligent act. No need to intend to trespass; mistake is generally no defence; involuntary conduct utterly without fault is not actionable.
  - name: Without lawful authority, justification or excuse
    test: No consent, licence or other lawful authority covers the entry or the purpose for which it was made.
    cases:
      - barker-v-the-queen
      - roy-v-oneill
