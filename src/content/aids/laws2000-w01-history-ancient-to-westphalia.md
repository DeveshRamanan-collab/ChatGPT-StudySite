plain: Before modern states, rules between communities came from shared expectations and natural-law ideas; the 1648 Peace of Westphalia organised Europe into sovereign states, starting the modern state system.
concrete: In 1532 Francisco de Vitoria, a Spanish Dominican professor, lectured on Spain's conquests in the New World. He concluded that the native peoples were part of the human race, so natural law applied to them. Because people already lived there, there had been no 'discovery', and conquest — like any other war of territorial expansion — was not justified under natural law.
formal: "Natural law (jus naturale) holds that intrinsic values govern relations universally, across time and place. Hugo Grotius (1583–1645) secularised it and distinguished jus gentium: human-made law, variable in time and place, valid because states consent. The Peace of Westphalia (1648; Treaties of Munster and Osnabruck) organised territory and people into sovereign states — the 'beginning' of the state system and birth of 'sovereignty'."
