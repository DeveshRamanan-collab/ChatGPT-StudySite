plain: "Wrongs fall into two historical families: trespass, for direct interference with a person, land or goods, and action on the case, for indirect or consequential harm. Each family needs different elements."
concrete: "In Williams v Milotin (1957) 97 CLR 465 a boy riding his bicycle was hit by a truck. He sued more than three years later. Trespass to the person then had a three-year limitation period; negligence, an action on the case, had six years. The High Court let him sue in negligence: the same facts gave rise to two causes of action, and he could choose."
formal: "Intentional torts developed as two sets of causes of action: trespass (a direct interference with the person, property or goods) and action on the case (indirect and consequential damage). The distinction remains critical in Australia because different elements must be proved. All forms of trespass share four elements: voluntary act, directness, actionable per se (no proof of damage) and fault."

terms:
  - term: Trespass
    gloss: "A direct interference with the person, property or goods: trespass to the person (assault, battery, false imprisonment), to land, and to chattels/goods."
  - term: Action on the case
    gloss: Actions for indirect and consequential damage, e.g. Wilkinson v Downton, malicious prosecution, conversion and detinue; beyond intentional torts, negligence and nuisance.
  - term: Cause of action
    gloss: The essential ingredients of the right the plaintiff seeks to enforce (Williams v Milotin); one set of facts can found two.
  - term: Limitation period
    gloss: The deadline to start proceedings. In Williams v Milotin, three years for trespass to the person and six for negligence under the law then in force.

elements:
  - name: Voluntary act
    test: The defendant's conduct must be a positive act. An act during an epileptic fit, one caused by a third party's force, or an omission does not count.
  - name: Directness
    test: The interference must follow immediately from the act, not consequentially after an intervening event. Directness is often an issue of time.
    cases:
      - hutchins-v-maughan
  - name: Actionable per se
    test: No proof of damage is required, to protect bodily integrity, liberty and property. (An action on the case requires proof of damage.)
  - name: Fault
    test: Intention, recklessness or negligence. The plaintiff proves the direct interference; the defendant must then disprove fault, except where the harm arose from use of the highway.
