plain: If a person freely agrees to what would otherwise be a tort, they generally cannot sue for it. Courts disagree about whether the plaintiff must disprove consent or the defendant must prove it.
concrete: A patient sues a dentist in battery over unnecessary treatment, as in White v Johnston. If consent is a defence (McHugh J in Marion's Case), the dentist must plead and prove that the patient agreed. If consent is an element (Leeming JA in White v Johnston), the patient must prove she did not agree. When the evidence about consent is evenly balanced, whichever party carries that onus loses.
formal: "Consent is a defence to battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass to land and interference with chattels, underpinned by deference to individual autonomy. England and New Zealand treat it as an element (plaintiff proves lack of consent). In Australia it is unsettled: McHugh J (Marion's Case, obiter) — a defence the defendant proves; Leeming JA (White v Johnston, obiter) — an essential element of assault and battery."

terms:
  - term: Onus of proof
    gloss: Which party must prove a point. If the evidence on that point is inconclusive, the party bearing the onus loses on it.
  - term: Element vs defence
    gloss: An element is part of the plaintiff's cause of action, which she must prove; a defence is raised and proved by the defendant.
  - term: Obiter
    gloss: "A judge's remark that was not necessary to decide the case: persuasive, not binding."
    example: Both McHugh J's and Leeming JA's views on consent are obiter.
