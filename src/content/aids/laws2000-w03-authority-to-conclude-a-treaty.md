terms:
  - term: full powers
    gloss: A document from the competent authority designating a person to represent the state — e.g. Singapore's Foreign Minister authorising its Trade Minister to sign an investment agreement.
  - term: executive agreement
    gloss: In the US, an agreement the President makes without Senate approval; still binding on the parties under international law.
  - term: royal prerogative (foreign relations)
    gloss: The UK Crown's power, exercised by the government, to conclude treaties; s 20 of the 2010 Act requires laying before Parliament before ratification.
  - term: s 61 of the Constitution
    gloss: Vests executive power, including concluding treaties, in the Australian executive; DFAT and the AG's Office of International Law manage treaties in practice.
misconceptions:

    tempting: Treaties are international acts, so international rules should govern who makes them.
    correction: This is not resolved by international law; look at each state's domestic law. Art 7 VCLT only tells other states whom they may treat as representing a state.
  - claim: The attorney-general is one of the three officials who need no full powers.
    tempting: The Attorney-General's Office of International Law helps manage Australian treaties.
    correction: Art 7(2)(a) names heads of state, heads of government and ministers for foreign affairs; everyone else should produce full powers.
  - claim: A US executive agreement binds only domestically until the Senate approves it.
    tempting: Formal US treaties need the advice and consent of two-thirds of the Senate.
    correction: Though not brought before the Senate, executive agreements "are still binding on the parties under international law" (slide 10) — as the Paris Agreement shows.
