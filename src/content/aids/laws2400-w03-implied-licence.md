plain: Anyone on lawful business may walk up an open path to the front door and knock, unless a locked gate or sign says no. Entering only for some other purpose makes you a trespasser.
concrete: "In Roy v O'Neill [2020] HCA 45 a police officer went to Ms Roy's unit to check compliance with a domestic violence order protecting Mr Johnson, and approached the front door. There he required Ms Roy to give a breath sample. The High Court held he was not a trespasser: his purposes included checking on the protected person's welfare, which was within the implied licence, even though he had another purpose too."
formal: A path or driveway to the front door carries an implied licence for any member of the public on lawful business to approach and knock, unless negated by a locked gate or sign (Halliday v Nevill). It does not extend to entry to breath-test the occupier (Munnings v Barrett) or serve a summons (Plenty v Dillon). An additional alien purpose does not negate it (Roy v O'Neill).

terms:
  - term: Implied licence
    gloss: Permission the law implies for any member of the public on lawful business to go up an unobstructed path or driveway to the door.
  - term: Lawful communication
    gloss: "The licensed purpose: lawful communication with, or delivery to, a person in the house (Halliday v Nevill, quoted in Roy v O'Neill)."
  - term: Negation
    gloss: An occupier negates the implied licence by locking the gate or by a notice indicating that entry is not permitted.
  - term: Revocation
    gloss: The occupier may revoke the licence at any time, e.g. by asking the visitor to leave; a visitor who remains becomes a trespasser.
