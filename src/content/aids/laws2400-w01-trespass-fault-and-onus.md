plain: "Trespass needs fault: intention, recklessness or negligence. The plaintiff proves only the direct interference; the defendant must then disprove fault — except where the harm arose from use of the highway."
concrete: "The lecture's example: a defendant touches someone, mistakenly thinking it is their partner. They intended the act — the touch — so intention is present, and the mistake is no defence. Because the onus shifts in trespass, the person touched need only prove the direct interference; the defendant would then have to disprove fault, for example by proving an inevitable accident."
formal: "Trespass requires fault: intention or recklessness (subjective) or negligence (objective: a reasonable person in the defendant's position). Intention need only go to the act. The plaintiff proves the direct interference; the defendant must then disprove fault, e.g. by proving inevitable accident. Exception: harm from use of the highway, where the plaintiff proves fault — as in any action on the case."

terms:
  - term: Intention
    gloss: A subjective state of mind. For trespass it need only go to performing the act (the interference), not its consequences.
    example: Touching someone mistakenly believed to be your partner.
  - term: Recklessness
    gloss: Where the consequences are so likely that the defendant is taken to have foreseen them; subjective, and generally rare in trespass.
  - term: Negligent trespass
    gloss: Direct interference through failure to exercise reasonable care, judged objectively; rare, as such cases are now mostly brought in negligence.
  - term: Inevitable accident
    gloss: "How a defendant disproves fault once the onus shifts: by proving the interference was a so-called inevitable accident."
misconceptions:
  - claim: In trespass the plaintiff must prove the defendant intended to cause harm.
    tempting: These are called "intentional" torts.
    correction: The plaintiff proves only the direct interference; the defendant must then disprove fault. And intention need only go to the act itself, not its consequences (Lecture 2, slides 23–24).
  - claim: Touching a stranger you mistook for your partner is not intentional.
    tempting: You never meant to touch a stranger.
    correction: You intended the act, which is enough; the mistake is not a defence (Lecture 2, slide 23; text p 46).
  - claim: A pedestrian hit on a public road gets the usual trespass onus shift.
    tempting: In trespass the onus of disproving fault normally falls on the defendant.
    correction: "Highway harm is the exception: the plaintiff must prove fault, because highway users and adjoining owners accept a risk in exercising the public's right to use the highway (slide 24; text p 43)."

    hint: Who carries the onus on fault in trespass?
    answer: No. In trespass she proves only the direct interference; the onus then shifts to the defendant to disprove fault.
  - ask: How can the defendant discharge that onus?
    hint: The lecture names one way.
    answer: By proving the interference was a so-called inevitable accident.
  - ask: Does it matter that the collision happened on a public road?
    hint: Is there an exception?
    answer: Yes. Where harm arises out of use of the highway, the plaintiff must prove the defendant's fault, because the public's right to use the highway carries accepted risk.
  - ask: Which states of mind count as fault, and how is each judged?
    hint: Two subjective, one objective.
    answer: Intention and recklessness are subjective (the defendant's own mind); negligence is objective (a reasonable person in the defendant's position).
  - ask: Would the onus differ if she sued in an action on the case?
    hint: Compare the last line of slide 24.
    answer: Yes. In an action on the case the plaintiff must always prove fault.
elements:

  - name: Plaintiff's onus
