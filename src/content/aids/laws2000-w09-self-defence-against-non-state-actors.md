plain: Can a state defend itself against an armed group operating from another state's territory? Art 51 doesn't say who must attack; the ICJ stresses attribution to the host state, and practice is shifting.
concrete: After Al Qaida's attacks on the United States in September 2001, the US struck Al Qaida in Afghanistan, claiming self-defence. In a letter to the Security Council it argued that the Taliban regime had let Al Qaida operate from its territory and refused to change course. Resolution 1373 (2001) reaffirmed the inherent right of self-defence and called international terrorism a threat to peace — but, the lecturer notes, it did not call the attacks armed attacks.
formal: "Art 51 does not say an armed attack must come from a state. Nicaragua: armed bands, groups, irregulars or mercenaries sent by or on behalf of a state can carry out an armed attack. Resolution 1373 (2001) affirmed the right of self-defence after 9/11. The ICJ treats attribution to the host state as important (Wall, 2004; Armed Activities, 2005). \"Unwilling or unable\" remains controversial; invited action is less contentious."
terms:
  - term: non-state actor
    gloss: An armed group that is not part of any state — e.g. Al Qaida or Islamic State.
  - term: "\"unwilling or unable\""
    gloss: The contested argument that a victim state may act against a non-state actor in a host state that is unwilling or unable to deal with the threat.
  - term: host state
    gloss: The state from whose territory a non-state actor operates; force against the group there engages that state's territorial integrity.
misconceptions:

    correction: Art 51 does not say who must carry out the attack. The ICJ, though, treats attribution to the host state as important (Wall; Armed Activities).
  - claim: "\"Unwilling or unable\" is an accepted rule permitting strikes on armed groups in another state."
    tempting: More and more states invoke it.
    correction: The doctrine remains controversial (e.g. Turkey v PKK in Iraq); the ICJ has emphasised attribution to the host state.

    tempting: It affirmed the right of self-defence in response to them.
    correction: It reaffirmed the inherent right of self-defence and called international terrorism a threat to peace, but did not call the attacks armed attacks (lecture).
