## Overview

- Trespass to the person – battery, assault and false imprisonment – protects bodily integrity and liberty.
- Action on the case for intentional harm – malicious prosecution and intentional infliction of personal
  injury – catches indirect harm trespass cannot reach.
- Remedies for intentional interference with the person.
- Interference with chattels – trespass to goods, conversion and detinue.
- These torts matter well beyond textbook facts: bullying, domestic violence, medical treatment and police
  action are all real settings where they apply.

## General principles of trespass to the person

:::rule
General principles applicable to all intentional torts to the person: the defendant must commit a voluntary
act (an omission will not suffice); the interference must follow directly upon the defendant's act; the torts
are actionable per se, so the plaintiff does not need to prove damage; and the plaintiff must prove fault —
intentional, reckless or negligent conduct on the part of the defendant.
:::

Except in highway cases, the onus is on the defendant to prove that the act was neither intentional nor
negligent.

## Battery

Battery is a direct and intentional or negligent act of the defendant which causes physical contact with the
body of the plaintiff without consent or other lawful justification.

The key elements: voluntary and positive act; direct interference; intentional or negligent contact; physical
interference (bodily contact); unlawful touching (no consent or other lawful excuse).

:::case
*Innes v Wylie* (1844) 174 ER 800 — a police officer standing passively in a doorway committed no battery;
standing passively is not a positive act.
:::

:::case
*Fagan v Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [1969] 1 QB 439 — the defendant's failure to move a car off an
officer's foot was still a positive act, because of its nexus with the earlier positive act of driving onto
the foot — one continuous positive act.
:::

Physical interference need not be the defendant's own body touching the plaintiff — contact through a weapon
or a car, or the use of tear gas that touches the plaintiff's eyes, is enough (*Binsaris v Northern Territory*
(2020) 270 CLR 549).

:::definition
Unlawful touching: the touching need not be hostile — any slight touching without a lawful excuse will
suffice, on the principle of personal inviolability. Express or implied consent, the exigencies of everyday
life, and reasonable force in self-defence will not amount to unlawful touching.
:::

:::case
*Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd* (2001) 53 NSWLR 98 — the Court of Appeal held that the absence of anger or
hostility is not a satisfactory basis for concluding a touching was not a battery; a casino employee's hand on
Rixon's shoulder, placed only to gain his attention, was within the physical contact generally acceptable in
the ordinary conduct of daily life, so no battery was made out.
:::

:::examtip
Is there battery in: tapping a shoulder to attract attention; touching someone with a paper rocket; conducting
surgery without consent? Run each through all five elements — the last two usually turn on unlawful touching.
:::

## Assault

Assault is a direct and intentional act by the defendant which creates in the plaintiff a reasonable
apprehension of an imminent physical interference with their person.

Elements: positive act; direct interference; intentional act; reasonable apprehension in the plaintiff of
imminent harmful or offensive contact; no lawful justification.

The intention required is the intention to cause apprehension of the battery, not the intention to commit the
battery itself (*Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd* (2001) 53 NSWLR 98 [56]–[59]). The act may be mere words or a
gesture. The weight of Australian authority is that assault requires intention, though *New South Wales v
McMaster* [2015] NSWCA 228 [206]–[208] contains dicta suggesting a negligence basis may also do.

Reasonable apprehension focuses on the plaintiff's mind, but the test is objective: would a reasonable person
in the plaintiff's position have apprehended the threat? Fear is not necessary, and words alone suffice
provided they directly raise the apprehension in the plaintiff's mind.

:::case
*Zanker v Vartzokas* (1988) 34 A Crim R 11 — the defendant, driving a girl in his van after she declined his
offer of money for sexual favours, told her he was taking her to a friend who would "really fix her up"; the
threat was held sufficiently imminent even though the harm was not to occur that instant, because imminence
does not require immediacy.
:::

Battery and assault often occur in quick succession, but assault requires no touching and can occur without
any battery; battery, on the other hand, will often — but not always — involve an assault.

:::examtip
Would the following be assault: pointing a toy gun at someone; texting an ex-partner "I'm gonna get you fixed
up!"; raising a fist to punch someone who cannot see it, behind their back? Check apprehension and imminence
separately — an unseen threat usually fails on apprehension.
:::

## False imprisonment

False imprisonment is the direct total restraint of the liberty of the plaintiff by an intentional or
negligent act of the defendant without lawful justification.

Elements: positive act; direct interference; intentional or negligent act; total restraint of the plaintiff's
liberty without lawful justification.

A plaintiff can be totally imprisoned without any physical boundaries, and may be falsely imprisoned even
without knowing it at the time. Directness includes a defendant who directs, promotes or causes another
person to detain the plaintiff, not only one who detains personally.

:::rule
The deprivation of liberty must be total — there must be no reasonable means of escape. A conditional entry
onto premises, such as being required to pay a further fee before leaving, gives a lawful excuse that defeats
the claim.
:::

:::case
*Balmain New Ferry v Robertson* (1906) 4 CLR 379 — a passenger who missed his ferry was not allowed to leave
the wharf without paying a further penny; the court held there was no false imprisonment because his entry was
conditional, which gave the ferry company a lawful excuse.
:::

An intentional act of imprisonment can still attract liability even where the defendant acted under a
reasonable but mistaken belief that they were acting lawfully.

## Action on the case for intentional interference

In contrast to trespass, an action on the case may lie for malicious prosecution or for intentional (indirect)
interference with the person.

:::definition
Malicious prosecution: procuring, with malice, the prosecution of another person. Elements: the defendant
initiated proceedings against the plaintiff; the proceedings terminated in the plaintiff's favour; the
defendant acted maliciously in initiating them; and the defendant acted without reasonable and probable cause.
Because it is an action on the case, the plaintiff must also show damage to life, liberty, reputation or
property.
:::

:::case
*A v New South Wales* (2007) 230 CLR 500 — a police detective initiated a prosecution he felt pressured into
bringing; the High Court held that malice (a positive element, such as acting for a non-legitimate purpose)
and the absence of reasonable and probable cause (a negative element, tested both subjectively and
objectively) are distinct requirements that must both be satisfied.
:::

Intentional infliction of personal injury covers intentional but indirect infliction of harm, filling the gap
where neither trespass (which needs directness) nor negligence provides an adequate remedy.

:::case
*Wilkinson v Downton* [1897] 2 QB 57 — a practical joke telling the plaintiff her husband had been smashed up
in an accident triggered nervous shock; the court held the cause of action lies in wilfully doing an act
calculated to cause physical harm to the plaintiff, and actually causing such harm.
:::

:::case
*Nationwide News v Naidu* (2007) 71 NSWLR 471 — extreme bullying by a superior at work caused major depression
and post-traumatic stress disorder; the court held an actual subjective intention to harm is not required for
this tort — reckless indifference to the result will suffice.
:::

## Remedies for intentional interference with the person

Damages are the most common remedy; an injunction may also be issued in suitable cases to prevent an
anticipated tort, such as a threatened battery, from taking place.

Damages may be nominal, compensatory and aggravated and/or exemplary. Nominal damages are likely where no harm
or damage was caused. Compensatory damages aim to put the plaintiff in the position they would have been in
had the wrong not occurred. Aggravated damages are awarded for deliberate conduct of the defendant which has
resulted in an affront to the plaintiff. Exemplary damages are awarded to punish the defendant where the
conduct was particularly egregious.

## Interference with chattels: meaning and trespass to goods

Chattels are tangible, moveable, visible goods, transferable by delivery — pets, livestock, motor vehicles,
books, computers, mobile phones and artwork — but not fixtures such as buildings or equipment permanently
attached to land. There is no property in a dead body (subject to rare exceptions), though stored sperm may
be protected as a chattel; whether intangible property such as bitcoin, domain names and digital files should
be protected as chattels remains an open question.

:::rule
Trespass to goods requires a direct interference with a chattel in the possession of the plaintiff at the time
of interference. Since the action is in trespass, no proof of actual damage is generally required.
:::

Possession is different from ownership, and is critical to this tort. Possession has two elements: an
intention to exercise control and exclude others, and actual control.

:::case
*Penfolds Wines Pty Ltd v Elliott* (1946) 74 CLR 204 — Penfolds sued Elliott, who used Penfolds's bottles
(embossed as remaining Penfolds's property, and left with Elliott by his brother) to sell his own wine. The
trespass claim failed because Penfolds was not in possession of the bottles at the relevant time — Elliott
was, as sub-bailee. A bailor generally cannot sue a bailee in trespass, though Dixon J noted an exception
where the bailee has completely destroyed the goods.
:::

:::case
*National Crime Authority v Flack* (1988) 86 FCR 16 — Mrs Flack sued in conversion after the NCA neither
prosecuted nor returned a briefcase of money found on her premises, after holding it for three years; the
court held she had possession in law over all chattels on the premises, even ones she was unaware of, giving
her possessory rights against everyone except the true owner.
:::

## Conversion and detinue

Conversion evolved from the old action of trover and expanded into an action for any wrongful taking, use or
destruction of the plaintiff's goods inconsistent with her possessory rights. It is a tort of strict
liability — good faith or mistake, for example by an auctioneer, is no defence.

:::rule
Conversion differs from trespass to goods in two ways: conduct — conversion requires "dealing" with the goods
(a serious interference amounting to an assertion of dominion contrary to the rights of the person entitled
to possession), not mere interference with their physical state; and title to sue — trespass needs actual
possession at the time of interference, while a right to immediate possession will suffice for conversion.
:::

To sue for conversion, the plaintiff must have had possession, or the right to immediate possession, when the
wrongful act was done, and some form of legal (proprietary) title — merely having a contractual right or a
custody right to possession will not suffice (*Hocking v Director General of the National Archives of
Australia* [2020] HCA 19).

:::case
Continuing *Penfolds Wines Pty Ltd v Elliott* — a majority of the High Court held Elliott's use of Penfolds's
bottles to hold his own wine was conversion, being inconsistent with Penfolds's dominion over the bottles,
even though Penfolds lacked possession; Dixon J dissented on this point, describing the use as "temporary and
harmless" and not serious enough to amount to conversion.
:::

:::case
*Bunnings Group Ltd v CHEP Australia Ltd* (2011) NSWCA 342 — Bunnings used CHEP's timber pallets; the court
held using the pallets to store or display goods delivered on them was not conversion, but using empty
pallets to transport Bunnings's own goods was — showing how fine the "serious interference" judgment can be.
An action in conversion may also be brought where a plaintiff with an immediate right to possession
unconditionally demands the return of a chattel and the defendant wrongfully refuses.
:::

Detinue provides a remedy for the wrongful detention of chattels and, uniquely, lets a court order their
return (though the order is discretionary). The plaintiff needs only a right to immediate possession, plus
proof that she made a demand for the goods — specific, clear and unequivocal — and that the defendant
expressly and wrongfully refused to return them; no actual possession or ownership is required. A refusal made
to verify the plaintiff's entitlement may be reasonable, and so not wrongful.
