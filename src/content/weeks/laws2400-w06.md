## 1. Overview

This week we look at various characteristics which have a bearing on whether a given case falls within one
of the accepted categories of duty of care. We analyse physical harm — including product liability and
sport injuries — and psychiatric harm, and consider the effect of the Civil Liability Act throughout.

## 2. Duty of care and types of harm

Breach of a potential duty of care may result in a variety of harms: physical harm, psychiatric harm, and
pure economic loss. However, not all of these harms are legally recognised in the same way — courts
consider a number of factors to determine whether a case falls within an accepted category of harm or not.

:::rule
Three groups of factors recur. How was the harm caused — by an act or an omission, and should "words" be
treated differently from "acts"? Who is the defendant — a private individual or a public authority, and is
there a special relationship between plaintiff and defendant, such as occupier and entrant, employer and
employee, or manufacturer and consumer? What is the nature of the harm — physical injury, psychiatric harm,
or pure economic loss?
:::

## 3. Physical harm caused by act and omission

Physical harm caused by an act is regarded as the typical case in negligence, but the distinction between
acts and omissions may not always be clear — was the harm caused by an act or an omission in *Donoghue* and
*Grant*? Where the physical harm is caused by an omission, more than reasonable foreseeability is required
to establish a duty of care.

:::case
*Hargrave v Goldman* (1963) 110 CLR 40 — lightning struck a red gum tree on the defendant's Western
Australian farm; the defendant had the tree felled but left the fire to burn itself out; the weather turned,
the fire revived and spread to the plaintiff's neighbouring properties. The relevant factors for a duty
based on an omission were reasonable foreseeability, the occupier-neighbour special relationship, the
defendant's knowledge of the danger, and their control over it.
:::

### Occupiers, obvious risk and the scope of duty

:::case
*Romeo v Conservation Commission (NT)* (1998) 192 CLR 431 — Romeo and a friend had gone to a beach party at
a Reserve managed by the Commission; Romeo fell from a cliff in suburban Darwin and became paraplegic,
arguing that the accident happened because the Commission had failed to fence off the reserve from the
cliff. The High Court, by majority, held that the duty of care did not extend to erecting a fence along the
coastline.
:::

Toohey and Gummow JJ observed that the Commission "was under a general duty to take reasonable steps to
prevent persons entering the Reserve from suffering injury. But the taking of such steps did not extend to
fencing off an area of natural beauty where the presence of a cliff was obvious." The nature of the danger
should be "assessed before the event according to its obviousness and the care ordinarily exercised by the
public" (adopting Brennan J's test from *Nagle*). Kirby J, part of the majority, noted that foreseeability
and proximity were satisfied, but "it is one thing to hold that a person owes a duty of care of some kind to
another. But the critical question is commonly the measure or scope of that duty." *Romeo* matters for three
reasons: duty of care is not owed in the abstract in practice; the question of the scope of the duty borders
between the elements of duty and its breach; and there is a distinction between an obvious and a concealed
risk — there is no duty to warn of an obvious risk.

:::statute
Section 5F CLA defines an "obvious risk" as one that would have been obvious, in the circumstances, to a
reasonable person in the plaintiff's position; obvious risks include those that are patent or a matter of
common knowledge, and a risk can be obvious even though it has a low probability of occurring or is not
prominent, conspicuous or physically observable.
:::

:::statute
Section 5G presumes an injured person was aware of an obvious risk unless they prove, on the balance of
probabilities, that they were not. Section 5H provides that a defendant does not owe a duty to warn of an
obvious risk — unless the plaintiff requested advice about the risk, a written law requires a warning, or
the defendant is a professional and the risk is one of death or personal injury from a professional service.
:::

### No duty to rescue, and duties over the conduct of others

Generally, there is no legal duty to rescue: the High Court held that police did not owe a duty of care to a
person apparently preparing to take his own life (*Stuart v Kirkland-Veenstra* (2009)).

:::statute
Sections 55–58 CLA protect "Good Samaritans". Section 56 defines a good samaritan as a person who, in good
faith and without expectation of payment or other reward, comes to the assistance of a person who is
apparently injured or at risk of being injured. Section 57 removes liability for acts or omissions done in
an emergency when assisting such a person. The protection does not apply if the good samaritan created the
risk, or was under the influence of alcohol or a drug (s 58(1)–(2)).
:::

In certain situations a person may be liable for failing to exercise care over the conduct of others who
cause harm to the plaintiff — for example, parents' failure to control their children's conduct may entail
liability, and the *Dorset Yacht* case also illustrates this. An occupier of land also owes a duty of care to
persons lawfully on the land, including a duty to take reasonable care to protect them from the conduct of
third parties.

:::case
*Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre v Anzil* (2000) 205 CLR 254 — Anzil, an employee of a video shop in the
centre, was attacked by unknown assailants at 10.30pm while walking to his car after the car park lights
(usually left on until 11pm) had gone off on their 10pm timer. The High Court, by majority, held that the
shopping centre's duty of care "as an occupier of land did not extend to taking reasonable care to prevent
physical injury to [Anzil] resulting from the criminal behaviour of third parties on that land."
:::

Gleeson CJ said the basis of an occupier's duty over the state of the premises was "control over, and
knowledge of, the state of the premises"; here, unlike *Dorset Yacht*, the defendant had no control over the
men who attacked Anzil, and neither reliance nor an assumption of responsibility could found a duty —
responsibility for lighting the car park did not extend to a responsibility to protect Anzil from criminals
in it. Gleeson CJ nonetheless observed that a duty to take reasonable steps might arise where criminal
conduct has a high degree of foreseeability and predictability.

## 4. Product liability

The duty of care may arise in relation to the manufacture, distribution and consumption of goods. Until
*Donoghue v Stevenson*, the owner of "dangerous things" had a duty to take reasonable precautions to keep
them from third parties, but no such duty existed for non-dangerous things; after *Donoghue* and *Grant*,
the ordinary principles of negligence became the basis for liability for defective products, alongside
contract, sale of goods, and consumer-protection legislation (the Australian Consumer Law).

:::case
*Graham Barclay Oysters v Ryan* (2002) 211 CLR 540 — consumers became ill with Hepatitis A after eating
oysters grown and distributed by the defendant from a NSW lake; the virus came from faecal contamination
after a storm, and the "depuration" process used only reduced, but did not eliminate, the viral load. The
trial judge found all defendants (the grower, the local council and the NSW government) had breached a duty
of care; the High Court, by majority, held that the NSW government did not owe a duty of care, and that
although the grower did owe a duty, it was not breached.
:::

Gummow and Hayne JJ observed that while a risk of injury to oyster consumers was reasonably foreseeable, it
was not possible "to eliminate entirely the risk of contamination of oysters"; despite the significant
magnitude of the risk, "the degree of probability of its occurrence" did not justify the "difficult,
expensive and inconvenient alleviating action" the consumers argued for.

## 5. Sport and recreation injury

The duty of care question arises frequently in sport and recreational activities, and may be owed to fellow
players, spectators, coaches or administrators.

:::case
*Rootes v Shelton* (1967) 116 CLR 383 — Rootes, an experienced water-skier, was performing a "crossover" on
the Macquarie River when he collided with a stationary boat after being briefly blinded by spray, and the
towing boat's observer had failed to warn skiers of the obstacle. The High Court held that the boat driver
owed Rootes a duty of care and that the duty had been breached.
:::

Barwick CJ: "By engaging in a sport or pastime, the participants may be held to have accepted risks which
are inherent in that sport or pastime … but this does not eliminate all duty of care of the one participant
to the other. Whether or not such a duty arises and, if it does, its extent, must necessarily depend in each
case upon its own circumstances." The key question is distinguishing an inherent risk of the sport from a
separate failure of duty of care, on all the circumstances of the case.

:::case
*Agar v Hyde* (2000) 201 CLR 552 — two rugby players injured in a scrum argued that the International Rugby
Football Board owed them a duty to monitor and change the rules of the game to reduce "unnecessary risk" to
players. The High Court rejected the argument: no such duty was owed.
:::

Gleeson CJ asked: "The game is based on activities … which, by the standards of most members of the
community, are obviously dangerous. … What is an unnecessary risk in an inherently dangerous sport?" The
answer, the Court found, is very fact-specific.

:::statute
Division 5 of Part 1A CLA deals with recreational activities. Section 5L provides that a defendant "is not
liable in negligence for harm suffered by another person … as a result of the materialisation of an obvious
risk of a dangerous recreational activity engaged in by the plaintiff." Section 5M provides that a defendant
"does not owe a duty of care to another person who engages in a recreational activity … to take care in
respect of a risk of the activity if the risk was the subject of a risk warning" to the plaintiff.
:::

## 6. Psychiatric harm

Compensation may now be recovered for psychiatric harm — a harm traditionally labelled "nervous shock" and
not recoverable. It may arise following a physical injury to oneself, after exposure to a situation of
danger creating fear for oneself or a relative, after seeing or hearing a relative killed in an accident, or
after a rescuer witnesses a horrific scene. In cases of pure psychiatric harm, the plaintiff must show it was
reasonably foreseeable that a person in their position would suffer psychiatric harm, and that the injury is
a recognised form of psychiatric harm.

:::case
*Tame v New South Wales; Annetts v Australian Stations Pty Ltd* (2002) 211 CLR 317 — Tame: a police officer
mistakenly recorded Mrs Tame's blood alcohol reading as 0.14 rather than nil; although corrected within
weeks, her solicitor told her of the mistake over a year later and she developed psychotic depression.
Annetts: the Annetts let their 16-year-old son work as a jackaroo only after the employer promised constant
supervision; he was sent to work alone, went missing, and died of dehydration in the desert. The High Court
held the police officer did not owe Mrs Tame a duty of care (her psychiatric injury was not reasonably
foreseeable, and coherence with the officer's other duties told against one), but that Australian Stations
did owe the Annetts a duty of care — the relationship between the parties, coupled with reasonable
foreseeability, gave rise to it, given the employer's broken promise to supervise a 16-year-old.
:::

Gleeson CJ noted that the control mechanisms of "sudden shock" and "direct perception or immediate
aftermath" did not help decide either case. Gummow and Kirby JJ identified three control mechanisms — harm
assessed against a hypothetical person of normal fortitude, a requirement of sudden shock, and a requirement
of direct perception of the event or its immediate aftermath — and held that these are unsound and "not a
pre-condition to liability for negligently inflicted psychiatric harm." They critically examined four
reasons commonly given for treating psychiatric harm differently from physical harm: it is less objectively
observable; litigation over it may disincentivise rehabilitation; it risks indeterminate liability; and it
may impose an unreasonable or disproportionate burden on defendants.

:::examtip
The position at common law in *Tame* has since been modified for NSW cases by CLA ss 30–32 — see below.
:::

### The Civil Liability Act and mental harm

:::statute
Part 3 CLA deals with mental harm. Section 27 defines "mental harm" as "impairment of a person's mental
condition"; "consequential mental harm" is mental harm that is a consequence of a personal injury of any
other kind, and "pure mental harm" is mental harm that is not consequential.
:::

:::statute
Section 30 limits recovery for pure mental harm arising from shock: the plaintiff is not entitled to recover
unless they witnessed, at the scene, the victim being killed, injured or put in peril, or are a close member
of the victim's family (a parent, spouse or partner, child or stepchild, or sibling or step-sibling of the
victim).
:::

:::statute
Section 31 provides there is no liability to pay damages for pure mental harm resulting from negligence
unless the harm consists of a recognised psychiatric illness. Section 32 provides that a defendant does not
owe a duty of care to take care not to cause the plaintiff mental harm unless the defendant ought to have
foreseen that a person of normal fortitude might, in the circumstances, suffer a recognised psychiatric
illness if reasonable care were not taken; the circumstances include whether the harm resulted from a sudden
shock, whether the plaintiff witnessed the victim being killed, injured or put in peril at the scene, the
plaintiff's relationship to the victim, and any pre-existing relationship between plaintiff and defendant.
:::

:::case
*Wicks v State Rail Authority (NSW)* (2010) 241 CLR 60 — two police officers attended the scene of a train
derailment (State Rail admitted negligence) and, after spending several hours assisting the injured,
suffered psychiatric injury. The High Court allowed their appeal and remitted the case, holding that s 32
controls what would be a duty of care at common law and must be considered before the s 30(2) limitation.
:::

Section 32(1) is cast negatively — a duty "is not to be found unless a condition is satisfied" — and
reflects *Tame*: the four factors in s 32(2) "are considerations relevant to foreseeability", not fixed
conditions for a duty. Contrary to *Tame*, though, s 32(1) requires foreseeability from the perspective of a
person of "normal fortitude". On s 30, *Wicks* tells us it would be wrong to look only at what a rescuer
perceived on first arriving at the scene: a series of shocking experiences may be relevant, and not all
cases of death, injury or peril begin and end in an instant — the survivors of a derailment remained in
peril until they had been rescued.

:::examtip
Feminist scholars have critiqued tort law's approach to psychiatric harm: claimants for such harm have often
been women, privileging physical injury over emotional harm is likely to disproportionately affect women,
and the application of the "reasonable person" test is itself problematic.
:::
