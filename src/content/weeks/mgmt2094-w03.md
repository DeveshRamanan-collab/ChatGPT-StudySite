## What design thinking is

In essence, design thinking is a process that harnesses creativity to resolve problems and create solutions with the
intent of making a product, a service, or a business, better. Companies that have used it are often of a similar
mindset: to cultivate a more customer-centric or human-centred culture. It is not just the preserve of start-ups —
IBM, Barclays, Microsoft and Singtel have all ramped up their intake of designers.

:::definition
**Design thinking (Tim Brown, IDEO):** "a system that uses the designer's sensibility and methods to match people's
needs with what is technologically feasible and what a viable business can convert into consumer value and market
opportunity."
:::

- Gerda Gemser (RMIT): "a set of practices that help companies see problems from a new perspective and solve them in
  an atypical way". It is "not suited to a program or desire for incremental improvement" — companies should be
  looking at something radically different, something that hasn't been thought about.
- Guest lecture: design thinking is a **human-centred, creative and experimentation-driven approach to innovation**.
  Human-centred: the customer at the core of the innovation process. Creative: creative thinking skills, and working
  with creators such as designers. Experimentation-driven: how do we quickly and cheaply test our ideas — what is the
  minimum we need to build to find out whether people want it? A mock-up can be enough.
- Its iterative nature lets an organisation work on the issues of the present and the future at the same time,
  explore a range of solutions, open alternative paths, or even redefine the initial problem.
- You do not need to be a designer. Deloitte calls it 'designful thinking': thinking like a designer is a disposition
  and a mindset — human-centric, explorative, asking 'what if' and 'why not?', optimistic, reframing problems as
  opportunities.

## Why organisations use it

- The guest lecture's number one reason: research and experience show that more innovations and start-ups fail from a
  lack of customers than from a failure of the product — people make something that works, but nobody wants it. So
  focus on the customer, consumer or user (depending on the industry), their needs and pain points, and build what
  they want.
- It is a good tool when you don't know what the problem is or what the solution is, and in VUCA conditions —
  volatile, uncertain, complex and ambiguous. The DMI introduction: the VUCA nature of the 21st century is exactly the
  right time for organisations to embrace design thinking, even though some commercial organisations see it as risky.
- The Design Management Institute's Design Value Index: over the last 10 years, design-led companies outperformed the
  S&P by 228 percent.
- The DMI also calls design thinking **non-denominational** (teamwork, visualisation and creative research tools are
  not the sole domain of designers), a way of **aligning process, place and people**, **a means to deliver
  creativity** (problem framing and reframing give design and business a common, customer-centric language), **a
  predictive mechanism** (a 'hedge' against disruptive start-ups like Airbnb, Twitter and Zipcar) and **experiential**
  (it creates solutions from a user-need perspective rather than defining a market and force-fitting solutions).

## Design, meaning and value (Verganti)

Roberto Verganti's foreword: any organisation needs innovation to compete or simply survive, but innovation is not
just an improvement in performance (a faster car, a razor with seven blades). Innovation is useful only when it
creates value for people, and people give value to things that mean something to them.

Firms often assume meaning exists 'out there' and search for a new 'how' to serve an existing purpose better — a
brighter lamp, a faster robot. But people also search for new meanings, new 'whys'. Families buy candles lasting only
30 hours, not to illuminate but to dim the light and perfume a room when welcoming friends; hospitals buy slow robots
like the da Vinci system not to replace doctors but to help them during complex operations. Meaning can be designed
and proposed: design is "making sense (of things)" (Klaus Krippendorff).

:::rule
No design, no meaning, no value, no innovation, no business.
:::

## Desirable, feasible, viable

The sweet spot for innovation is where an idea is **desirable** (people want it), **feasible** (we can make it,
technically and organisationally) and **viable** (we can make money from it — or, for government or a not-for-profit,
it has a big impact for citizens). Brown's definition contains the same three lenses: people's needs, what is
technologically feasible, what a viable business can convert into value.

What happens too often (guest lecture): a team starts with "how cool is the thing we can make" (feasibility), writes a
business case of largely made-up numbers — there is no data yet for an innovation that lies in the future — and
launches. The first time the customer sees it is at launch, after possibly millions of dollars and a lot of time.
Start with desirability.

The lecture's pair (product names from the transcript; see the example card): the Segway, whose inventor feared
copying, was not tested with customers and launched to people who already had a bike, skateboard or scooter; the Dyson
vacuum cleaner started from customers' frustration with a dusty bag and lost suction as it filled, then ran a couple
of thousand experiments to make a solution feasible and viable.

## The design thinking process

The reading's infographic (adapted from Designworks' Luke van O and from Herbert Simon's *The Sciences of the
Artificial*, 1969) runs **Empathy → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test**. Above the stages sit three headings:
**Understand** (over empathy and define — "Understanding ends in insight"), **Create** (over ideate — "Creation ends
in ideas") and **Deliver** (over prototype and test — "Delivery ends in reality").

1. **Be empathetic in defining the problem:** decide what issue you are trying to resolve; agree on who the audience
   is; prioritise the project in terms of urgency; determine what will make the project successful; establish a
   glossary of terms.
2. **Be explorative in researching the problem:** review the history of the issue and remember any existing
   obstacles; collect examples of other attempts to solve it; note the project supporters, investors and critics; talk
   to your end users — this brings you the most fruitful ideas for later design; take thought leaders' opinions into
   account.
3. **Be creative in generating ideas:** identify the needs and motivations of your end users; generate as many ideas
   as possible to serve them; log your brainstorming session; do not judge or debate ideas; have one conversation at a
   time.
4. **Be iterative in refining your ideas:** combine, expand and refine ideas; create multiple drafts; seek feedback
   from a diverse group of people, including your end users; present a selection of ideas to the client; reserve
   judgement and maintain neutrality; create and present actual working prototype(s).
5. **Be rational in choosing an idea:** review the objective; set aside emotion and ownership of ideas; avoid
   consensus thinking; remember the most practical solution isn't always the best; select the powerful ideas.
6. **Be practical in delivering it to the client:** make task descriptions; plan tasks; determine resources; assign
   tasks; execute; deliver.
7. **Be honest in evaluating performance:** gather feedback from the consumer; determine if the solution met its
   goals; discuss what could be improved; measure success and collect data; document.
8. **Repeat.**

The guest lecture's version of the journey (stage names as heard in the transcript): kick off the project, discover
customers' needs, distil them into needs and insights, generate ideas, then build simple prototypes and test them with
customers. It is drawn in a line but runs as a loop: at any stage a team may go back a stage, keep progressing,
**pivot** (change one key element of the product or business model), or kill the idea when time or money runs out and
move on to the next project.

## The four rules of design thinking

In *Design Thinking: Understand, Improve, Apply* (2011, Springer), Christoph Meinel and Larry Leifer write that there
are four rules to design thinking:

1. **The Human Rule: all design activity is ultimately social in nature.** Successful innovation always brings us back
   to the 'human-centric point of view': solve technical problems in ways that satisfy human needs and acknowledge the
   human element in all technologists and managers.
2. **The Ambiguity Rule: design thinkers must preserve ambiguity.** "There is no chance for 'chance discovery' if the
   box is closed tightly, the constraints enumerated excessively and the fear of failure is always at hand."
   Innovation demands experimentation at the limits of our knowledge and control, with freedom to see things
   differently.
3. **The Redesign Rule: all design is redesign.** The human needs we seek to satisfy have been with us for millennia;
   because technology and social circumstances change, understand how these needs have been addressed in the past,
   then apply 'foresight tools and methods' to estimate conditions five, 10 or even 20 years ahead.
4. **The Tangibility Rule: making ideas tangible always facilitates communication.** Conceptual prototyping has been
   central throughout their research, but only in the past few years did they realise that 'prototypes are
   communication media'.

## Empathy and human-centred design

Twenty years ago design shifted from an objective perspective, where designers worked from their own assumptions, to a
human-centred perspective that involves users and puts them at the heart of the process. Empathy is one of the
'mindsets' of human-centred design (HCD), which IDEO is famous for championing. Tim Brown: "Empathy is at the heart of
design. Without understanding what others see, feel and experience, design is a pointless task."

HCD works on the premise that a deep understanding of users' **unarticulated needs** (through empathy) inspires
creativity and innovation: simple human insights reveal needs users may not recognise, so a company can get ahead by
doing what customers haven't asked for. MailChimp treats empathy as a key differentiator — "When everyone can create
very quickly, what is it that will distinguish your product or brand from the rest? Caring for your customers"
(Aarron Walter). Empathetic design observes users in their environments, in the course of everyday habits: closer to
anthropology than to market science. Dorothy Leonard: 'delight the customer' acquires real meaning when products push
beyond what customers anticipate. Henry Ford: "If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster
horses."

:::definition
**Affective empathy:** a shared emotional response, a mirrored response — you see someone sad and you feel sad.
**Cognitive empathy:** perspective taking — stepping into someone else's world and understanding their worldview,
beliefs, fears and experiences.
:::

Temple Grandin, a professor of animal science, noticed that livestock reacted adversely when led in a straight line
and designed curved corrals that reduced stress, panic and injury, making the process more humane and safe. She
observed behaviour from an unaffected standpoint, where others might have assumed that is just what cows do.

**Roadblocks to empathy** (attributed in the reading to Daniel Gilbert): **idealism** — people see things as they
expect them to be; **egoism** — as they want them to be; **realism** — they think they see things as they are, and
blame the scene rather than their own expectations when wrong; **circumstantialism** — they think only about the
things they see, failing to connect information not present at the moment. True empathy means setting aside personal
beliefs, expectations and values, being willing to ask the stupid questions, and seeking further evidence without
judgement.

## How close to the customer?

Gemser describes two streams in design thinking research. IDEO is a proponent of going into the customer's world to
see what the issues are. Roberto Verganti says it is important not to engage with the customer too much, so that you
can see beyond their direct environment: most customers aren't trained to see possibilities in the far future. Steve
Jobs said you shouldn't ask customers what they want — until you show them, they don't know. Gemser's own conclusion:
to succeed, "get out of the ivory tower", engage stakeholders at all levels, and stand in the shoes of the actual
customer.

## Discover and distil (guest lecture)

Names and terms in this section come from the machine transcript of the guest lecture and are not in the reading;
the concept and example cards built on them are marked verify.

- Fall in love with the problem, not the solution. Solutions and technology come and go; good problems stay.
- We can't sit in the office and make up customers' needs: customers don't live in your building, so get out of the
  building. Customers do know what is painful to them and what they like; ask about their lives, not for solutions.
- Pursue multiple lines of inquiry, like a detective: **be the customer** (a team redesigning a fitness class took
  part in the whole experience, from booking to the changing rooms); **be with the customer** (observe, then
  interview — shopping with consumers, looking in their pantry); **learn about them** from people around them
  (partners, hairstylists, nutritionists, personal trainers); **talk to extreme users** — people who use the product a
  lot or not at all. Their experience is heightened, so needs and insights come out more easily. Watching grandparents
  and grandchildren struggle to cut pizza led to a two-handed pizza cutter that mainstream customers bought too.
  Innovation is not polling: you are uncovering needs, not predicting an election.
- Distil with an **empathy map** — what customers say and do, and more importantly what they think and feel, based on
  actual research — and then a **customer narrative**: a descriptive customer, a **need written as a verb** (nouns are
  often solutions) and an **insight** that goes deeper than the reason, to the root cause. Ideas are then brainstormed
  off the need and the insight.
- Stanford's d.school was asked for a low-cost incubator for premature babies in Nepal. The team found the hospitals'
  incubators mostly empty: babies were born in villages, and one premature baby had died on the journey to the city.
  The real need was to keep a premature baby warm without the means to get it to hospital; the answer was a cheap
  warmer, a cross between a survival blanket and a sleeping bag, kept warm by a wax pellet heated in water.
- Insights also feed marketing: the lecture's insight behind Magnum is that indulgent food needs guaranteed
  satisfaction, because if the pleasure falls short of expectation, guilt follows.

## Generating ideas: divergent thinking

:::definition
**Divergent thinking:** "The goal of divergent thinking is to generate many different ideas about a topic in a short
period of time." It involves breaking a topic down into its various component parts to gain insight about its various
aspects, and typically occurs in a spontaneous, free-flowing manner, so ideas are generated in a random, unorganised
fashion.
:::

The Week 3 creativity exercises:

- **Paper clip:** you have 60 seconds — how many uses can you think of for a paper clip?
- **Think in threes:** identify your problem (used in your assignment): A = usual solution, B = different solutions,
  C = radical solutions.
- **Asking new questions:** select a problem; in 2 minutes write all the existing questions you can think of about
  it; then think of at least 5 new and varied questions (no solutions — focus on asking interesting questions).
- **Place 2 unrelated things together** → new opportunity.
- **Who are your future competitors?** A grid of current and future, direct and indirect competitors: the usual answer
  is current and direct; the other boxes are question marks to fill.
- **Speed thinking:** idea ping-pong (how can we raise money while we enjoy ourselves? 30 seconds to develop an idea,
  two groups bouncing off each other's ideas); a mind map at speed (give your problem to a partner for 6 different
  ideas in 2 minutes); 120 seconds for as many ideas as possible (how can we reduce the number of cars on the road?
  how can we increase awareness of the threat of global warming?); 120 seconds on why these ideas will NOT work; and
  nine different perspectives in 120 seconds (how can we reduce the current level of childhood obesity?).

The guest lecture boils the thousands of creative techniques down to three areas (names from the transcript):
**random connections** (Velcro came from burrs stuck to socks and a dog's coat; the class used a banana as inspiration
for a new sports shoe), **best in world** (who else in the world solves this challenge? — roll-on deodorant came from
a giant ballpoint pen; the further from your category, the more innovative the idea is likely to be) and **breaking
the rules** (useful in old industries, as Uber and Airbnb did with taxis and hotels). Brainstorming rules: there is no
such thing as a bad idea, build on others' ideas, and if stuck, think of a really bad idea first.

Then converge: harvest the ideas down to a shortlist and be rational in choosing (step 5). Deloitte describes its own
habit as "Diverging, before converging."

## Prototyping and testing

After ideation the team has been in its own head; it needs to get back to customers quickly to find out whether they
are as excited about the ideas — so it prototypes. The guest lecture's advice: think with your hands — build
something, try something, then talk about it, not the reverse. Designers often solve by doing; design thinking
"probably should be called design doing".

- **Fail early.** Like an Olympic athlete trying different diets and routines in the years before the Games, not at
  the Games, a team should fail early, when failure costs little. Building something costly and then launching it
  means a lot of money spent on something likely to fail.
- **A prototype can be anything:** scrap materials, a mock-up, a storyboard. Early on, the question is not "can we
  make it?" but "if we made it, would it matter to the customer?" — because the number one reason ideas fail is that
  no one wants them.
- **Match the prototype to the uncertainty.** More finished prototypes cost more to build, so don't use them too
  early, when uncertainty is high; as it becomes clearer that people want the idea, build more expensive ones and test
  for feasibility and viability (with tools such as the business model canvas).
- **Cheap experiments** (names from the transcript): Zappos's founder tested whether people would buy shoes online by
  photographing a local shop's shoes, selling them on a one- or two-page website and, when a pair sold, buying it at
  full retail and posting it himself — 'Flintstoning', doing the work manually behind the scenes until the business
  model is proven. He also learned about distribution and warehousing without buying a warehouse.
- **Pivot or kill.** If a test bombs, the idea may be killed, or pivoted: Hermès moved from saddles to other leather
  goods when cars replaced horses; Stage Kings, a Sydney stage builder, turned to work-from-home furniture when
  COVID-19 stopped gatherings.

The reading agrees: create multiple drafts and actual working prototypes, and seek feedback from end users (step 4);
prototypes are communication media (the tangibility rule).

## Case: Deloitte's design thinking transformation

Deloitte CMO David Redhill's opinion piece ('Redesigning the bumblebee') is the reading's main case. Labelled the
'sick puppy' of professional services by BRW in 2004, Deloitte took up design thinking after its leaders met Roger
Martin, Roberto Verganti and Stanford d.school professors Sara Beckman and Michael Barry, who took hundreds of its
leaders through a design thinking program. In 2014 it won an International Design Award — for audit — by recognising
that audits are conducted for real people, and its audit win rate improved. Design thinking played a key role in over
70 percent of the consulting team's major wins in a year; Deloitte Private Connect, a cloud-based accounting platform,
was built on user-centric design thinking principles; the tax practice ran 'Design Inside' workshops.

Design thinking is now one of Deloitte's four lenses (the '4D' factor: design, data analytics, digital strategy and
macroeconomic analysis), wrapping solution development in visual storytelling, divergent and convergent thinking and
persona development. In a decade the firm doubled in size and tripled in revenue. Redhill: genuine curiosity — "the
most powerful, underrated talent in business" — is the foundation of design thinking.

## Where design thinking sits, and what it is worth

- Gemser: it depends who is tasked with innovation — a natural fit with marketers in a market-driven organisation, but
  perhaps not in an engineering-driven one; design should be core to innovation processes and sit where they are
  initiated and developed. Luke van O: marketing is "the easiest door for design thinking to walk through". Redhill:
  logical within marketing, "but if it transcends the marketing function, even better".
- Craig M Vogel's **levels of design**: design strategy (broad goals with social/cultural corporate implications);
  design management (directing designers and interdisciplinary teams); design planning (products, information,
  services); design execution (converting strategy and insights into objects, images and action).
- The reading's debate — 'Design's role in business is unquantifiable' — drew four disagreements: impact can be
  measured by comparing alternatives through prototypes and market trials (Doug Nash), through click-through rates,
  open rates and visits (Ted Rubin), and through sales of classic designs year on year (Timm McVaigh); design is the
  visual projection of what you stand for (Geoff Brooks).
- Simon Stacey (Designworks): great customer experiences combine **relevance** (user-focused), **purpose** (they work
  brilliantly) and **joy** (they connect emotionally) — Amazon's Taco Truck and mall kiosks, and Warby Parker's home
  try-on of five frames, low prices and 'buy one, give one'.
