## Different types of innovation

Industrial innovation doesn't include only major (radical) innovation, but also minor (incremental) technological
advances. The lecturer stresses that the incremental advances happen far more frequently than the development of a
completely new product (a new smartphone model with a better camera simply improves on an existing product), and that
"technological" is not only about technology: you can innovate how you do business as well as your product.

- **Product innovation**: the development of a new or improved product, eg the Braille Rubik's cube, so that people
  with vision impairment can also use the puzzle.
- **Process innovation**: the development of a new process, such as a manufacturing process, or online recruiting in
  HR. It mostly happens internally, so it is less noticeable, but it can be very substantial for the organisation.
- **Organisational innovation**: a new venture division; a new accounting procedure.
- **Management innovation**: TQM (Total Quality Management).
- **Production innovation**: quality circles; JIT (just in time).
- **Commercial/marketing innovation**: guerrilla marketing campaigns.
- **Service innovation**: internet-based financial services — how you serve your customer, easing the way the
  customer uses your services.

:::definition
**Radical** innovation is a major advance, such as a completely new product; **incremental** innovation is a minor
advance that improves on what already exists. Both count as innovation, and the incremental kind is far more common.
:::

## Views of innovation

Popular views of innovation are mainly seen through Hollywood and Disney movies such as *Back to the Future* (see the
link in iLearn, Week 11). Popular culture shapes the way we think about innovation, and it goes both ways: how society
innovates influences writers and directors, whose films then influence how we think about innovation.

- **The social deterministic school** argues that innovations are the result of a combination of external social
  factors and influences, eg demographic changes and cultural changes — thus when conditions are right, innovation will
  occur, eg Covid and the increase of online offerings (teaching, work and services all moved online).
- **The individualistic school** argues that innovations are the result of unique individual talents, and as such
  innovators are born ("if you have that gene of innovation, then you will innovate"). This is linked to serendipity.
- **Resource-based innovation**: a firm's own resources provide a context to develop innovative activity, eg Starbucks
  has innovated the style and type of coffee-based drinks being offered.
- **Market-based innovation**: market conditions provide the context which facilitates or constrains the innovative
  activity, so the firm must recognise the opportunities that arise in the marketplace, eg the Share a Coke campaign
  (made each person feel special and unique).

## Models of innovation

**Serendipity**: unexpected discovery, or could be seen as luck. In order to recognise the significance of your
innovation, you would at first require previous knowledge in the area; discoveries may not be expected. "Chance
favours the prepared mind" (Louis Pasteur). The lecturer's example is the discovery of penicillin, made largely by
chance by a scientist who was not looking for an antibiotic.

**Linear models**: the linkage between key components — science and technology based — to technological
developments, to the needs of the market, which will give you innovation.

**Simultaneous coupling model**: the coupling of all knowledge within the key components above; the point where
innovation begins is not known. Bring the different streams of knowledge, technology and market opportunity together
and something innovative comes up, but we can't say which component was responsible.

### Example of a linear model (Fig 6.3, Linear Model of Innovation)

1. **Idea generation** → Gate 1: **project charter**.
2. **Concept feasibility** — financial and technical feasibility: can we afford it, is it technically possible, can we
   secure enough financing? → Gate 2: **business plan**.
3. **Capability development** — prototype development: test the ideas and play around with them → Gate 3: **launch and
   proposal**.
4. **Ramp up** — test marketing: focus groups and market research to see how much we can produce and sell → Gate 4:
   **sanity check** — feedback from whatever parties we can find, then corrective measures.
5. **Launch** — commercialisation: the product goes to market.

This is a depiction of the process: the lecturer says it is not necessary to follow it exactly to the letter.

## Closed and open models for innovation

- **Closed model**: the steps go consecutively — project start, concept development, concept frozen, then
  implementation, then market introduction.
- **Open model**: the project starts and the concept is developed, but implementation starts somewhere in the middle of
  concept development, before the concept is frozen. As you start implementing you do a "trial by fire": you see the
  response of the market and of your users, continue to develop the concept, and keep tweaking the product or service
  as implementation continues to market introduction.

## Architectural and modular innovation

We can divide innovation into architectural and modular.

- **Architectural innovation**: change in product structure — the mobile phone becomes the smartphone. It departs quite
  a bit from what came before, but it grows out of the legacy product: essentially the same product, a phone, with a lot
  more capabilities.
- **Modular innovation**: change in component technology — the steam engine of a car becomes a fuel engine. It is the
  same product, but an important component changes.

## Example of innovation: Yellow Tail takes on the US wine industry

### Before Yellow Tail

The lecture describes Yellow Tail as a budget wine trying to enter the US market: huge, but saturated with wines
produced in the United States and with wines from the old wineries of Europe. Nobody knows you there, so you have to
differentiate yourself one way or the other.

The strategy canvas compares premium and budget wines on the factors the industry competes on: price, (enological)
terminology, marketing, ageing, vineyard (prestige), wine complexity and range. Premium wines sit high on every factor;
budget wines sit well below them, lowest on price.

### What did Yellow Tail do?

"To fundamentally shift the strategy canvas of an industry you must begin by reorienting your strategic focus from
competitors to alternatives and from customers to non customers" (Kim & Mauborgne). Yellow Tail had to think about its
competitors, but also about people who generally are not wine buyers, and how to attract those new customers.

:::rule
**The four action framework questions** (Blue Oceans): Which of the factors that an industry takes for granted should
be **eliminated**? Which of the factors should be **reduced** well below the industry's standards? Which factors should
be **raised** well above the industry's standard? Which factors should be **created** that the industry has never
offered?
:::

| Eliminate | Reduce | Raise | Create |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Enological terminology | Wine complexity | Price versus budget wine | Easy drinking |
| Ageing qualities | Wine range | Retail store involvement | Ease of selection |
| Above-the-line marketing | Vineyard prestige | | Fun and adventure |

Yellow Tail entered the market with a curve unlike both premium and budget wines: price raised above budget wine (but
not to premium level), terminology, marketing and ageing cut right down, vineyard prestige, complexity and range low,
and three new factors (easy drinking, ease of selection, fun and adventure) high. Rather than marketing directly, it
secured contracts with prominent US retailers and relied on marketing through them; by repositioning its wine it almost
created a new category.

### What did Yellow Tail learn? (Blue Oceans Strategy: key lessons)

- Understanding the consumers' needs is key to innovation.
- Challenge existing business models: just because they represent the historic norm doesn't mean that they are right.
- Focus is more important than ticking all the boxes.
- Look across industries and look across time to understand how industries can and will evolve.

## When we innovate we need to consider all these (business modelling)

Key partners, key activities, key resources, value proposition, customer relationships, channels, customers, costs and
revenue. Yellow Tail thought all of these through.

## Value innovation

:::definition
**Value innovation (VI)** is one of the most prominent programmatic approaches to innovation currently in use. It is
different from building layers of competitive advantage, and does not mean segmenting the market and accommodating
customer needs. Its main focus is on offering those products and services which create a superior buyer value in
existing markets and enable a quantum leap for the firm to create new markets.
:::

Value innovation also differs from technology innovation: new technology developed does not become a value innovation
unless it is cheap enough for mass buyers.

| Three basic building blocks of strategy | Conventional focus | Value innovation process |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Competition | Outperforming the competition | Seeking radically superior value to capture the entire mass market |
| Customers | Retaining and better satisfying existing customers | Targeting the mass of buyers by following non-customers closely and willingly losing some existing customers |
| Corporate capabilities | Leveraging and extending the current capabilities | Willing to combine a company's capabilities |

Yellow Tail is an example of value innovation: it is the same product — just wine — in a market that already had
budget and premium wines at every price, but it repositioned its product and created value for the customer by
rethinking what wine and wine consumption are, and found its niche.

## Which innovation model is right for your company? (BCG reading)

Innovation is a leading priority for CEOs (more than 70% list it in their top three areas of focus), yet only 16% of
companies BCG surveyed believe they are better innovators than their peers. Innovators typically fall short for one of
two reasons: they pursue the wrong innovation model for their business and competitive context, or they don't support
a good model with the capabilities it requires. BCG studied more than 100 of the world's most innovative companies and
found six distinct innovation models.

- **Creator — "We know best."** Typically led by a strong, bold, visionary leader; creators disrupt their core markets,
  protect their intellectual property and make highly focused big bets. Characteristics: a closed innovation approach,
  deliberate life cycle management, a limited number of big bets. Apple is the classic example; also Tesla, Netflix,
  Lululemon Athletica.
- **Solution builder — "We're listening."** Look to the market for inspiration, drawing on observation and deep
  insight to address customer priorities and problems. Characteristics: excellence in customer insight, a focus on the
  full purchase experience, centralised governance to keep the customer at the centre of innovation. Nike combines
  customer insight with cutting-edge design and technology (shoe-based sensors linked to web platforms that give
  personalised feedback); also Starbucks, GoPro, Target, Under Armour.
- **Leverager — "We do it best."** Create a superior business model and capitalise on it to sustain industry
  leadership. Characteristics: concentration on innovations that can speed the expansion of the model, continuous
  improvement of the core model, transferring expertise and staff continuity. Zara's breakthrough design,
  manufacturing and distribution processes shorten the time it takes new items to reach stores; also Toyota, Costco.
- **Expander — "Seeking share to steal."** Apply core capabilities in new ways to take over adjacent markets.
  Characteristics: a mandate for growth, a focus on one or two advantaged capabilities, consistent in-market
  experimentation, an open, empowering innovation culture. Gilead keeps entering new disease categories (acquiring
  Pharmasset in 2011 gave it two best-in-class hepatitis C treatments); also Amazon, Danaher.
- **Defender — "Protecting our turf."** Win in mature or slow-changing industries and innovate defensively to protect
  their advantage. Characteristics: continuous improvement to maintain the edge of current offerings, rigorous
  defensive monitoring of potential disruptions, M&A and partnerships to keep pace. As technology transforms more
  industries, this model becomes increasingly risky. Allstate Insurance identified the shift to online and app-based
  products and acquired the pioneer Esurance; also AIG, Pfizer.
- **Fast follower — "Ready to react."** Optimise capabilities across all dimensions to respond quickly to — and often
  improve upon — competitive innovations. Characteristics: sensing and speed to improve on competitive innovations and
  lower risk, investment in go-to-market excellence, a flat organisation for rapid decisions. Reckitt Benckiser invests
  downstream in product testing, with minimal energy up front on consumer insight and ideation; also Tencent, JPMorgan
  Chase.

### Context is critical

Industry context matters because only a subset of models can succeed in most industries. In consumer retail, four
models drive shareholder-return premiums: creators (Lululemon's distinctive lifestyle brand built on the yoga
movement), solution builders (Target's "cheap but chic" offer for young, trendy shoppers), leveragers (Costco's
everyday low prices, lean supplier network and members-only approach) and expanders (Amazon carrying its data
analytics, logistics and customer service into fashion, luxury apparel and, with Whole Foods, grocery). Companies
struggle when they pursue a model their industry doesn't reward: Sears used the defender model, counting on its brand
and stores, and lost its edge when agile online players upended retail.

The company's own context matters too: how important innovation is to its strategy, its competitive position, and the
capabilities and advantages that set it apart. Companies in the same industry can succeed with different models, but
the model must align with strategy, strengths and capabilities: the expander model suits Amazon better than Costco's
leverager model because it reaches a much broader pool of consumers and drives faster top-line growth.

Questions that reveal your context: is innovation a growth engine or a defensive tool? How strong and durable is your
competitive position? How important is brand? How robust are your innovation capabilities? How much can you invest?
And, most important, how quickly does your sector change? Look for a model competitors aren't using at all or are using
poorly, weigh the investment in dollars, time and upgraded capabilities, and filter the options through your ambition
and resources.

### Making it work

Migrating to a new model, or aligning capabilities with an existing one, is the hardest part. An innovation blueprint
lays out the pieces that must align with the model: organisational structure and culture; tools and processes for idea
generation, commercialisation and portfolio management; and metrics and incentives. Reckitt Benckiser's flat structure
maximises speed to market; Under Armour invests in advanced analytics to understand its fitness community; Amazon's
expander model would not work without a high tolerance for risk, reflected in its metrics and incentives.

## Guest lecture: Mark, founder of a furniture business

The Week 11 recording is a guest lecture by Mark, who has run his own furniture design and manufacturing business for
more than 30 years.

- **Why he started.** He wanted to create tangible things he could stand back and look at, and to run an
  environmentally friendly business: everything was made of recycled timber. He now also uses new timber, sourced
  sustainably, because customers wanted straight grain without bolt holes, and finding it in recycled
  timber became too arduous. He also needed to make a living: of all the things he likes, he makes the ones he knows he
  can make money out of.
- **How he started.** Very little money but a fair bit of knowledge: an earlier business designing and making men's
  clothing, then about three years as PA to the owner of a substantial recycled-timber furniture business ("my
  university was having worked in the industry"). He started alone in a double garage with an old truck, collecting
  and restoring furniture. His first sale: a table bought for $200, done up over a weekend, sold for $800 to a passer-by
  off the back of his truck; that night he bought three more tables at auction. The host called it a serendipitous
  moment; Mark's own theory is that business is like surfing — you can't sit in one spot, you paddle around and keep
  your eyes open for opportunities.
- **How it grew.** Organically, to 50 staff and four retail shops; he then consolidated into one factory and showroom
  under one roof and now has about 25 staff. "Sticking to your knitting": make your core business really successful
  before you branch out. He makes marketable, quality, timeless pieces rather than constant new ranges, was featured in
  design magazines for years without any PR, has about 80,000 organic Instagram followers (staff run social media) and
  has licensed his brand to big companies.
- **His advice.** Marketing is seeing a hole or an opportunity in the market and filling it with what people want, at a
  price they will pay. Remain flexible: companies plan to go from A to B and end up at C, because things change. Keep a
  diary and records, always do what you say, write lists, learn from people who are already successful, make mistakes
  but don't repeat them, invest early in the right equipment (money is a tool), and go into every deal making sure it
  is a win–win.

Mark is the week's real-world example: market-based innovation (a hole in the market), resource-based innovation (his
trade knowledge, truck and equipment), serendipity meeting a prepared mind (the first sale and the surfing theory), the
open model's adjust-as-you-go habit, and choosing an approach that fits your strengths ("stick to your knitting").
:::

## Reflective writing (for the video project reflection)

The Week 11 slides close with reflective writing, covered fully in Week 6. Reflection is "a form of mental processing
with a purpose" (Moon 1999): like Dumbledore's Pensieve, it lets you siphon off excess thoughts and spot patterns and
links. Relate your reflection to your group experiences, the theory behind your innovative project, and how it affected
the type and outcome of the project. You may use "I", but keep a professional, academic tone, support feelings with
specific evidence, reveal information wisely, and don't drag anyone else down. Ask what you learnt, what insights you
gained, what challenges you and the team met, and what you would do differently next time. Do not just describe: you
must analyse and reflect.
