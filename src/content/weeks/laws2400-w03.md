## 1. Overview

This week we focus on torts that protect various interests in land: trespass to land, public nuisance
(this lecture) and private nuisance (lecture 2), plus the remedies and defences available for each.

## 2. Trespass to land

Trespass entails direct interference with land in the possession of the plaintiff. The key elements are:

- A direct and positive act (interference with land)
- Intentional or negligent
- In the possession of the plaintiff
- Without consent or other lawful justification

:::rule
Trespass to land is actionable per se — without proof of damage.
:::

Except in highway cases, the plaintiff is not required to prove fault: the onus is on the defendant to
disprove fault. Mistake is no defence as long as there was an intentional or negligent trespassory act.
There should be no express or implied consent to the entry, and no other lawful excuse (eg exercise of a
lawful duty).

Real-life examples: parking a car on another person's land, spraying graffiti on a building, gatecrashing a
private party, dumping old furniture on someone else's property.

### What is land?

Land includes the soil and fixtures such as a house, factory or crops (prior to harvesting), and the
airspace above the land.

:::case
*Bernstein v Skyviews & General Ltd* [1977] 2 All ER 902 — taking photographs from an aircraft several
hundred feet above the ground was not trespass: an owner's rights in the airspace extend only to the
height necessary for the ordinary use and enjoyment of the land.
:::

By contrast, in *Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco Co* [1957] 2 All ER 343 an advertising sign placed 24 cm into
the airspace above the plaintiff's property constituted trespass, and in *LJP Investments v Howard Chia
Investments* (1989) 24 NSWLR 490 encroaching scaffolding constituted a trespass. What about drones, or
minerals?

### Exclusive possession

Exclusive possession of the land at the time of the trespass is required to bring an action — not
ownership. A tenant can therefore sue for trespass to land. Possession has two elements: factual control,
and an intention to exclude all others. Ownership can assist in proving possession, provided conduct
indicates control amounting to factual possession; if the plaintiff is not the owner, they must show a
sufficient degree of physical control and an intention to exclude others.

:::case
*Newington v Windeyer* (1985) 3 NSWLR 555 — the plaintiff occupied a vacant area of land adjoining her
property for 50 years. She could maintain an action in trespass despite not being the registered owner:
she had exclusive possession, shown by hosting parties, maintaining the garden and trees, and paying
rates.
:::

### Trespassory conduct

Entry upon the land without consent is trespassory, but there are several other possibilities: driving
onto the land; remaining on the land after permission to remain has been withdrawn; throwing any object or
animal upon the land; and encroachment by scaffolding, cranes or structures.

### Implied licence

A path or driveway to the front door carries an implied licence for any member of the public on lawful
business to approach and knock, unless negated by a locked gate or sign.

:::case
*Halliday v Nevill* (1984) 155 CLR 1 — a police officer had an implied licence to enter an open driveway
when he arrested a disqualified driver.
:::

The implied licence does not extend to entry for the purpose of administering a breath test to the
occupier (*Munnings v Barrett* (1987) 5 MVR 403), nor for service of a summons (*Plenty v Dillon* (1991)
171 CLR 635).

In *Roy v O'Neill* (2020) 273 CLR 91; [2020] HCA 45 the High Court considered whether police officers had
an implied licence to be at the front door if their purpose was to investigate a crime or check on
welfare (here, domestic violence). It held there was no trespass: the implied licence to enter as far as
the front door included the purpose of checking on the welfare of an occupant, and is not negated merely
because the entrant also has an additional purpose in mind.

Also see *Lincoln Hunt Aust v Willesee* (1986) 4 NSWLR 457 (entry onto commercial premises with television
cameras, not for the purpose of bona fide seeking information or business with the occupier, constitutes
trespass), and *Barker v The Queen* (1983) 153 CLR 338 (a person authorised to keep an eye on a house
while the occupier was away, and told where the key was, was a trespasser when he entered to steal).

## 3. Remedies for trespass to land

One may seek: damages (nominal, compensatory, aggravated and exemplary); injunctions (prohibitory or
mandatory); and self-help (eg physical removal of a trespasser using reasonable force).

:::rule
The general rule for damages is to put the injured party in the same position as she would have been if no
wrong was committed. Damages are generally assessed as the diminution in the value of the property; in
suitable cases the higher cost of reinstatement may be ordered. It is also possible to recover for
consequential injury or damage — eg if the trespass resulted in a psychiatric injury.
:::

## 4. Nuisance

Nuisance involves unreasonable interference with the plaintiff's use or enjoyment of land. It differs from
trespass to land because in nuisance the interference is with the land's use or enjoyment, and need not be
direct or physical. Due to its wide ambit, a variety of situations may amount to nuisance: smell, noise,
pollution of water or air, water overflow, loud music, the presence of a brothel near residences, damage
by tree roots.

Nuisance is of two types: private nuisance (a tort action) and public nuisance (a common law crime, but
also a tort action if special or particular damage has been suffered).

### Public nuisance

:::definition
Public nuisance is an unlawful act or omission which (i) endangers the lives, safety, property or comfort
of the public, or (ii) obstructs the enjoyment of any right common to all.
:::

To initiate a case for public nuisance there should be: title to sue (special/particular damage);
interference with a public right; and interference that is substantial and unreasonable.

:::case
*Walsh v Ervin* [1952] VLR 361 — a farmer ploughed and planted crops on the highway and erected gates
preventing the plaintiff entering his land by motor vehicle. The court held that special or particular
damage is not limited to actual pecuniary loss: delay and inconvenience of a substantial character,
dissimilar to that experienced by the rest of the public, may amount to special or particular damage.
:::

There should be interference with a public right — the general public should be impacted — because of
which addressing public nuisance becomes the responsibility of the community (the state should act
generally). Not every annoyance, discomfort or inconvenience amounts to public nuisance: the interference
must be substantial and unreasonable (eg a merely temporary obstruction of a highway will not do).

### Private nuisance

Private nuisance is an action on the case. Unlike trespass to land, it protects both tangible (eg fire,
water, dust) and intangible (eg noise, smell) interferences with a person's right to use and enjoy her
land — but not every interference is actionable.

:::rule
To initiate an action for private nuisance, one should establish: the plaintiff has title to sue; the
nuisance caused a substantial and unreasonable interference; and the defendant created, authorised or
continued a state of affairs giving rise to the harm.
:::

**Title to sue.** The traditional position is that only a person with proprietary rights in the land has
standing to sue — the owner or tenant with actual possession. A licensee, allowed onto land by permission
only (eg a daughter of the tenant, a guest, or a short-term Airbnb occupant), has no title to sue.

:::case
*Oldham v Lawson (No 1)* [1976] VR 654 — a wife and husband sued their neighbours for nuisance from
persistent noise. The wife had legal rights to the land and could sue; the husband was a licensee only, so
had no title to sue.
:::

Courts have not always been consistent: in *Khorasandjian v Bush* (1993), a UK decision, a licensee
daughter had title to sue for nuisance of prank calls, and *Deasy Investments Pty Ltd v Monreast* (1996), a
Queensland decision, followed Bush. But *Hunter v Canary Wharf Ltd* (1997), a UK decision, overruled Bush:
title to sue requires a proprietary interest.

**Substantial and unreasonable interference.** Some allowance must be made for the reasonable action of
neighbours; courts strike a balance between the competing rights of two people using and enjoying their
respective lands, considering factors such as frequency, duration, time, locality and distance from the
alleged nuisance, on a case-by-case basis.

:::case
*Munro v Southern Dairies* [1955] VLR 332 — noise, smell and flies from horses kept for a milk
distribution business. The test is not one of "abnormal sensitiveness" — it is an objective test, the
standards of an ordinary person in the claimant's position. On the facts, keeping the horses caused a
nuisance.
:::

:::case
*Victoria Park Racing and Recreation Grounds Co Ltd v Taylor* (1937) 58 CLR 479 — a broadcaster on
neighbouring land gave live radio descriptions of races from a platform overlooking the plaintiff's
racecourse, causing paying patrons to stay away. Held (majority): no actionable nuisance. Private nuisance
protects an occupier's use and enjoyment of land; the broadcasting only diverted custom through lawful
competition. "Overlooking" is not a nuisance — the natural rights of an occupier do not include freedom
from being overlooked. But every factual scenario is unique: what if the overlooking is akin to harassment
or is hostile, or some other factor is present?
:::

The motive or malice of the defendant may also be relevant to whether the interference was substantial and
unreasonable.

:::case
*Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett* [1936] 2 KB 468 — the defendant deliberately shot guns on his own
land to disturb the plaintiff's breeding foxes.
:::

**Created, authorised or continued.** A wide range of people may be sued for nuisance, not merely its
creator. The creator may be liable even if not the occupier at the relevant time (eg a trespasser on the
land), and is strictly liable in the sense that even reasonable steps to mitigate the interference will not
avoid liability. An occupier who authorises others to undertake activities constituting a nuisance (eg
leasing land knowing it will be used as a lime quarry that will cause nuisance) can also be liable. A
person who does not create but *continues* a nuisance can also be held liable:

:::rule
"If the defendant did not create the nuisance, he must, if he is to be held responsible, have continued
it, which I think means simply neglected to remedy it when he became, or should have become, aware of
it." — Lord Wright
:::

### Defences for nuisance

Several defences may be pleaded: a prescriptive right to continue the interference complained of (must
have continued for more than 20 years); the plaintiff's conduct or consent (the most pleaded defence —
though it is no defence to say the plaintiff "came to the nuisance"); statutory authorisation (if the
authorised activity could not have been performed without committing the nuisance); and contributory
negligence.

### Remedies for nuisance

Three remedies are available: damages, injunction and abatement (self-help). Damages may be awarded for
damage to the land, financial loss (eg loss of trade, decline in property value) and loss of subjective
experience — the harm claimed must be reasonably foreseeable. An injunction is normally granted where
damages will not be an adequate remedy, and the chance of recurrence is a further factor. Abatement is an
alternative to legal proceedings, but the plaintiff should be cautious in using it and should only employ
reasonable methods.

:::examtip
Start every land-tort problem by asking: is the interference direct (trespass) or does it merely affect
use/enjoyment (nuisance)? Then work through the relevant checklist — trespass's four elements, or
nuisance's title-to-sue / substantial-and-unreasonable / created-authorised-or-continued elements — before
reaching for a defence or remedy.
:::

## 5. Trespass to land: the textbook flowchart

The textbook flowchart (Tort Law Principles 3e, p 142) sets trespass to land out as a definition followed by general
principles, which work as a checklist in order.

:::definition
Trespass to land involves the direct, intentional or negligent invasion or interference with land in possession of the
plaintiff without lawful authority, justification or excuse: *Barker v The Queen*; *Roy v O'Neill*.
:::

- **Exclusive possession.** Often referred to as "title to sue": the plaintiff must be in possession of the land at the time the interference is said to have taken place. It typically arises by title (where the title holder is in actual possession), by consent (where the plaintiff is in possession under a lease) or by adverse possession (without title or consent). Licences typically will not confer a right of exclusive possession. Where a tenant does not vacate a property at the end of a lease, the owner will need to retake possession in order to have title to sue in trespass.
- **Of land.** Can extend to invasion or other direct interference with possession of airspace, fixtures or subsoil.
- **Direct invasion or interference.** Can include exceeding the terms of a licence, or going beyond the expiry or revocation of a licence. Indirect interference may constitute another tort, such as private nuisance.
- **Fault.** The act constituting the trespassory conduct must be intentional or negligent. The defendant need not intend to trespass, and mistake is generally not a defence. Involuntary conduct (where the defendant is utterly without fault) is not actionable.
- **Without lawful authority, justification or excuse.** The flowchart refers this box to a later textbook chapter that is not in the week's materials. In *Roy v O'Neill*, Keane and Edelman JJ said a person who enters the land of another must justify that entry by lawful authority, including consent or licence; the lecture's examples are express or implied consent and other lawful excuse, such as the exercise of a lawful duty.
- **Remedies.** No requirement for damage: trespass is actionable per se. However, substantive damages are available for actual losses where they are the natural and probable consequences of the trespass; alternatively, damages may be made on the basis of the "user fee" principle. Injunctive relief may, at a court's discretion, be granted to prevent a continuing or threatened trespass where damages would be an inadequate remedy.

:::examtip
Run the flowchart's boxes in order on a trespass problem — possession, land, directness, fault, lawful authority — and
stop at the first one the facts fail. If the interference was indirect, move to private nuisance instead.
:::
