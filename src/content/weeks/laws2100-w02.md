## Law and coercion

The readings: John Austin, *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832), Lectures I and VI; H.L.A. Hart, *The
Concept of Law* (1961), Chapter II ('Laws, Commands, and Orders'), Chapter III ('The Variety of Laws') and Chapter IV
('Sovereign and Subject'). The lecture asks you to read Chapter IV closely, and Fred Schauer's article on Austin and
Hart; the lecture gives the context from Chapters II and III, and the three chapters should be read together.

- Can law exist without coercion and/or sanctions?
- Is coercion an essential feature of law?
- Is coercion a necessary feature of law?
- What is this week's problem?

From a philosophical point of view there is a difference between an essential feature and a necessary feature of
law. For our discussion it is not too important, but you will meet the distinction in the literature, and Hart in
*The Concept of Law* moves fairly quickly between the two.

## Hart on law and coercion

Hart's three questions from Chapter 1: (1) How does law differ from and how is it related to orders backed by
threats? (2) How does legal obligation differ from, and how is it related to, moral obligation? (3) What are rules
and to what extent is law an affair of rules? This week is the first.

:::definition
This week, we are analysing the claim that "…the key to the understanding of law is to be found in the simple notion
of an order backed by threats, which Austin himself termed a 'command'." (CL 16) In the literature Austin's theory is
called the **imperative theory** or the **command theory** of law, and is often grouped with Jeremy Bentham's.
:::

## Preface to The Concept of Law

"I am heavily and obviously indebted to other writers; indeed much of the book is concerned with the deficiencies of
a simple model of a legal system, constructed along the lines of Austin's imperative theory. But in the text the
reader will find very few references to other writers and very few footnotes. […] I hope that this arrangement may
discourage the belief that a book on legal theory is primarily a book from which one learns what other books
contain." (CL vii)

Note that Hart says a simple model of a *legal system*: explaining law and explaining legal systems are not
necessarily the same thing, and Hart has been criticised for making this move. We do not read books to learn what
other books contain; we use them to think hard about the problems behind them.

## Commands, habits, and the essence of law

"To some it has seemed clear that in this situation where one person gives another an order backed by threats, […]
we have the essence of law, or at least 'the key to the science of jurisprudence'. This is the starting-point of
Austin's analysis by which so much English jurisprudence has been influenced." (CL 6–7)

"The clearest and the most thorough attempt to analyse the concept of law in terms of the apparently simple elements
of commands and habits, was that made by Austin in the Province of Jurisprudence Determined." (CL 18)

Remember last week's distinction: 'the essence of law' is a claim about the nature of law; 'the key to the science of
jurisprudence' is a claim about the nature of jurisprudence.

## John Austin (1790–1859)

Called to the Bar (1818); married Sarah Taylor, who sustained him financially and emotionally and without whose
contribution the 2nd edition and the *Lectures* would not exist; Chair of Jurisprudence and the Law of Nations,
University of London (1826–1835); Bonn, Germany (1827–1828), where reflecting on a civilian system and the reception
of Roman law coloured his general theory; *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832; 2nd edition 1861);
*Lectures on Jurisprudence, or the Philosophy of Positive Law* (1863).

## Jurisprudence and positive law

Law = "a rule laid down for the guidance of an intelligent being by an intelligent being having power over him" (PJD
18). According to Austin, there are four kinds of laws:

1. Divine law (Austin rejects the notion of Law of Nature)
2. Human laws — laws set by political superiors (positive law) and laws set by men not political superiors
3. Objects improperly but by close analogy termed laws — rules set and enforced by mere opinion, including
   international law (positive morality)
4. Objects metaphorically termed laws (eg the laws determining the movements of inanimate bodies or masses)

:::rule
"The matter of jurisprudence is positive law: law, simply and strictly so called: or law set by political superiors to
political inferiors." (PJD 18)
:::

## Lecture I: commands

- Laws or rules properly so called are a species of commands.
- A command has two elements: (i) a signification of desire; and (ii) the party to whom the command is directed is
  liable to evil in case they do not comply with the expressed desire.
- Command and duty are correlative terms: wherever a duty lies, a command has been signified; and wherever a command
  is signified, a duty is imposed.
- Evil = sanction, or an enforcement of obedience (for Austin, punishments are a class of sanctions).

:::definition
Three ideas are captured by the term **command**: a wish/desire conceived by a rational being that another rational
being shall do or forbear; an evil to proceed from the former, and to be incurred by the latter, in case the latter
does not comply with the wish; an expression/intimation of the wish by words or other signs. For Austin, command,
duty and sanction are inseparably connected terms/concepts.
:::

## A law or rule properly so called

- A law is a command which obliges a person or persons, and obliges generally to acts or forbearances of a class.
- Laws (and other commands) proceed from superiors and bind/oblige inferiors.
- Austin understands superiority to mean might: the power to affect others with evil or pain, and of forcing them
  through fear of that evil to fashion their conduct to one's wishes.
- Whoever can oblige another to comply with their wishes is the superior. Superiority is the power to enforce
  compliance with a wish.

## Lecture VI: sovereignty

How can we distinguish positive laws from other kinds of commands? "Every positive law is set by a sovereign person,
or a sovereign body of persons, to a member or members of the independent political society wherein that person or
body is sovereign or supreme" (PJD 165). Three inseparably connected ideas: sovereignty, subjection, independent
political society.

:::definition
A society is **political** iff the generality of its members is in a habit of obedience to a determinate and common
superior. It is **political and independent** iff that superior does not habitually obey another person or body. A
society is in a state of nature, or split into two or more independent political societies, unless habitual obedience
is "…rendered by the bulk of its members […] to one and the same superior…" (PJD 169).
:::

The definition does not need territory or the other concepts associated with the modern state: think of a city
state, a large empire, an absolute monarch, or a council of elders. The key idea is the power relation between the
common superior and the rest of the community; you can apply it to a constitutional democracy just as well.

## Austin's legal positivism

"The existence of law is one thing; its merit or demerit is another. Whether it be or be not is one enquiry; whether
it be or be not conformable to an assumed standard, is a different enquiry. A law, which actually exists, is a law,
though we happen to dislike it…" (PJD, Lecture V)

"Expounding principles and distinctions which are the appropriate matter of jurisprudence, I shall present them
abstracted from every particular system. But I shall also endeavour to illustrate them by examples from the two
particular systems which I have studied with some accuracy, namely, the Roman Law, and the Law of England."
(*Lectures on Jurisprudence*, Lecture XI)

The first passage is about the descriptive nature of legal theory (law as it is, not law as it ought to be); the
second is about its generality — the same two ideas we find in Hart. Is it possible to develop a general theory of
law, and at what level of generality should we work?

## The gunman situation

"The gunman orders his victim to hand over his purse and threatens to shoot if he refuses; if the victim complies we
refer to the way in which he was forced to do so by saying that he was obliged to do so." (CL 6) Hart works with the
distinction between *being obliged* and *having an obligation*: the clerk is obliged to hand over the money, but is
not under an obligation to.

The key aspects: the gunman is giving an order; the bank clerk is in the gunman's power; coercive orders / orders
backed by threats. "Legal obligation is to be found in this situation writ large; A must be the sovereign habitually
obeyed and the orders must be general, prescribing courses of conduct not single actions." (CL 82) Can the gunman
situation help us answer the question 'what is law'?

## Commands and orders

"…we shall use the expressions 'orders backed by threats' and 'coercive orders' to refer to orders which, like the
gunman's, are supported only by threats…" (CL 19)

"To command is characteristically to exercise authority over men, not power to inflict harm, and though it may be
combined with threats of harm a command is primarily an appeal not to fear but to respect for authority. It is
obvious that the idea of a command with its very strong connection with authority is much closer to that of law than
our gunman's order backed by threats…" (CL 20)

## Four elements added to the gunman situation

According to Hart, four elements should be added to the gunman situation to have a plausible model for law:

1. Generality (general type of conduct + general class of persons)
2. Enduring character
3. General habit of obedience
4. Supremacy and independence (CL 24)

"If, following Austin, we call such a supreme and independent person or body of persons the sovereign, the laws of
any country will be the general orders backed by threats which are issued either by the sovereign or subordinates in
obedience to the sovereign." (CL 25) At the end of Chapter II the model really resembles Austin's theory; in
Chapters III and IV Hart attacks the model — an indirect way of criticising Austin.

## Hart's criticisms

Three groups of objections (Chapter III):

- **The content of laws** — duty-imposing and power-conferring rules. Some criminal law resembles coercive orders,
  but contracts, wills and marriage give people powers; there is no duty to make a will.
- **Their range of application** — laws made by the legislature also apply to the legislators, whereas Austin's
  sovereign issues commands only to others.
- **Their modes of origin** — customary law is not issued by the sovereign. Austin treats customs as tacit orders of
  the sovereign, who by not interfering tacitly accepts them.

A more fundamental criticism (Chapter IV): "…the whole conception of a supreme and independent sovereign habitually
obeyed, on which the model rests, is misleading, since there is little in any actual legal system which corresponds
to it." (CL 27)

:::examtip
Hart criticises Austin on Austin's own metatheoretical criteria: a descriptive theory of law must fit the facts of
real legal systems. If the theory does not fit the facts, you cannot change the reality of law; you need to change
the theory.
:::

## Chapter IV — Sovereign and Subject

1. The idea of a habit of obedience. Can it explain two salient features of most legal systems: the continuity of
   law, and the persistence of law?
2. The legally unlimited power enjoyed by the sovereign.
3. The position occupied by the sovereign above the law.

Austin's idea of sovereignty conceptually depends on the concept of a habit of obedience; that is the pillar Hart
attacks. His conclusion is that we need to replace it with the idea of a rule. Because the concept of a rule is
absent, a theory like Austin's cannot properly explain what a legal obligation is — the normative force of law. Law
is not just a matter of power; it is also a matter of authority. Legal obligation is more than coercion, but
different from a moral obligation.

## Habits vs rules

In both cases we can observe convergence of behaviour (going to the cinema on a Saturday night; a man removing his
hat in church). However, according to Hart, there are three salient differences:

1. Deviations from a rule are met with criticisms.
2. The rule is regarded as a good reason for making such criticism.
3. (Social) rules have an internal aspect.

:::rule
For Hart, "…habits are not 'normative': they cannot confer rights or authority on anyone." (CL 60)
:::

## The relationship between law and coercion

"There are two sources of continuing interest in the relationship between law and coercion, one clarificatory, the
other evaluative." (G. Lamond, 'Coercion and the Nature of Law' (2001) 7(1) *Legal Theory* 36) Views on the
connection (ibid 42–45):

- Coercion is the genus of which law is a species (Austin)
- Law is a distinctive form of social ordering because of its reliance upon coercion (for Kelsen, law is a coercive
  social order)
- Coercion is the most salient aspect of law (Bentham, Holmes, Raz)
- Law regulates the use of coercion (Bobbio)
- The point of legal practice is to guide and constrain the coercive power of the state (Dworkin)

Bobbio builds on Kelsen: coercion is the object of law, so acts of revenge are forbidden and criminal law developed to
control how the community responds to violence. The list is not conclusive.

## Is coercion a necessary feature of law?

- According to Lamond, "…the right to regulate coercion lies within the scope of law's distinctive claim to
  authority." (p 56)
- Lamond also argues that coercion "is not the most salient aspect of law – at best it is the most salient aspect of
  *certain* legal systems" (ibid).
- Raz's thought experiment of the society of angels: angels comply with the rules, so there is no need for
  coercion, but there may still be coordination problems that need law. If we can imagine a legal system without
  coercion, coercion is not a necessary feature of the concept of law.

Cf. Fred Schauer, 'Was Austin Right After All?': "The phenomenon of law as it is experienced is overwhelmingly
coercive […] Insofar as a common view of the enterprise of legal theory treats ignoring this highly salient dimension
of law as largely non-problematic, it may, as Hart put it, fail to "fit the facts"[…]. But then the question shifts to
a question about the nature and value of that view of the task of legal theory." (p 18)

## Fred Schauer on the role of sanctions

- Whether Austin's account is sound "may be a function of the answer to the question of what a theory of law is
  designed to accomplish" (abstract).
- The issue of legal obligation; Hart's critique; Hart's criticism of Austin is "problematic in two distinct ways"
  (p 7). Schauer is critical of the way Hart used the distinction between being obliged and having an obligation.
- "…the focus of legal theory is dependent on a conception of what a legal theory is supposed to accomplish" (p 16).
  For Schauer, legal theory should capture the salient, significant, important features of law, not only its
  necessary ones.

:::examtip
Schauer's p 16 line is a direct answer to the essay's methodology question: whether coercion matters to a theory of
law depends on what a theory of law is supposed to accomplish.
:::

## Curiosity, charity, clarity

The principle of charitable interpretation requires us to interpret a given statement/claim in the most rational way
possible and to discuss its best, strongest possible version. Hart on Austin: "[…] we have not hesitated where
Austin's meaning is doubtful or where his views seem inconsistent to ignore this and to state a clear and consistent
position. Moreover, where Austin merely gives hints as to ways in which criticisms might be met, we have developed
these (in part along the lines followed by later theorists such as Kelsen) in order to secure that the doctrine we
shall consider and criticize is stated in its strongest form." (CL 18)

:::examtip
Do this once, well, to one opponent: state the view at its strongest, then criticise it. Hart is revisiting Austin
for theoretical purposes, not for historical accuracy.
:::
