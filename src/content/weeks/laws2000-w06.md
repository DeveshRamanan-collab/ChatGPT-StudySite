## Immunities: overview

'Immunities' refers to instances where a State's adjudicatory and enforcement jurisdiction cannot be exercised as
normal because another foreign State (or a foreign State representative) are involved in the case.

- **Part A.** 1st: state immunity — sovereign acts, *acta jure imperii* (absolute immunity); commercial acts,
  *acta jure gestionis* (restricted immunity). 2nd: immunity of state representatives — personal immunity
  (*ratione personae*) and functional immunity (*ratione materiae*).
- **Part B.** 3rd: diplomatic immunities — special protections given to ambassadors, embassy staff and embassy
  premises (Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations). Then the immunities of international organisations.

:::examtip
The lecture stresses that these are clearly divided into categories because they are governed by different
regimes of international law, and different conditions apply. Keep them apart in an answer.
:::

## State immunity

The international law of immunities sets limits on when a state may exercise jurisdiction within its own
territory. Grounded in the fundamental principle of sovereign equality, which reflects the independence of states
=> also juridically equal, therefore no state may exercise jurisdiction over another without consent. That is, the
domestic courts of one state may decline to exercise jurisdiction if the respondent in the case is another state
(incl. cases concerning foreign states' officials or property). Originally, state immunity was considered to be
absolute; that is not so much the case anymore.

**Immunity from adjudicative jurisdiction vs immunity from enforcement jurisdiction.** Adjudicative jurisdiction
relates to the power of a court to adjudicate on a matter — to look into the merits of the subject matter and
render a decision based on the applicable legal rules and principles. Enforcement jurisdiction concerns the
implementation of whatever judgment is rendered, so the question of enforcing arises only after (a) adjudicative
jurisdiction has been established and (b) a judgment entailing some sort of enforcement measures (e.g.
confiscation of certain assets) is rendered. In practice, state immunity from adjudication means that a state
cannot be sued before a foreign court unless it gives its consent; state immunity from enforcement means that the
judgment which has been rendered cannot be enforced upon the state due to immunity. Prescriptive jurisdiction is
not affected.

:::rule
Immunity acts as a procedural barrier to a court's adjudicatory jurisdiction. State immunity is a principle of
customary international law — a principle well established in State practice and in the jurisprudence of
national and international courts.
:::

There have been a few efforts to codify this understanding: initial work by the International Law Commission
(ILC), Draft Articles on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and their Property (1991), resulted in the UN
Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and their Property (2004), which comes into force once there are
30 ratifications (as of 2026, 25 ratifications/accessions have been received). The convention is not yet in
force, so the rules are customary international law; the lecture refers to its articles because they mostly
reflect custom.

:::statute
UN Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and their Property (2004). Art 5: "A State enjoys immunity,
in respect of itself and its property, from the jurisdiction of the courts of another State". Art 6: modalities
for giving effect to state immunity (refraining from exercising jurisdiction before its courts). Art 7: no
immunity if the state expressly consents to the exercise of foreign jurisdiction (e.g. by agreement). Art 8: no
immunity if the state participates in court proceedings. Art 9: no immunity in counterclaims (arising from
proceedings initiated by a state or otherwise participated in).
:::

Once a state has waived its immunity — in writing or by participating in the proceeding — it cannot claim
immunity at the end when the judgment goes against it.

:::definition
Definition of "State", art 2(1)(b): the State and its various organs of government; constituent units of a
federal State or political subdivisions of the State that are entitled to perform acts in the exercise of the
sovereign authority of the State; agencies or instrumentalities of the State and other entities, to the extent
that they are entitled to perform acts in the exercise of the sovereign authority of the State; representatives
of the State acting in that capacity.
:::

**Domestic legislation.** UK: State Immunity Act 1978 (broadly consistent with the 2004 UN Convention, which was
signed by the UK in 2005 but not yet ratified) treats State immunity as a preliminary matter (s 1(2)); there is a
presumption that a State possesses immunity, with the plaintiff bearing the burden of proof to the contrary.
Similar position in Australia: Foreign States Immunities Act 1985 — foreign states are in principle immune to the
jurisdiction of Australian courts, unless and to the extent that the state has waived immunity, or the case
concerns a commercial transaction.

## Sovereign acts and commercial acts

**Distinction!** *Jure imperii*: act of a sovereign nature → immunity applies (e.g. foreign and military affairs;
legislation, police power, administration of justice). *Jure gestionis*: act of a non-sovereign nature →
immunity does not apply (e.g. activities of a commercial nature). Common law countries such as the UK and US
originally saw immunity as absolute, while many civil law countries distinguished sovereign acts; common law
decisions evolved to recognise the distinction. About one-third of states in the world, including China, still do
not recognise it.

:::case
*Empire of Iran Case* (1963), Germany: the German Federal Constitutional Court found that absolute State
immunity was no longer customary international law — distinguishing between sovereign acts (jure imperii) and
commercial acts (jure gestionis).
:::

:::case
*Trendex Trading Corporation v Central Bank of Nigeria* (1977), English Court of Appeal: the Court rejected
immunity in proceedings against the Central Bank of Nigeria, which had failed to honour a letter of credit (for
cement bought from Trendex). The bank was an organ of the state, but the nature of the transaction was
commercial, not a sovereign act.
:::

:::statute
UN Convention, art 10: "If a State engages in a commercial transaction with a foreign natural or juridical person
… the State cannot invoke immunity from that jurisdiction in a proceeding arising out of that commercial
transaction." Exceptions: commercial transactions between States; if the parties to the commercial transaction
have expressly agreed.
:::

**What is a commercial transaction?** Art 2(1)(c): any commercial contract or transaction for the sale of goods or
supply of services; any contract for a loan or other transaction of a financial nature, including any obligation
of guarantee or of indemnity in respect of any such loan or transaction; any other contract or transaction of a
commercial, industrial, trading or professional nature, but not including a contract of employment of persons.
Whether there is a profit to be made is not relevant.

**Proceedings in which state immunity cannot be invoked** (UN Convention): art 10 commercial transactions; art 11
certain contracts of employment; art 13 rights of ownership, possession and use of property; art 14 intellectual
and industrial property; art 15 participation in companies or other collective bodies; art 16 ships owned or
operated by a state (unless used for government non-commercial purposes, exception for warships); art 17
arbitration agreements relating to commercial transactions.

**Nature or purpose?** Art 2(2): in determining whether a contract or transaction is a "commercial transaction",
"reference should be made primarily to the nature of the contract or transaction … but its purpose should also be
taken into account if the parties to the contract or transaction have so agreed". The classification depends
primarily on the nature of the activity (e.g. purchasing goods or providing services), not on the broader policy
objective; courts assess whether the activity resembles private commercial conduct, but may also consider purpose
if the parties so agreed. This qualification raises further issues, as States generally apply public policy
objectives in their commercial transactions. The lecture's examples: buying office supplies, or catering for an
official function, at an embassy is a commercial transaction, whatever its official purpose.

:::case
*Playa Largo v I Congreso del Partido* (1983), UK House of Lords: Cuba's state-owned shipping company agreed to
deliver sugar to Chile but was ordered to divert the ships due to political tensions after a coup in Chile. Cuba
claimed sovereign immunity, arguing the diversion had a foreign policy objective. The House of Lords rejected the
claim: the shipping activity was commercial (jure gestionis), not sovereign (jure imperii).
:::

## Injury, damage and serious violations

:::statute
UN Convention, art 12 (injury to individuals and damage to property): "Unless otherwise agreed between the States
concerned, a State cannot invoke immunity from jurisdiction before a court of another State which is otherwise
competent in a proceeding which relates to pecuniary compensation for death or injury to the person, or damage to
or loss of tangible property, caused by an act or omission which is alleged to be attributable to the State, if
the act or omission occurred in whole or in part in the territory of that other State and if the author of the
act or omission was present in that territory at the time of the act or omission."
:::

The lecture adds that art 12 covers material damage only, not moral damage.

Recall: state immunity serves as a procedural legal shield that prevents national courts from exercising
jurisdiction over foreign states. If a state is entitled to immunity, the court must dismiss the case. Will this
hold regardless of the seriousness of the allegations made against the state — in cases involving serious human
rights violations or grave breaches of international law?

:::case
*Jurisdictional Immunities of the State (Germany v Italy: Greece Intervening)* [2012] ICJ Rep 99: civil
proceedings in Italian courts against Germany with respect to serious crimes committed by German forces against
civilian populations and prisoners of war during the occupation of Italy in World War II (Italian courts also
ordered measures against Villa Vigoni, German property in Italy). Germany claimed that Italian courts, and thus the
Italian state, were violating its right to state immunity. The ICJ ruled that (as of 2012) customary international
law did not deprive a state of immunity because of alleged serious violations of international human rights law
and IHL.
:::

What about *jus cogens* norms? "The rules of State immunity are procedural in character and are confined to
determining whether or not the courts of one State may exercise jurisdiction in respect of another … It regulates
the exercise of jurisdiction in respect of particular conduct and is thus entirely distinct from the substantive
law which determines whether that conduct is lawful or unlawful" (para 93). "State practice in the form of
judicial decisions supports the proposition that State immunity for acta jure imperii continues to extend to civil
proceedings for acts occasioning death, personal injury or damage to property committed by the armed forces and
other organs of a State in the conduct of armed conflict, even if the relevant acts take place on the territory of
the forum State" (para 77). Despite strong arguments that states should not be immune when committing such acts, a
State enjoys immunity in foreign national courts — even when the case involves grave breaches of international
law: "Powerful policy arguments may be advanced in favour of such an exception, but state practice does not, at
present, support the existence of a rule depriving states of immunity in such circumstances" (Rose et al).

## Immunities of state officials

State representatives enjoy certain immunities from national jurisdiction because they 'personify' the State on
whose behalf they act and because they need protection from national jurisdiction in order to fulfil their
functions as state representatives. These immunities rest on customary international law; the 2004 UN Convention
is not relevant here.

:::case
*Arrest Warrant of 11 April 2000 (Democratic Republic of Congo v Belgium)* [2002] ICJ Rep 3: "In customary
international law, the immunities accorded to Ministers for Foreign Affairs are not granted for their personal
benefit, but to ensure the effective performance of their functions on behalf of their respective States. … he or
she is frequently required to travel internationally, and thus must be in a position freely to do so whenever the
need should arise."
:::

State officials/representatives of the state (individuals): head of state; ministers; government officials/civil
servants. The extent of immunity offered to various representatives differs substantially depending on the office
they hold (function they serve) and whether they acted in official capacity.

**Functional immunity (*ratione materiae*)** — this immunity relates not to the person but to the function the
official performs; it is about the act itself being an official act of the state.
- Immunity only in relation to official acts taken while in office.
- Continues after the official leaves office.
- Based on customary international law.
- As long as an act is performed by an individual with official authority ('under the colour of authority') it is
  an 'official' act, and for the act the individual is immune from criminal prosecution before foreign national
  courts.
- Can be waived only with the state's consent.

**Personal immunity (*ratione personae*)** — protection enjoyed because of the office held, personifying the
state.
- Complete immunity, even in relation to acts of a private nature.
- Applies only to very high-ranking state officials (in particular heads of state, heads of government, foreign
  ministers). Heads of state and foreign ministers enjoy privileged status in international law (see *Certain
  Questions of Mutual Assistance in Criminal Matters (Djibouti v France)* 2008: the ICJ found this is customary
  international law).
- Temporal limit: only for the duration of the official's time in office (after which they continue to enjoy
  functional immunity).
- Can be waived by the state.

:::examtip
The lecture's examples: a soldier who kills an enemy soldier on the battlefield acts officially, with authority —
functional immunity. A sitting Greek president who drives drunk on holiday in Australia and kills someone is
covered by personal immunity (Greece's own courts could still try them); once they leave office, that private act
is not covered by functional immunity and they could be arrested and prosecuted in Australia.
:::

## Immunity and international crimes

Would a State representative be immune from domestic criminal proceedings for alleged serious violations of
international law, such as war crimes or crimes against humanity? Under the current state of international law,
both functional and personal immunities continue to apply, even in cases of serious international crimes (e.g.
war crimes, crimes against humanity, genocide) — see *Arrest Warrant*, paras 58 et seq. A Belgian court, under
universal jurisdiction, issued an arrest warrant for the sitting Congolese foreign minister, Yerodia, accused of
inciting hatred; the ICJ ruled that current state practice does not support the existence of an exception to
immunity from foreign criminal jurisdiction in situations concerning the commission of international crimes. That
case concerns an incumbent foreign minister (i.e. personal immunity).

:::rule
"The immunity from jurisdiction enjoyed by incumbent Ministers for Foreign Affairs does not mean that they enjoy
impunity in respect of any crimes they might have committed … Immunity from criminal jurisdiction and individual
criminal responsibility are quite separate concepts. While jurisdictional immunity is procedural in nature,
criminal responsibility is a question of substantive law" (*Arrest Warrant*, paras 58–61).
:::

Criminal prosecution of the individual is still possible:
1. Domestic prosecution in their own countries — such persons enjoy no criminal immunity under international law
   in their own countries.
2. Their countries can waive their immunity.
3. Once the individual leaves their functions, other states with jurisdiction could try them, in respect of acts
   committed before or after their period of office and for acts committed during that period of office in a
   private capacity.
4. International criminal courts may also be able to prosecute an incumbent or former Minister for Foreign
   Affairs where they have jurisdiction, e.g. the ICTY, the ICTR and the International Criminal Court, whose
   statutes say the office the person occupies does not matter.

**Former state officials prosecuted for international crimes after they have left office** (i.e. questions of
functional immunity).

:::case
*R v Bow Street Metropolitan Stipendiary Magistrate, Ex parte Pinochet Ugarte (No 3)*, UK House of Lords (1999):
former Chilean president Pinochet (in office 1973–1990) travelled to the UK for medical treatment; Spanish courts
brought a criminal case against him under Spain's universal jurisdiction laws, including charges of torture, and
requested extradition. He was arrested in the UK. The House of Lords held that while there is absolute immunity
for serving heads of state, there is no immunity for former heads of state in relation to acts which, at the time
they occurred, constituted an international crime for which a form of universal jurisdiction has been
established (e.g. torture as enshrined in the Convention against Torture). A few judges relied on the *jus
cogens* nature of the prohibition on torture; the majority relied on the UK, Chile and Spain all being parties to
the Convention against Torture. He was ultimately allowed to leave on medical grounds (died in 2006).
:::

Emerging exceptions in state practice regarding functional immunity in cases of international crimes? See the ILC
Report, 'Immunity of State officials from foreign criminal jurisdiction' (2017), draft article 7: immunity should
not apply in relation to genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, torture and enforced disappearances. There
is still debate whether this reflects state practice, and the commissioners disagreed about whether there was
enough evidence of state practice when it was adopted.

## Diplomatic and consular immunities

Functions of diplomatic missions include the representation, promotion and protection of the interests and
nationals of the sending state. Diplomats and consular officials benefit from a special legal protection regime
codified in two conventions (prior to 1961 entirely governed by customary international law or bilateral
treaties): the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (VCDR, 1961) and the Vienna Convention on Consular
Relations (VCCR, 1963).

:::case
*United States Diplomatic and Consular Staff in Tehran (United States of America v Iran)* [1980] ICJ Rep 3, para
86: "the rules of diplomatic law … constitute a self-contained regime, which on the one hand, lays out the
receiving State's obligations regarding the facilities, privileges and immunities to be accorded to diplomatic
missions and, on the other, foresees their possible abuse by members of the mission and specifies the means at
the disposal of the receiving State to counter any such abuse." During the Iranian revolution in 1979 militants
stormed the US Embassy; over 50 US diplomats and consular officials were held hostage for 444 days, and Iranian
security forces did not intervene. The ICJ confirmed the inviolability of diplomatic envoys and embassies and
determined that Iran's inaction had violated its international obligations.
:::

**To whom do diplomatic immunities apply?**
- Diplomatic agents (e.g. ambassadors but also lower-level diplomatic agents).
- Consular agents (who represent the state in administrative or technical matters).
- Members of the family of a diplomatic agent forming part of the household, unless nationals of the receiving
  state.
- Members of the administrative and technical staff of the mission (subject to greater exceptions), with family
  members forming their households, if not nationals or permanent residents of the receiving State.
- Members of the service staff who are not nationals or permanent residents, in respect of acts performed in the
  course of their official duties.

:::statute
VCDR art 31: a diplomatic agent shall enjoy immunity from the criminal jurisdiction of the receiving State
(personal immunity), and shall also enjoy immunity from its civil and administrative jurisdiction, except in the
case of: (a) a real action relating to private immovable property situated in the territory of the receiving
State, unless he holds it on behalf of the sending State for the purposes of the mission; (b) an action relating
to succession in which the diplomatic agent is involved as executor, administrator, heir or legatee as a private
person and not on behalf of the sending State; (c) an action relating to any professional or commercial activity
exercised by the diplomatic agent in the receiving State outside his official functions.
:::

- Art 29: the person of a diplomatic agent is inviolable (e.g. cannot be arrested).
- Art 30: the private residence of a diplomatic agent, his papers and correspondence, are also inviolable.
- Art 41: a diplomatic agent has a duty to respect the laws and regulations of the receiving State, and a duty not
  to interfere in the internal affairs of the state.

**Consuls (VCCR 1963).** Immunities are more restricted: limited personal inviolability from arrest or detention
(e.g. can be arrested for a 'grave crime') (art 41); immunity ratione materiae only with respect to criminal and
civil matters (art 43).

**Diplomatic missions.** Immunities and inviolability also extend to diplomatic missions (VCDR art 22), except with
the consent of the head of mission. Immune from search, requisition, attachment and execution: the premises of the
mission, furnishings and other property within, means of transportation, and archives (art 24). The receiving
State has a duty to protect the premises of the mission against any intrusion or damage and to prevent any
disturbance of the peace of the mission or impairment of its dignity (art 22(2)) — see also the UN Convention on
the Prevention and Punishment of Crimes against Internationally Protected Persons, including Diplomatic Agents
(1973). As the lecture puts it, Australia cannot enter the embassy of New Zealand in Canberra.

**Abuses of diplomatic immunity.** What happens if diplomatic immunity is abused and no exception applies?
- *Persona non grata*: the receiving state declares the presence of a person not acceptable, revoking diplomatic
  credentials (VCDR art 9) — e.g. the US against Russian diplomats after allegations of Russian interference in
  the 2016 US elections.
- Severance or breach of diplomatic relations.
- Other 'unfriendly acts'.
- Nothing.
- Waiver of immunity (by the sending state).

:::examtip
Tutorial (the Julian Assange case): Assange sought refuge in the Ecuadorian Embassy in London in June 2012 to
avoid extradition to Sweden, was granted asylum by Ecuador in August 2012 and stayed nearly seven years on the
premises. Questions: the jurisdictional issues (with Topic 5); whether Ecuador could grant him asylum; whether the
UK could enter the embassy to arrest him; and whether Ecuador, after granting him citizenship, could ask the UK to
recognise him as a diplomatic agent, and why. (The 1951 Refugee Convention is irrelevant.)
:::

## Diplomatic immunities: the Part B lecture

*Added from the Part B online lecture on diplomatic immunities (Echo360 captions), paraphrased. It fills in the
slides above; points marked † come from the recording alone, not the slides, and should be verified.*

Diplomatic relations are the main way states communicate with each other: they set up diplomatic and consular
missions abroad and send ambassadors (high commissioners† in Commonwealth countries, doing the same job), consuls
and envoys. These people are an extension of the state and represent its interests overseas, a subset of the state
officials met in Part A, but governed by their own protection regimes. Two terms recur: the **receiving** (host)
state, where the staff are posted, and the **sending** state, which the staff come from and represent.

Why a protective regime at all? A mission's staff and premises sit inside another state's jurisdiction, with few
resources of their own for security, so they are particularly vulnerable. The rules were long customary and are now
codified in the VCDR (1961, diplomats) and the VCCR (1963, consuls). There are two conventions because diplomatic
work is more sensitive and so attracts greater immunities; consular immunities are more restrictive. Where the VCDR
is silent, customary international law may still apply.

### Article 31 and the commercial-activity exception

A diplomatic agent's immunity from the receiving state's criminal jurisdiction is absolute; the three art 31
exceptions apply only to civil and administrative jurisdiction. The lecturer called the first two (private
immovable property, private succession) unproblematic. The third, professional or commercial activity outside
official functions, has been debated. Paid employment outside the mission, or professional services for
remuneration by a member of the mission or of their family, clearly falls within it: a diplomat who also works as a
teacher in the receiving state can be sued over that work.

:::case
*Tabion v Mufti*† (US Court of Appeals, 1996): the exception does not reach contracts for goods and services
incidental to a diplomat's daily life (the lecture's example is the diplomat's dry cleaner), so immunity covers
disputes arising from them. States have since interpreted the exception in different ways.
:::

The art 41 duties (respect local law; do not interfere in internal affairs) are the flip side of the immunities, but
they are without prejudice† to them: breaching art 41 does not by itself remove a diplomat's immunity.

### Consuls

Consuls do not represent the sending state in its political relations. They act for it in administrative and
technical matters that are often private in nature, such as issuing passports to its nationals in the receiving
state or giving them legal assistance. Consulates are often outside the capital: in Australia most embassies are in
Canberra, with consulates in cities such as Sydney and Melbourne, while the political relationship is handled by the
diplomatic staff in Canberra.

Because their political role is limited, so are their immunities. A consul can be arrested for a grave crime (VCCR
art 41), which never applies to a diplomat, and has functional immunity (ratione materiae) only, in criminal and
civil matters (art 43), not personal immunity.

### Missions and the duty to protect

Inviolability means the receiving state's police may not enter the premises (the embassy or consulate) or the
mission's cars without the consent of the head of mission, and the premises, furnishings, means of transport and
archives are immune from search, requisition and execution of a court decision. The lecturer's example: diplomatic
cars in Canberra carry blue "DC" plates† and share these protections.

*Tehran Hostages* in more detail. After the Iranian revolution the deposed Shah† went into exile in the United
States, which was seen as allied with the old regime. In November 1979 students (the slides say militants) seized
the US Embassy in Tehran and held more than 50 diplomats and consular officials until January 1981, while Iran's
security forces ignored appeals for help from inside. The US claimed breaches of both the VCDR and the VCCR. The
hostage-takers were not Iranian officials, so the breach lay in Iran's own failure to act: its authorities knew they
were obliged to take appropriate steps to protect the premises and staff, knew from the situation and the staff's
appeals that action was urgently needed, had the means to act, and did nothing, in violation of VCDR art 22.

:::rule
The duty to protect in VCDR art 22 is not merely passive. The receiving state must take positive steps to protect
missions on its territory, not just keep its own agents out.
:::

### When the sending state's people abuse immunity

Abuse is rare but real. The lecturer's everyday example is New York, where diplomats (mostly UN diplomats rather
than those posted to the United States) owed about US$16 million† in unpaid parking fines in 2014; graver cases
involve drug trafficking, money laundering, tax evasion or even murder. The receiving state's options are the
slide's list: declare the person persona non grata (art 9), revoking their credentials, as the US did with Russian
diplomats over alleged interference in the 2016 election; sever or break off relations; other unfriendly acts; or do
nothing. Waiver is different in kind: it is the sending state's decision, not one the receiving state can make.

## Immunities of international organisations

International organisations also benefit from immunity from domestic jurisdiction under certain circumstances
(e.g. UN, EU). These immunities are considered necessary to carry out their functions without interference from
the state where they are operating, and to secure similar treatment in different countries. International
organisations enjoy those immunities and privileges as granted to them by states — sometimes through treaties,
e.g. the Convention on the Privileges and Immunities of the United Nations (1946). They are immune from the
adjudicatory jurisdiction of states in civil and criminal proceedings and from enforcement jurisdiction; the
immunity of their officials is limited to acts carried out in their functions. They also have privileges (e.g. UN
officials do not pay tax on their UN employment income where they are posted).

:::statute
UN Charter art 105: "1. The Organization shall enjoy in the territory of each of its Members such privileges and
immunities as are necessary for the fulfillment of its purposes. 2. Representatives of the Members of the United
Nations and officials of the Organization shall similarly enjoy such privileges and immunities as are necessary
for the independent exercise of their functions in connection with the Organization…"
:::

**Immunities vary depending on functions** (1946 Convention):
- Representatives of members: immunity from personal arrest or detention and from seizure of their personal
  baggage, and, in respect of words spoken or written and all acts done by them in their capacity as
  representatives, immunity from legal process of every kind; inviolability for all papers and documents (art IV).
- UN officials: immune from legal process in respect of words spoken or written and all acts performed by them in
  their official capacity (art V, section 18).
- Experts on missions: immunity from personal arrest or detention and from seizure of their personal baggage; in
  respect of words spoken or written and acts done in the course of the performance of their mission, immunity
  from legal process of every kind, continuing even when no longer employed on missions for the UN; inviolability
  for all papers and documents (art VI).

:::statute
Convention on the Privileges and Immunities of the United Nations (1946), art II. Section 2: "The United Nations,
its property and assets wherever located and by whomsoever held, shall enjoy immunity from every form of legal
process except in so far as in any particular case it has expressly waived its immunity. It is, however,
understood that no waiver of immunity shall extend to any measure of execution." Section 3: "The premises of the
United Nations shall be inviolable. The property and assets of the United Nations, wherever located and by
whomsoever held, shall be immune from search, requisition, confiscation, expropriation and any other form of
interference, whether by executive, administrative, judicial or legislative action." Section 4: "The archives of
the United Nations, and in general all documents belonging to it or held by it, shall be inviolable wherever
located."
:::

**Accountability of international organisations.** IOs may have to provide alternative modes for settling private
claims (for the UN, the lecture points to section 29 of the 1946 Convention, usually met by arbitration clauses,
e.g. in employment contracts), and IOs can waive their immunity (the Secretary-General may waive an official's
immunity where it would impede the course of justice and can be waived without prejudice to the UN's interests —
regularly done for car accidents, and done where UN civilian police were shown responsible for rape in peace
operations). Accountability is frequently raised in the context of peacekeeping, or sexual exploitation and abuse
committed by UN peacekeepers.

**Case study: the 2010 cholera outbreak in Haiti.** Nearly 10,000 people died. A UN-appointed panel concluded, in
2011, that contamination by a UN peacekeepers' camp was a likely cause of the epidemic, but said "it was not the
fault of, or deliberate action of, a group or individual." While the UN Secretary-General has apologised, the UN
has not accepted legal responsibility. Cases brought by human rights lawyers before US courts: in 2016, US federal
courts upheld the UN's immunity.

:::case
*Stichting Mothers of Srebrenica and Others v The Netherlands* (Supreme Court of the Netherlands, 13 April 2012;
ECtHR, 11 June 2013): UNPROFOR, Srebrenica 1995 — families of victims sued the UN and the Netherlands in Dutch
courts for failing to prevent the genocide. Both decisions recognised UN immunity before domestic courts. The case
continued against the Netherlands alone, which the Dutch Supreme Court in 2019 found partially responsible, for
the conduct over which it had effective control.
:::
