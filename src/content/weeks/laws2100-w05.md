## Positivity and normativity (a recap before Dworkin)

The lecture opened with two big concepts met so far: positivity (positive law, legal positivism) and normativity. The
adjective 'normative' is used in different senses:

- **Normative vs descriptive claims** (week 1): a normative claim is a claim about how law ought to be — whether
  family law doctrines are desirable, and how we could reform them.
- **Hart**: Austin's theory could not account for the normativity of law — law's binding, authoritative force,
  identified without passing a moral judgement on whether the directives are desirable.
- **Natural law**: the normativity of law is connected with its morality.
- **Kelsen**: 'normative' simply means dealing with norms; a legal order is a set of norms, belonging to the sphere
  of the ought, not the is.

Today the term comes back: Dworkin challenges the positivist idea that questions about law as it is can be separated
from questions about law as it ought to be.

## Ronald Dworkin (1931–2013)

- Professor of Jurisprudence, University of Oxford (1969–98). He succeeded Hart, and Hart supported his election
  although Dworkin had already criticised Hart's theory.
- *Taking Rights Seriously* (1977); *A Matter of Principle* (1985); *Law's Empire* (1986); *Justice for Hedgehogs*
  (2011).
- N. Stavropoulos, 'Legal Interpretivism', *The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (Spring 2021 Edition) gives a
  good overview of the phases of Dworkin's theory.

Dworkin was American; you will see why some people think that matters.

## Taking Rights Seriously (1977)

- Ronald Dworkin's book defends a liberal theory of law.
- Dworkin is critical of another liberal theory, which he calls "the ruling theory of law".
- According to Dworkin, the ruling theory of law has two parts, which derive from the philosophy of Jeremy Bentham:
  a theory about what law is (ie legal positivism) and a theory about what law ought to be (ie utilitarianism).
- Dworkin's book criticises both parts of the theory + the assumption that they are independent of one another.
- For Dworkin, a general theory of law must be normative as well as conceptual.

The lecture: it is impossible to neatly separate the 'is' claims from the 'ought' claims. This is the same idea we
found in chapter 1 of Finnis's *Natural Law and Natural Rights*, expressed in different terms and with different
arguments. What is common to these non-positivist theories is that challenge.

## Dworkin on legal positivism

"The most powerful contemporary version of positivism is that proposed by H.L.A. Hart, and it is Hart's version which
is criticized in this book." (TRS ix)

"Legal positivism rejects the idea that legal rights can pre-exist any form of legislation…" (TRS xi)

"Legal positivism […] is the theory that individuals have legal rights only insofar as these have been created by
explicit political decisions or explicit social practice." (TRS xii)

The point is broader than legislation: it is about explicit political decisions and explicit social practice
(including judicial practice). What Dworkin has in mind is that there are rights, like moral rights, that the law
takes in.

## The Model of Rules I (1967)

"I want to examine the soundness of legal positivism, particularly in the powerful form that Professor H.L.A. Hart
has given to it. I choose to focus on his position, not only because of its clarity and elegance, but because here,
as almost everywhere else in legal philosophy, constructive thought must start with a consideration of his views."
(TRS 16)

:::definition
According to Dworkin, these are the key tenets of legal positivism:
1. Legal rules can be identified in light of their **pedigree** (Hart's rule of recognition).
2. There are cases in which judges must exercise **discretion** (the law has gaps).
3. **Legal obligation** if and only if the case falls under a valid legal rule.
:::

Recall Hart on being obliged vs having an obligation: no obligation without a rule — none in the gunman scenario, but
there is one when you play chess. Dworkin sets out to show all three tenets are incorrect.

## Rules, principles, and policies

"I want to make a general attack on positivism […] My strategy will be organized around the fact that when lawyers
reason or dispute about legal rights and obligations […] they make use of standards that do not function as rules,
but operate differently as principles, policies, and other sorts of standards. Positivism, I shall argue, is a model
of and for a system of rules, and its central notion of a single fundamental test for law forces us to miss the
important roles of these standards that are not rules." (TRS 22)

The perspective is that of the lawyers and judges who argue within the system. Like Finnis, Dworkin takes on board
Hart's internal point of view, but each has a different understanding of it.

## Principles vs policies

:::definition
**Principle**: "…standard that is to be observed, not because it will advance or secure an economic, political, or
social situation deemed desirable, but because it is a requirement of justice or fairness or some other dimension of
morality." (TRS 22)
**Policy**: "…standard that sets out a goal to be reached, generally an improvement in some economic, political, or
social feature of the community (though some goals are negative, in that they stipulate that some present feature is
to be protected from adverse change)." (TRS 22)
:::

Example: "…the standard that automobile accidents are to be decreased is a policy, and the standard that no man may
profit by his own wrong a principle." (TRS 22) — the principle the judges refer to in *Riggs v Palmer*. (For now,
bracket whether 'morality' here means social morality or critical morality.)

## Legal principles vs legal rules

"The difference between legal principles and legal rules is a logical distinction. Both sets of standards point to
particular decisions about legal obligation in particular circumstances, but they differ in the character of the
direction they give." (TRS 24)

- A rule is applicable in an all-or-nothing fashion (if A then B: modus ponens), whereas a principle states a
  reason that argues in one direction.
- Principles have the dimension of weight/importance, whereas rules do not have this dimension (consider what
  happens when principles conflict: two conflicting rules — you pick one; conflicting principles — you balance them).
- It is not always clear from the form of a standard whether that standard is a rule or a principle: look at how
  legal actors reason with it.

Principles, for Dworkin, cannot be identified in light of their pedigree: a counterexample that puts pressure on
Hart's theory.

## Dworkin on discretion

- "The concept of discretion is at home in only one sort of context; when someone is in general charged with making
  decisions subject to standards set by a particular authority." (TRS 31)
- "Discretion, like the hole in a doughnut, does not exist except as an area left open by a surrounding belt of
  restriction." (TRS 31)

:::definition
**Weak discretion** (two senses): "Sometimes we use 'discretion' in a weak sense, simply to say that […] the
standards an official must apply cannot be applied mechanically but demand the use of judgment" (TRS 31); "Sometimes
we use the term in a different weak sense, to say only that some official has final authority to make a decision and
cannot be reviewed and reversed by any other official" (TRS 32).
**Strong discretion**: "We use 'discretion' sometimes not merely to say that an official must use judgment in
applying the standards set him by authority, or that no one will review that exercise of judgment, but to say that
on some issue he is simply not bound by standards set by the authority in question" (TRS 32).
:::

"Do the principles judges cite in cases like Riggs or Henningsen control their decisions, as the sergeant's orders to
take the most experienced men or the referee's duty to choose the more aggressive fighter control the decisions of
these officials? What arguments could a positivist supply to show that they do not?" (TRS 34)

Dworkin accepts weak discretion; positivists, he argues, are forced to accept strong discretion when no rule applies.
Some commentators say he cherry-picked his cases — keep that in mind when you read *Riggs*.

## Riggs v Palmer

:::case
*Riggs v Palmer* — Francis B. Palmer's will (13 August 1880) gave small legacies to his daughters, Mrs Riggs and
Mrs Preston (the plaintiffs), and the remainder of his estate to his grandson Elmer E. Palmer (the defendant). Elmer,
sixteen, knew of the provisions and, to prevent his grandfather revoking them and to obtain speedy possession, wilfully
murdered him by poisoning. The will was made in due form and admitted to probate. The sole question: can he have the
property?
:::

- It is quite true that statutes regulating wills, if literally construed, give this property to the murderer.
- However, it is a familiar canon of construction that a thing which is within the intention of the makers of a
  statute is as much within the statute as if it were within the letter; and a thing which is within the letter of
  the statute is not within the statute, unless it be within the intention of the makers.
- Besides, all laws as well as all contracts may be controlled in their operation and effect by general, fundamental
  maxims of the common law: no one shall be permitted to profit by his own fraud, or to take advantage of his own
  wrong, or to found any claim upon his own iniquity, or to acquire property by his own crime — general principles of
  natural law and justice. Rational/equitable construction.

The minority: we are bound by the rigid rules of law established by the legislature; no support for avoiding the
succession because of his criminal act when the laws are silent (*Owens v Owens* (100 N. C. 240) cited as a case quite
like this); and to concede the appellants' views would impose an additional punishment or penalty on the respondent.

For Dworkin, these principles are found as maxims of the common law — within positive law, not in extra-legal
sources — yet they have a clear moral dimension. The strategy is Hart's against Austin: a descriptive theory that
cannot explain this feature of our law fails. The minority's point about punishment matters too: for Dworkin the
point of the legal system is to justify the use of coercion, and a punishment made up on the fly would be illiberal.

## Dworkin's key move

What is the role played by principles in reaching particular decisions of law?

- We might treat legal principles the way we treat legal rules and say that some principles are binding as law and
  must be taken into account by judges and lawyers who make decisions of legal obligation.
- We might deny that principles can be binding the way some rules are: in cases like *Riggs* or *Henningsen* the judge
  reaches beyond the rules that he is bound to apply (beyond the "law") for extra-legal principles. (Next week's
  legal realists, and Raz, a hard positivist, take this view: when the law runs out judges create new law.)

## Dworkin on legal principles and legal obligation

According to Dworkin, we cannot adapt Hart's version of positivism by modifying his rule of recognition to embrace
principles, because we cannot formulate a test of pedigree to capture principles.

"If we treat principles as law we must reject the positivists' first tenet, that the law of a community is
distinguished from other social standards by some test in the form of a master rule. We have already decided that we
must then abandon the second tenet – the doctrine of judicial discretion – or clarify it into triviality. What of the
third tenet, the positivists' theory of legal obligation?" (TRS 44)

For Dworkin, positivists hold that "…a legal obligation exists when (and only when) an established rule of law
imposes such an obligation. It follows from this that in a hard case – when no such established rule can be found –
there is no legal obligation until the judge creates a new rule for the future." (TRS 44)

If we abandon the doctrine of (strong) discretion and treat principles as law, "…we raise the possibility that a
legal obligation might be imposed by a constellation of principles as well as by an established rule. We might want to
say that a legal obligation exists whenever the case supporting such an obligation, in terms of binding legal
principles of different sorts, is stronger than the case against it." (TRS 44)

The lecture: a theory put in the language lawyers and judges use is one reason Dworkin's theory was so successful.
Dworkin's controversial **one-right-answer thesis** — even in hard cases there is only one correct legal answer,
because of principles — is developed in *Law's Empire*.

## The Model of Rules II (TRS)

"Most legal philosophers have tried to answer this question by distinguishing the standards that properly figure in
arguments on behalf of legal rights and duties." (TRS 68)

What if we cannot identify an exclusive list of standards? "My point was not that 'the law' contains a fixed number of
standards, some of which are rules and other principles. Indeed, I want to oppose the idea that 'the law' is a fixed
set of standards of any sort" (TRS 76). So what is law? That is the first chapter of *Law's Empire*: 'What Is Law?'.

## Hart's Postscript

1. The Nature of Legal Theory — a theory of what law is which is both general and descriptive (CL 239)
2. The Nature of Legal Positivism — Hart's soft/inclusive legal positivism
3. The nature of Rules
4. Principles and the Rule of Recognition
5. Law and Morality — "According to my theory, the existence and content of the law can be identified by reference
   to the social sources of law (e.g. legislation, judicial decisions, social customs) without reference to morality
   except where the law thus identified has itself incorporated moral criteria for the identification of the law"
   (CL 269)
6. Judicial Discretion

The progression of the Postscript feels like the response to Dworkin's criticisms. The CL 269 passage is the social
sources thesis (see John Gardner, 'Legal Positivism: 5½ Myths', in the further readings).

## Hart's inclusive legal positivism

- Inclusive/Soft Legal Positivism vs Exclusive/Hard Legal Positivism. Does law necessarily set moral tests for legal
  validity?
- According to inclusive legal positivists (eg Hart), particular legal systems may make moral criteria necessary or
  sufficient for legal validity (in that system only).
- According to exclusive legal positivists (eg Raz), moral criteria can be neither sufficient nor necessary conditions
  for legal validity.

The lecture's example: a constitutional principle protecting equality lets a court invalidate a procedurally correct
but discriminatory law — substantive, not only formal, validity. For Hart this is a contingent connection, not a
necessary one. For Raz, once moral criteria are incorporated into the law they become legal criteria. The debate
(Bix 46–49) has been criticised as intramural; it all started because of Dworkin's criticisms.

## Legal disagreements (Law's Empire)

"Lawsuits always raise, at least in principle, three different kinds of issues: issues of fact, issues of law, and the
twinned issues of political morality and fidelity." (LE 3)

1. What happened? — empirical disagreement
2. What is the pertinent law? — "theoretical" disagreement about the law
3. If the law denies compensation (for example), is that unjust? — moral disagreement

'Fidelity' is Fuller's term from his exchange with Hart.

## The disagreement in Riggs v Palmer

- Was Elmer legally entitled to the inheritance that his grandfather's last will provided?
- How can judges disagree about what the law is, if the text of the statute is clear?
- General principles of law → principles of justice assumed elsewhere in the law.
- Two reasons: (1) it is sensible to assume that legislators have a general and diffuse intention to respect
  traditional principles of justice (unless they indicate the contrary); and (2) given that the statute is part of a
  larger system, it should be interpreted in a way that makes the legal system as a whole coherent in principle.

## Theoretical disagreement about law (LE)

- Law is a social phenomenon.
- Legal practice (unlike many other social phenomena) is argumentative.
- External vs internal perspective on the argumentative aspect of legal practice.
- "This book takes up the internal, participants' point of view; it tries to grasp the argumentative character of our
  legal practice by joining that practice and struggling with the issues of soundness and truth participants face. We
  will study formal legal argument from the judge's viewpoint…" (LE 14)

The argument against Hart: the rule of recognition exists because officials maintain it — a convergence of behaviour,
an agreement. How can judges agree on the criteria of validity and at the same time disagree on the content of the
law? If Hart is right, we should never see theoretical disagreements about law. (The lecturer: "I'm not saying that
Dworkin is right when he pushes this argument, but you must be able to see the argument.")

## Constructive interpretation

- Pre-interpretive stage
- Interpretive stage
- Post-interpretive stage

Dworkin's example: the practice of courtesy. We identify actions we associate with courtesy (letting people go first,
removing a hat); we ask what the point of the practice is; then we revisit the examples in light of that point. What
is the point of a social practice? What is the point of the law? For Dworkin, the point of the legal system is to
justify the exercise of state coercion. Hart's minimum conditions for the existence of a legal system say nothing
about its point: they are about structure.

## Law as integrity

Integrity → consistency in principle.

- The chain novel (LE 228).
- Judge Hercules, "…an imaginary judge of superhuman intellectual power and patience who accepts law as integrity"
  (LE 239).
- Dimension of fit + dimension of justification (LE 255).

Please reflect on: Dworkin's 'one-right-answer' thesis; integrity as an interpretative ideal. Cf. John Finnis, 'On
reason and authority in Law's Empire' (1987) 6 *Law and Philosophy* 357 — a natural lawyer who agrees with some of
Dworkin's criticisms of positivism but pushes against the one-right-answer thesis, on the incommensurability of fit and
justification.

For Kelsen or Hart, the structure of the system gives it unity; for Dworkin, principles and integrity do. Does law as
integrity work for evil legal systems — apartheid South Africa, say? Is it general jurisprudence or particular
jurisprudence, a theory that made sense for the United States?
