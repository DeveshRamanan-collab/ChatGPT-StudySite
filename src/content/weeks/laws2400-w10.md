## 1. Damage, causation and remoteness

Duty of care was Topics 5–8 and breach Topic 9. The third element of negligence is **damage** — not damages — suffered by the plaintiff. That damage must be **causally connected** to the breach of duty, and it must be **not too remote**. The burden is on the plaintiff to prove both on the balance of probabilities (CLA s 5E).

:::rule
Damage is the gist of the action: no damage, no action.
:::

Causation is a **factual** question: did the defendant's act or omission 'in fact' cause the harm? Remoteness is a **legal** question that involves policy considerations: if the breach caused (or contributed to) the injury, should the defendant be held legally responsible for it? The terminology has changed, but the common law principles still apply under the CLA. This week is factual causation; remoteness, or scope of liability, is next week.

:::statute
Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) s 5E: In proceedings relating to liability for negligence, the plaintiff always bears the onus of proving, on the balance of probabilities, any fact relevant to the issue of causation.
:::

:::examtip
Damage (the harm) is not damages (the money). Factual causation is about evidence — often expert evidence — of what can be proved happened, on the balance of probabilities; reasonable foreseeability belongs to breach and to scope of liability, not here.
:::

## 2. Causation: the map

The textbook's *Guide to Problem Solving* for causation (Tort Law Principles, 3rd ed, pp 433–435) is three linked flowcharts. **Causation 1** is the map: causation divides into **factual causation** and **scope of liability**.

- **Factual causation** → *Common law*: apply the 'but for' test. *Civil liability legislation*: apply the 'necessary condition' test — s 5D(1)(a).
- **Scope of liability** → *New intervening acts* AND *Remoteness*.

:::statute
Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) s 5D(1): A determination that negligence caused particular harm comprises the following elements— (a) that the negligence was a necessary condition of the occurrence of the harm (factual causation), and (b) that it is appropriate for the scope of the negligent person's liability to extend to the harm so caused (scope of liability).
:::

## 3. Factual causation at common law: the 'but for' test

The 'but for' test is the test for factual causation at common law. The court asks: would the plaintiff have suffered damage 'but for' the negligence of the defendant?

:::case
*Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital* [1969] 1 QB 428 — the deceased drank tea at work that was poisoned with arsenic; the duty doctor refused to see him because the doctor was also feeling unwell; later another doctor attended, but it was too late. Although the duty doctor was negligent, even timely treatment would not have saved the deceased's life: the 'but for' test was not met.
:::

The 'but for' test poses challenges where an intervening event breaks the chain of causation, and where multiple sufficient causes are involved.

:::case
*March v Stramare* (1991) 171 CLR 506 — the defendant parked a truck in the middle of a six-lane road in the early morning to unload fruit and vegetables, a practice used for a long time; the plaintiff, drunk and speeding, was injured when his car hit the truck. At trial the defendant was liable, with 70% contributory negligence against the plaintiff; the Full Court of South Australia put the entire responsibility on the plaintiff. The High Court held the defendant's negligence was the cause of the injury, but the judges disagreed about the reasoning.
:::

- **Mason CJ**: the 'but for' test was never the exclusive test of causation in negligence — it does not work where two or more acts or events are involved, or where the chain of causation is broken by an intervening event. Causation is a question of fact which 'must be determined by applying common sense to the facts of each particular case'.
- **McHugh J**: the 'but for' test should be the exclusive test, with a 'scope of the risk' test allowing policy factors to be employed as part of remoteness of damage.

## 4. Factual causation under the CLA

The **Ipp Report** identified problems with the common law approach, in particular references to causation as a 'common sense' test. Applying 'common sense' hides important policy choices: under what looks like a simple factual question, value judgments are made about who should be responsible, how far liability should extend and what is fair. Mixing those considerations into factual causation made the law less transparent and less predictable.

The CLA has restructured and codified the common law — the cases are still important. Section 5D(1)(a) asks whether the negligence was a **necessary condition** of the occurrence of the harm.

:::rule
But for = necessary condition. 'But for X, would Y have happened?' (common law language) and 'Was X necessary for Y to occur?' (statutory language) are two ways of saying the same thing, and courts often still use 'but for' language when applying s 5D(1)(a).
:::

:::case
*Adeels Palace v Moubarak* (2009) 239 CLR 420 — after a fight in a nightclub on New Year's Eve, Moubarak was shot by another patron; he argued the club was negligent in providing no security. In contrast to Mason CJ in *March*, s 5D(1) 'treats factual causation and scope of liability as separate and distinct issues', and factual causation is determined by the 'but for' test. The evidence did not show that security staff would have deterred or prevented the gunman's re-entry; that changing 'any of the circumstances in which the shootings occurred might have made a difference does not prove factual causation'. Nor was there any reason to treat it as an exceptional case under s 5D(2) [57].
:::

:::case
*Strong v Woolworths Ltd* (2012) 246 CLR 182 — Ms Strong, who walks with crutches, slipped outside the entrance to a Big W store when her crutch slid on a greasy chip; Woolworths had no system to inspect and clean the area. The District Court found negligence; the Court of Appeal reversed. In the High Court: duty (occupier/entrant); breach — no system for periodic inspection and cleaning (an appropriate system = inspection and removal of slipping hazards at intervals of not greater than 20 minutes); s 5D(1)(a) 'is a statutory statement of the "but for" test of causation'; and on the balance of probabilities (s 5E) 'the chip was deposited in the longer period between 8.00am and 12.10pm and not the shorter period between 12.10pm and the time of the fall' [38].
:::

### The flowchart's two columns (Causation 2)

Must find a connection between the defendant's breach of duty and the plaintiff's injury (*Adeels Palace*; *Strong v Woolworths*).

- **Common law — 'but for'.** Would the plaintiff's injuries have been suffered but for the defendant's negligence? *No* → satisfied. *Yes* → not satisfied.
- **Legislation — 'necessary condition', s 5D(1)(a).** Was the defendant's fault a necessary condition of the occurrence of the plaintiff's harm? *Yes* → satisfied. *No* → not satisfied.

:::examtip
The two questions are mirror images: "No" satisfies the 'but for' question and "Yes" satisfies the 'necessary condition' question. If the CLA applies, name s 5D(1)(a); using 'but for' as well is not an error — the courts do it all the time.
:::

A note on the flowchart says the provision "is the 'but for' test of causation at common law" (*Adeels Palace*, *Wallace v Kam*, *Cox v New South Wales*). It names "Section 5C(1)"; in the NSW Act the provision is s 5D(1)(a), which *Strong v Woolworths* calls the statutory statement of the 'but for' test.

### What the plaintiff would have done — s 5D(3)

:::statute
s 5D(3): If it is relevant to the determination of factual causation to determine what the person who suffered harm would have done if the negligent person had not been negligent— (a) the matter is to be determined subjectively in the light of all relevant circumstances, subject to paragraph (b), and (b) any statement made by the person after suffering the harm about what he or she would have done is inadmissible except to the extent (if any) that the statement is against his or her interest.
:::

### Dust disease claims sit outside the CLA

*Amaca v Booth* never mentions the CLA. Section 3B(1) excludes the Act from civil liability in dust disease proceedings under the Dust Diseases Tribunal Act 1989 (NSW), and from personal injury damages resulting from smoking or other use of tobacco products.

## 5. Scope of liability and intervening acts (introduced; Topic 11)

Once factual causation is established, s 5D(1)(b) asks: is it appropriate for liability to extend to this harm? That is a **normative** question, and s 5D(4) says the court must consider whether and why responsibility should be imposed. The flowchart gives scope of liability two parts joined by AND: **new intervening acts** and **remoteness**.

:::statute
s 5D(4): For the purpose of determining the scope of liability, the court is to consider (amongst other relevant things) whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed on the negligent party.
:::

**Novus actus interveniens**: a later event that exacerbates the original harm or causes new harm. It could be the plaintiff's own act, a third party's act, or a natural event. If the chain is broken, the original wrongdoer is not liable for harm after the break.

Not every later careless act severs the chain. In *March v Stramare* the truck created the very risk that materialised, so the driver's own negligence did not break it: "the defendant's wrongful conduct has generated the very risk of injury resulting from the negligence of the plaintiff or a third party" (Mason CJ).

:::case
*McKew v Holland Hannen & Cubitts Ltd* [1969] 3 All ER 1621 — the plaintiff's leg, injured by the defendant's negligence, was prone to give way. He went down a steep staircase without a handrail, unaided; the leg gave way and he suffered a severe ankle fracture. His unreasonable conduct was a novus actus: the chain was broken and the defendant was not liable for the second injury.
:::

## 6. When the 'but for' test is inconclusive

Sometimes evidential gaps make causation impossible to prove — for example, where scientific evidence cannot identify the cause. The 'but for' test also has limitations in complex cases. Mason CJ in *March v Stramare* noted three: an inability to distinguish between causes and necessary preconditions; situations involving an intervening event (Topic 11); and situations involving multiple sufficient causes.

### Multiple sufficient causes

The 'but for' test does not work where two or more events exist, each of which is sufficient to cause the harm. The Negligence Review Panel made no recommendations on these, so common law principles continue to apply. Courts have taken different approaches:

- *Cook v Lewis* (1961) (accidental shooting while hunting) — the Supreme Court of Canada held both negligent defendants liable, as the jury had been unable to decide which gun fired the single bullet that struck the plaintiff.
- **Market share liability** — where several defendants are potentially negligent, each pays a portion of the compensation in line with its market share.

## 7. Loss of a chance of a better medical outcome

:::case
*Tabet v Gett* (2010) 240 CLR 537 — a six-year-old child suffered permanent brain damage after a delay in performing a CT scan; she alleged the doctor's negligence deprived her of a chance of a better medical outcome. The issue was whether the court should "permit recovery for physical injury not shown to be caused or contributed to by a negligent party, but which negligence has deprived the victim of the possibility (but not the probability) of a better outcome" [25]. The chance was less than 50%: possible, not probable.
:::

Gummow ACJ: in personal injury cases the law of negligence as understood in the common law of Australia does not entertain an action for recovery where the damage is characterised as the loss of a chance of a better outcome [46]. That does not require acceptance, in absolute terms, that destroying the chance of obtaining a benefit or avoiding a harm can never supply the damage that is the gist of an action in negligence [27].

## 8. Dust disease cases: increased risk or material contribution?

Proving causation is hard in dust disease cases, where the science cannot say which exposure caused the disease. The key question: is increasing the risk of harm enough, or must the defendant's conduct have contributed to the harm?

| Increased risk of harm | Material contribution to harm |
| --- | --- |
| Probabilistic: the conduct made harm more likely | Causal: the conduct was part of how the harm came about |
| Looks forward, before the harm occurs | Looks back, after the harm has occurred |
| Says nothing about what actually happened to this claimant | Must be more than negligible |

:::case
*Bonnington Castings v Wardlaw* [1956] AC 613 — pneumoconiosis from cumulative inhalation of silica dust, only part of it from the employer's negligence. It was enough that the negligent exposure made a contribution that was more than de minimis (negligible); the claimant need not show it was the sole or main cause.
:::

:::case
*Amaca v Ellis* (2010) 240 CLR 111 — lung cancer in a claimant who had been exposed to asbestos and had also smoked. Showing that exposure increased the risk is not sufficient: the claimant must prove, on the balance of probabilities, that the exposure actually contributed to the cancer, and the evidence could not establish this.
:::

:::case
*Amaca v Booth* (2011) 246 CLR 36 — mesothelioma after 30 years of exposure to asbestos in brake linings. The Tribunal drew an "overwhelming inference" of causation, upheld by the Court of Appeal and the High Court. French CJ: the exposure to asbestos in brake linings manufactured by Amaca not only prospectively increased the risk of the disease but, in the event, [causally] contributed to its development and continuation. A causal connection may be inferred from medical and expert evidence; French CJ referred to the 'Bradford Hill criteria' as circumstantial evidence of a causal relationship between asbestos and mesothelioma.
:::

Why the inference was available in *Booth*: asbestos is effectively the only known cause of mesothelioma; Booth had about 27 years of heavy brake-lining exposure and his other exposures were trivial by comparison; and expert evidence accepted at trial was that cumulative exposure contributes to the disease. Science could not say which exposure triggered it, but the inference of causation was reasonably open. In *Ellis*, by contrast: limited exposure, mainly epidemiological (risk) evidence, and a competing cause (smoking).

*Fairchild v Glenhaven* [2003] 1 AC 32 (UK): materially increasing the risk was treated as enough where the claimant could not prove which employer's exposure caused the disease — a policy-based exception to the ordinary causation rules. Australian common law has not adopted a risk-based test in that form.

## 9. Section 5D(2): exceptional cases

Based on the dust disease cases, the Ipp Report recommended that the CLA contain an exceptional case provision; it is now s 5D(2), an exception to the s 5D(1)(a) requirement.

:::statute
s 5D(2): In determining in an exceptional case, in accordance with established principles, whether negligence that cannot be established as a necessary condition of the occurrence of harm should be accepted as establishing factual causation, the court is to consider (amongst other relevant things) whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed on the negligent party.
:::

In practice s 5D(2) has rarely been considered by the courts, so there is some uncertainty about how it operates. *Adeels Palace* found it did not apply. And s 3B(1) excludes the CLA from harm related to dust and tobacco products, which special statutes such as the Dust Diseases Tribunal Act 1989 (NSW) deal with — so the NSW dust disease claims that prompted it do not reach s 5D(2).

### The flowchart's account (Causation 3)

Causation 2 ends with cases where the test yields unsatisfactory results — **multiple sufficient causes** and **material contribution to harm** — and Causation 3 says s 5D(2) can establish factual causation in "an appropriate case". Verify: the NSW text says "an exceptional case"; cite the NSW words.

- **Multiple sufficient causes** — "the cumulative operation of factors in the occurrence of the total harm in circumstances in which the contribution of each factor to that harm is unascertainable" — eg *Bonnington Castings v Wardlaw*, *March v Stramare*.
- **Material contribution to harm** — "the negligent conduct that materially increases the risk of harm in circumstances in which the state of scientific or medical knowledge makes it impossible to prove the cause of the plaintiff's harm" — eg *Fairchild*, *McGee v National Coal Board*, *Amaca Pty Ltd v Ellis*, *Henville v Walker*.

## 10. Contribution between tortfeasors ([12.170])

Where multiple tortfeasors are jointly and severally liable for damage, the plaintiff may choose to recover the whole amount against only one of them — for example, the person with the "deeper pockets". A footnote qualifies this where the proportionate liability provisions apply, in respect of claims for property damage and economic loss (those provisions are discussed earlier in the chapter, not in these pages).

There is a statutory mechanism, known as **contribution**, by which a tortfeasor held liable for the full amount of damage may seek a contribution against another tortfeasor. In NSW the textbook cites the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1946 (NSW) s 5(1)(c) and s 5(2).

:::rule
Contribution in no way affects the plaintiff; it is a matter between the tortfeasors themselves. So where there are joint or separate, concurrent tortfeasors, the risk that one of them is insolvent or not able to pay the full amount of damages lies with the other tortfeasors, not the plaintiff.
:::
