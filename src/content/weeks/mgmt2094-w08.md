## From creativity to innovation

The first half of semester was about creativity; the second half is about innovation, and the two are different.
Innovations range from very small, incremental ones — within a team, or in how you do things in your own life — to
large, revolutionary innovations that change the face of a whole industry, and maybe the culture of a society. They
differ in scope and level, and in the magnitude of the intervention.

**Types of innovation** (the slide is a sample; you can come up with more): management, revolutionary, service,
experiential, value, business model, structural, market, process, strategic, evolutionary, application, marketing,
user-led, product, technological, supply chain, brand and disruptive innovation. For the group innovation project,
choosing one type is probably sufficient.

## What is an innovation?

- It is an idea, practice or object that is perceived as new by an individual or other unit of adoption.
- It is a use of new knowledge to offer a new product or service that customers want. Thus, it is invention +
  commercialisation.
- Shaver (2014): innovation is the search for, and the discovery, development, improvement, adoption and
  commercialisation of, new processes, new products and new organisation structures and procedures.

Who perceives it as new? Most likely customers, but we can relax that focus: for an NGO it is any stakeholder — the
people it is trying to help, its donors, or another organisation it works with or wants to form an alliance with.

:::definition
**Innovation** = invention + commercialisation. If you cannot commercialise it, it is not innovation: you can be
extremely creative — a product, a piece of art, a book — but without commercialisation that is creativity, not
innovation.
:::

## Characteristics of innovation

- There is an object or target which is being changed: a product, a process, an individual's lifestyle, an
  organisation's strategy, a society's culture.
- Innovations vary in extent or magnitude, i.e. the degree to which one deviates from the past.
- It is closely related to problem solving, since the generation and implementation of ideas for change never
  transpire without difficulty.
- A final characteristic is the impact of the change: the significance or range of its effects.

The lecture's examples of innovation changing culture and lifestyle: the mobile internet, which changed our lives and
culture immensely, for good and bad (it is easy to communicate and order, but there is also alienation and anxiety);
and supermarkets, which over the past few decades changed shopping from many stops, made often, to one stop that lasts
several days. Organisations rarely innovate without a need: there is usually a problem to solve, often driven by
changes in the external environment — technological, legal, government.

## Desirable, possible, viable

Innovation sits where three circles overlap: what is **desirable** to users? what is **possible** with technology?
what is **viable** in the marketplace? Lots of things are desirable, but not all of them are viable in the
marketplace, and not everything is possible with the technology we have.

The lecture's example is the jetpack. It has long been desirable — especially for rescue missions, firefighters and
military operations — but only now is the technology coming into fruition, on a very limited basis, and it is
extremely expensive. Hold all three together: innovation is not just new ideas or desirability, because it needs to
be commercialised.

## Why innovate?

Turbulent and rapidly changing economies and organisations must prepare to innovate on a continuing basis, otherwise
their survival chances are seriously threatened (eg Apple). Innovation is difficult, so organisations do it mostly
when there is pressure: things are shifting, people want new things, competitors fight for the same customers and
technology changes rapidly.

The lecture's illustration: Apple introduced the iPhone and captured much of the market very quickly, while the
former leader in mobile phones lost sight of how the environment was changing — not only the technology of the phones
themselves, but the infrastructure around them: apps, platforms, social networking and the features people wanted.

## Goals of innovation

Improving quality · creation of new markets · extension of the product range · reducing labour costs · improving the
production process · reducing materials · reducing environmental damage · replacement of products/services · reducing
energy consumption · conformance to regulations.

Goals can go hand in hand: reduce environmental damage by reducing materials and improving production processes, and
you may reduce labour costs as well. Conformance to regulations is interesting: regulations change all the time, and
sometimes organisations conform to regulations they do not have to uphold, then advertise it to improve their image
and status and be viewed as a more legitimate actor. Innovation can find ways to conform at the least cost and reap
the most rewards.

## Sources of innovation

- **Organisational structure** — organic structures positively influence innovation, as there is lower vertical
  differentiation, formalisation and centralisation. Organic organisations facilitate flexibility, adaptation and
  cross-fertilisation.
- **Long tenure in management** — managerial tenure apparently provides legitimacy and knowledge of how to accomplish
  a task and obtain desired outcomes.
- **Slack resources** — having an abundance of resources allows an organisation to afford to purchase innovations,
  bear the cost of instituting innovation and absorb failures.
- **Intra-communications** — innovative organisations are high users of committees, task forces and cross-functional
  teams that facilitate interaction across departmental lines.

Tenure and slack are double-edged. A CEO who has been there a long time knows the organisation, its challenges and
its industry, but may be complacent and happy with things as they are. Abundant resources let you experiment and fail
until you find the right solution, but they can also make an organisation feel unthreatened: firms at the top of the
world that felt unbeatable did not invest in innovation, and failed.

## The innovation map

Two axes: internal–external (vertical) and continuous–discontinuous (horizontal).

| Quadrant | Position | Labels around it on the map |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operational innovation | internal, continuous | technological innovation, processes |
| Management innovation | internal, discontinuous | partner interfaces, internal structures and processes, networking, people management |
| Product & service innovation | external, continuous | brand, technological innovation |
| Strategic innovation | external, discontinuous | disruptive innovation, supply chain, customer interfaces and experiences, Blue Ocean Strategy, new offerings, channels, new markets |

Operational innovation is continuous and internal: nobody really sees much of it. Management innovation should not
change all the time, because people get tired of constant change: change it once in a while when it is appropriate,
let it sit so people get used to it and the rewards are reaped, and change it again when it is no longer appropriate
for the environment. Product and service innovation — your products, how you sell them, how you present your brand —
needs to be continuous. Strategic innovation is discontinuous: changing strategy is difficult, but necessary when it
is called upon.

## Difference between innovation and invention

| Invention | Innovation |
| --- | --- |
| Creation of a new product, service or process | Introduction of a new product, service or process into the marketplace |
| May not be commercialised | Results in commercialisation |
| Can be autonomous or induced | Usually induced |
| Can be for an economic or non-economic motive | Economic motive |
| Usually restricted to the R&D centre | Spreads across the organisation |
| May bring few changes in the organisation | Brings organisational change |
| Precedes innovation | Succeeds invention |

People can invent without an economic motive — as a hobby or a passion — and an invention may simply stay in R&D.
When engineers' invention becomes an innovation, it can change the whole organisation: marketing, management, the
supply chain, the production line, accounting and finance.

## Innovation process

1. Recognising or scanning the environment.
2. Aligning the overall business strategy and the proposed innovation.
3. Acquiring technology from outside.
4. Generating technology in-house.
5. Exploring and selecting the most suitable response to the environment.
6. Executing and implementing innovation.
7. Learning lessons for improvement.
8. Developing the organisation.

Usually a rocky road. Alignment asks whether the innovation fits who we are and makes the strategy stronger, or
whether the strategy must change. Steps 3 and 4 are a choice: acquire the technology, generate it in-house, or some
hybrid. As we learn the lessons, we develop the organisation and add to its capabilities, so it becomes more
competitive.

## Innovation occurs at three levels

- National level → government policies and support.
- Enterprise level → enterprise policies, support and initiatives.
- Individual level → individual and group innovation activities.

Government policy affects the enterprise, and the enterprise affects individuals and groups. A solution a team finds
at the individual or group level may rise to the organisational level.

## What is innovation management?

1. Learning to find the most appropriate solution to the problem, and consistently managing that process in the ways
   best suited to the particular circumstances in which the organisation finds itself.
2. The search for effective routines.
3. Managing the learning process to deal with the challenge of the innovation process.

Innovation, by definition, changes routines, and that is very difficult: people at every level — personally, as a
team, as a department — have been doing things the same way for years.

**Organisational process for innovation management:** market learning → effectively building business models →
creating a cause and not a business → designing an open market for ideas, capital and talent → listening to new
voices → setting unreasonable expectations and stretching the business definition → building partnership and
alliances → lowering the risks of experimentation → paying the innovators well. Unreasonable expectations, because we
want something unconventional that wasn't there before; partnerships can be with a community or an NGO, not only
other businesses; experimenting first lowers the risk; and paying the innovators well is not necessarily just
monetary — there must be a good result for the organisation and for the people involved.

## Value innovation (VI)

- One of the most prominent programmatic approaches to innovation currently in use.
- It is different from building layers of competitive advantage, and does not mean segmenting the market and
  accommodating customer needs.
- Its main focus is on offering products and services which create a superior buyer value in existing markets and
  enable a quantum leap for the firm to create new markets.
- Value innovation also differs from technology innovation: new technology does not become a value innovation unless
  it is cheap enough for mass buyers.

The lecture's examples were Uber and Airbnb. For Uber the market was already there — people needed to get places —
and the cars, the drivers and the internet were all already there; Uber rethought the value. It was not necessarily
the very first to think of this, but it was the most successful implementation, and it changed the culture as well.

## Let's ask the "What if" questions…

- Many times we get held back by the status quo, which stifles imagination. Overcome it by asking "What if"
  questions of conventional assumptions.
- With the right business model ingredients, what we think of as impossible may just be doable. These questions
  should provoke us, challenge our thinking, disturb us.
- Eg what if newspapers stopped printing altogether and went to purely digital distribution? What are the issues,
  the benefits and the costs?
- "What if" questions are merely starting points: the challenge is to find the business model that could make the
  suggestion work. Not all will be answered; some will be too provocative.

**Examples of "What if" questions that got answered** (Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2010): IKEA introduced flat packs and
DIY furniture assembly in the 1950s. In 2003 Skype launched free voice calling over the internet; after five years it
had 400 million registered users who had made 100 billion free calls. Grameenphone, a partnership with Grameen Bank
the micro-financier, asked what if every Bangladeshi villager had access to a telephone; at its inception Bangladesh
had the lowest teledensity, and Grameenphone is now the country's largest taxpayer.

## The Ideation Tool Kit

According to Mattimore (2012), there are seven tools:

1. **Questioning assumptions** — question why it is. Decide how to frame your creative challenge, generate 20–30
   assumptions you might be making (true or false), then pick several as thought starters and creative triggers. To
   increase sales of women's electric razors by 30%, the assumptions include: mostly women buy their razors; a razor
   is bought primarily for what it does; the target market is women aged 25–49; razors should last five years;
   department stores are the main channel. Questioning them: what if we targeted teenage girls with an inexpensive
   razor — what would it look like?
2. **Opportunity redefinition** — take the opportunity statement ("How do we sell more insurance to Catholics?"),
   pick the three most interesting words and generate 8–10 creative alternatives for each (the who, what, where, when
   or how: who is "we"? what is "sell"? what constitutes a "Catholic"?), then randomly combine them into a new
   statement: "How do we get practising young Catholics to be incentivised to sell life insurance to Catholic
   grandparents?"
3. **Wishing** — start by wishing for the impossible, then figure out a way to make it, or some of it, happen. "We
   cannot know what's possible without first considering the impossible" (Arthur C. Clarke). Money, energy and time
   are no object. The facilitating leader has the team generate 20–30 wishes; the team then uses the more fantastic
   ones as creative stimuli for novel but realistic ideas. Maybe the Uber concept came from wishing for more
   convenient transport?
4. **Triggered brainwalking** — lends itself to being combined with other techniques and ensures everyone gets the
   chance to contribute ideas. Think of vanilla ice cream: delicious by itself, but add banana, chocolate sauce and
   sprinkles and it becomes something very different and special. (In the lecture: a different flavour of
   brainstorming, with ideas written on sheets of paper as people move around the room.)
5. **Semantic intuition** — combine several categories of key words to create a name for a new idea, possibly before
   you have any idea what the concept will be. Go beyond the literal meaning; the words are only stimuli, and it does
   not matter whether you use any or all of them. Problem: how can we improve our teamwork? Keywords: teamwork,
   improve. Synonyms: meeting, information exchange; advance, upgrade. Combination: upgrade information exchange.
   Solution: upgrade information software.
6. **Picture prompts** — visual techniques bring intuitions, emotions and feelings to the surface; good for personnel
   and management innovation challenges. Customise the visual to the challenge (for a manufacturing problem,
   industrial scenes and things being made, manipulated or transformed) and also use some random pictures: people
   make uncanny connections between seemingly random or irrelevant stimuli.
7. **Worst idea** — get people to create collectively a list of really terrible ideas, then turn them into good ideas
   by thinking of the opposite, or finding something of value in the idea. This takes the creative pressure off the
   team, and is also a bit of fun, with positive results.

## The ideation process

The ideation process can take several forms to come up with an innovative business model. Key questions:

- **Is your team sufficiently diverse** to generate fresh business model ideas? Diversity in age, seniority,
  experience, customer knowledge. (The lecture: a 20-year veteran may be stuck in the old routines, while an intern
  of two days may have great ideas.)
- **Which elements must be studied** before generating business model ideas? An immersion stage: general research,
  knowing your customer well, scrutinising new technologies.
- **What innovations are we imagining** for each business model building block? Expand the range of possible
  solutions, generating as many ideas as possible: quantity, not quality. Brainstorming rules are paramount.
- **What are the most important criteria** for prioritising the business model ideas? Criteria specific to the
  business, eg revenue potential, impact on competitive advantage, to reduce the ideas to a manageable few.
- **What does the complete business model look like** for each shortlisted idea? A shortlist of three to five
  potential business innovations.

## Reading: Brown and Wyatt (2010), Design thinking for social innovation

Shanti, near Hyderabad, fetches free water from a local borehole that periodically makes her family sick, rather than
buying safer water from the Naandi Foundation's treatment centre. The centre is close, affordable and well known, and
habit is not the reason: she cannot carry the 5-gallon jerrican the centre requires, her husband works until after it
closes, and she would have to buy a monthly card for more water than she needs. The centre produces clean water very
well, but its designers did not consider the culture and needs of everyone in the community. Social challenges need
systemic solutions grounded in the client's or customer's needs.

Design thinking incorporates constituent or consumer insights in depth and rapid prototyping, all aimed at getting
beyond the assumptions that block effective solutions. It is human centred, inherently optimistic, constructive and
experiential, and it crosses public, for-profit and non-profit sectors. The process is a system of overlapping spaces
rather than a sequence of orderly steps, and projects may loop back through them more than once:

- **Inspiration** — the problem or opportunity that motivates the search. It starts with a brief (a framework,
  benchmarks and objectives; too narrow a brief almost guarantees an incremental, mediocre outcome), then going into
  the world to observe what people actually do, since people often cannot say what they need. Kara Pecknold reframed
  a brief to build a website for Rwandan weavers once she found they had little access to computers.
- **Ideation** — synthesising what was seen and heard into insights, then divergent thinking by diverse,
  multidisciplinary, "T-shaped" people (depth of skill, plus empathy for people and disciplines beyond their own).
  Brainstorming defers judgement and discourages the devil's advocate. "To have a good idea you must first have lots
  of ideas" (Linus Pauling).
- **Implementation** — prototyping at the core: ideas turned into products and services that are tested, iterated
  and refined, then a communication strategy. VisionSpring found that children who were upset by traditional eye
  tests were at ease when they screened each other.

Other examples: the Sternins' positive deviance work in Vietnam found solutions among very poor families whose
children were well nourished (tiny shrimps, crabs and snails from the rice paddies, sweet-potato greens, several small
meals a day). One of the biggest impediments to design thinking is fear of failure: experimentation and failure are
fine as long as they happen early and are a source of learning.

## Reading: Reed (2018), Inside Lego's quest to create a plastic-free brick

Lego wants to eliminate its dependence on petroleum-based plastics and build its toys entirely from plant-based or
recycled materials by 2030, and to keep its packaging out of landfill by 2025. The challenge is designing blocks that
click together yet separate easily, retain bright colours and survive a laundry load or a parent's foot: in essence,
to switch the ingredients but keep the product exactly the same.

Lego is investing about 1 billion kroner and hiring about 100 people; researchers have experimented with around 200
alternatives to ABS. Lego already uses polyethylene made from sugar-cane husks, but only in flexible pieces such as
dragon wings, palm trees and fishing rods (1–2 per cent of output), and it is too soft for bricks. Most test materials
have fallen short: broken bricks with sharp edges, muddied colours, misshapen or pockmarked bricks. The chief
executive said it was difficult to know the financial implications, but "it's an agenda that we want to drive".

## Class activity: Is it really disruptive innovation?

Not every successful or radical innovation is disruptive. In Clayton Christensen's theory, disruptive innovation
usually begins by serving an overlooked, low-end or new-market customer segment with a simpler, cheaper or more
accessible offering, and then improves enough to challenge established firms.

Five questions: **Incumbent** — what established company, industry or business model is potentially being
disrupted? **Initial customer** — who did the innovation initially appeal to; was this customer underserved,
overlooked or a non-consumer? **Value proposition** — what made it cheaper, simpler, more convenient or accessible?
**Market movement** — did it start outside the mainstream and subsequently move toward mainstream customers?
**Verdict** — disruptive innovation, potentially disruptive, or not disruptive? Defend your decision.

The cases are questions to investigate, not settled answers: Netflix vs traditional video rental; Airbnb vs hotels
(disruptive, or simply a new platform business model?); Uber vs taxis; Canva vs professional design software (a new
market of non-designers?); Spotify vs CDs and downloads (where did the disruption actually occur?); Tesla vs
traditional car manufacturers; ChatGPT vs traditional search and knowledge work (could it become disruptive, and to
whom?).
