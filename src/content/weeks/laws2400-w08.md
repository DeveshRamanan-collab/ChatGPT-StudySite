## 1. Overview

This week we look at how the duty of care requirements change for certain special parties, and unpack specific
rules concerning such special parties. Lecture 1 deals with public authorities; Lecture 2 deals with landlords,
occupiers, immune defendants (legal advocates), children and third parties.

The paradigm case is an act by an ordinary person causing physical injury or property damage, where reasonable
foreseeability does most of the work. Where we have special parties — and in particular public authorities — we
need more than reasonable foreseeability, and the question is what more.

## 2. Public authorities

Public authorities are bodies created by statutes to perform certain public functions or act in the public interest.
We have already met several: the police (officers of the State, which can be vicariously liable for what a police
officer does negligently), the Conservation Commission in *Romeo*, and the council and the State in *Graham Barclay
Oysters*.

If a public authority does not act in the public interest or abuses its discretion, a remedy can be sought under
administrative law. Moreover, as there is no Crown immunity now from tortious liability, public authorities may also
be sued for negligence or other torts. But the threshold is much higher, at both the duty stage and the breach stage,
and the discussion often slides between the two.

:::rule
There are differences between a private person and a public authority as a defendant: the nature and
responsibilities of public authorities; public authorities exercise their functions as per statutory provisions; and
courts take note of public policy considerations relevant to public authorities.
:::

The lecture's biggest reason is resources. A public authority does not have unlimited money, and a court that says
it should have spent money on one thing is telling the executive how to allocate limited (taxpayers') funds — a
separation of powers problem. Indeterminacy of liability is the other big policy worry. A statutory authority's
powers are in its statute, so we sometimes need to look at what those powers are; the general presumption is that
there is no duty of care in a lot of circumstances, and what the authority did typically needs to have been quite
bad.

### From Anns to Heyman: policy, operations and reliance

Until the decision in *Anns v Merton London Borough Council* (1978), public authorities in the UK were not liable for
negligence for a failure to exercise a power. Much of this topic is in that space: the authority had the power to do
something, chose not to, and somebody lost because it did not — an omission, which is especially problematic.
Australian courts have employed diverse approaches to determine whether to impose a duty of care on public
authorities, looking at the circumstances of the case and the relationship between the authority and the person
harmed.

In *Sutherland Shire Council v Heyman* (1985) 157 CLR 424, the High Court considered the policy vs operation
distinction and the concept of general reliance (e.g., fire fighting). In the lecture's account, purchasers
discovered defective footings in their house years after it was built and sued the council over its inspection role
during construction; no duty was held because there was no reliance.

:::definition
Policy is high level: the decision to run or regulate an industry in a particular way — "this is our approach".
Operations are how you go about meeting that policy objective, and are far more detailed. Policy is pretty much off
limits for a duty of care, because of the separation of powers: setting policy is the executive's job. If there is
negligence in the execution of operations, you are more likely to be able to at least argue a duty, though there
are still hurdles.
:::

### Crimmins: a duty owed

:::case
*Crimmins v Stevedoring Industry Finance Committee* (1999) 200 CLR 1 — Crimmins worked as a waterside worker at Port
Melbourne 1961–65; stevedoring operations were regulated by the Australian Stevedoring Industry Authority, which
directed workers where to work; the Authority was later abolished and the SIFC assumed its liabilities; in 1997
Crimmins was diagnosed with mesothelioma caused by unloading asbestos cargoes. The questions before the High Court:
did the Authority owe him a duty of care, and did its liability pass to the SIFC? The High Court held that a duty of
care was owed because the Authority/SIFC had an overarching supervisory and regulatory role regarding safety, and
individual employers were required to supply safety equipment.
:::

The Authority was not Crimmins's employer — it was the regulator, sitting on top of the employers. (If a statutory
authority is your employer, a duty is much easier to establish.) The SIFC is the defendant only because it took
over; it stands in the same shoes as the Authority.

McHugh J observed: "The statutory authority owed a duty of care to the worker because it directed him to places of
work where there were risks of injury of which the authority was, or ought to have been, aware and in respect of
which, the authority knew or ought to have known that the worker was specially vulnerable. The worker's
vulnerability arose as a result of the casual nature of his employment and his obligation to obey the authority's
directions as to where he worked." Read it for knowledge (was, or ought to have been, aware), control (directing him
where to work) and vulnerability (a casual worker who refused a direction would lose the job).

### McHugh J's six questions

Judges in the majority disagreed on how the duty of care issue should be approached: start with the common law and
then look at the statute, or begin with an interpretation of the statute. McHugh J outlined six questions which
should determine whether an affirmative duty of care should be owed by a statutory authority in a novel case:

1. Was it reasonably foreseeable that an act or omission of the defendant, including a failure to exercise its
   statutory powers, would result in injury to the plaintiff?
2. By reason of its statutory obligations, did the defendant have the power to protect a specific class including
   the plaintiff (rather than the public at large) from a risk of harm?
3. Was the plaintiff vulnerable, in the sense that she could not reasonably be expected to adequately safeguard
   herself from harm?
4. Did the defendant know, or ought the defendant to have known, of the risk of harm to the specific class
   (including the plaintiff) if it did not exercise its powers?
5. Would such a duty impose liability with respect to the defendant's exercise of "core policy making" or
   "quasi-legislative" functions?
6. Are there any other supervening reasons in policy to deny the existence of a duty of care?

Reasonable foreseeability is always needed, but for a public authority it is not sufficient. The questions do not
all need to be answered "yes": understand the substance of each and what it says about a duty — a "yes" to
questions 5 or 6 points against one. The text combines them into four questions; the substance is the same.

On the other hand, Hayne J (in dissent) rejected that SIFC owed any duty of care: "The Authority owed no duty of care
to the deceased worker in deciding whether or not to exercise that [i.e., issuing an order requiring the use of
respirators] quasi-legislative power." He reasoned that imposing "such a duty of care on the Authority would have
affected the way in which the body went about its task". Very often there is no clear answer in this area — the
marks are in the arguing.

:::examtip
Where the common law and the Civil Liability Act are both relevant, the lecturer recommends starting with the common
law (it was there first and is the more complete body of law) and then looking at what the CLA says — this area is
"common law modified by statute". Even the High Court has not agreed on the order, and in an exam the order matters
less than the engagement; but you need to look at both. Use your own words: precise language for legal principles,
short key phrases from judgments, never slabs of quotation.
:::

### Graham Barclay Oysters: no duty

The decision in *Graham Barclay Oysters v Ryan* (2002) 211 CLR 540 is also relevant to a public authority's duty of
care. Consumers became ill after eating oysters contaminated by sewage; Ryan sued Graham Barclay, the Great Lakes
Council and the NSW government for negligence. The High Court by majority held that no duty of care was owed by the
Council or the NSW government. (Graham Barclay, as producer and distributor, owed a duty but did not breach it.)

Gleeson CJ noted that the complaint was "not about acts, but about omissions" — that is, alleged non-feasance on the
part of public authorities. The tortious liability of governments is different from that of ordinary citizens
because of the nature and responsibilities of governments. He raised doubts about the State's managerial control
over the oyster industry, and asked: "Why could not the State have relied on self-regulation by the industry itself?
… By what criterion can a court determine the reasonableness of a government's decision to allow an industry a
substantial measure of self-regulation?" Allowing the industry to self-regulate was a policy decision.

Regarding the Council, Gleeson CJ noted that the "content of the duty" raises difficulties: the "powers conferred
upon the Council … were conferred for the benefit of the public generally; not for the protection of a specific class
of persons". On the other hand, Gummow and Hayne JJ denied a duty on the part of the Council because it exercised a
much less significant degree of control over the risk of harm that eventuated — "the factor of control is of
fundamental importance". The sewage could come from many places outside the Council's control.

Compare *Crimmins*: there the Authority was much closer, with much more control, and Crimmins was very vulnerable to
its decisions. In *Graham Barclay Oysters* there is a big gap between the authorities and the plaintiffs — not the
vulnerability, definitely not the control, and really not the knowledge. With a hypothetical, ask whether your facts
are closer to *Crimmins* or to *Graham Barclay Oysters*, and why.

### Amaca v NSW: five propositions

:::case
*Amaca v NSW* [2004] NSWCA 124 — Hay sued his employer, Rolls Royce, and Pacific Power for exposure to asbestos; both
defendants cross-claimed against James Hardie, the manufacturer of asbestos; James Hardie was held liable, but it
cross-claimed against the State of NSW. The cross-claim against the State was rejected at trial and by the Court of
Appeal; the High Court reversed on the principles and remitted; on remitter the Court of Appeal again held no duty
was owed (that decision, in S&V, is optional).
:::

The Court of Appeal synthesised the following five propositions from past decisions:

1. The totality of the relationship between the parties is the proper basis to determine the duty of care.
2. The category of control that may contribute to the existence of a duty of care includes control of any situation
   that contains a risk of harm to others.
3. A duty of care does not arise merely because an authority might have prevented harm by exercising a statutory
   power.
4. The existence of statutory powers and the mere prior exercise of those powers from time to time do not create a
   duty to exercise those powers in future.
5. Knowledge that harm may result from a failure to exercise statutory power is not itself sufficient to create a
   duty of care.

The Court of Appeal held that the State of NSW did not owe a duty of care. James Hardie and others had no less
knowledge than the State of the potential dangers of asbestos dust. The control exercised by the State was much less
than that enjoyed by Rolls Royce (employer), Pacific Power (site owner) and James Hardie (supplier). The plaintiffs
were not vulnerable to the State, as they could protect themselves by relying on their employers, the suppliers and
the owners and occupiers of the property involved. And imposing a duty of care would impose a massive obligation on
the State.

:::statute
*Amaca* is decided at common law because it is a dust disease case: CLA s 3B(1)(b) provides that the Act does not
apply to civil liability in proceedings of the kind referred to in s 11 of the Dust Diseases Tribunal Act 1989 (the
whole Act except ss 15A, 15B and part of s 18(1)), and s 40(3) confirms Part 5 does not apply to liability excluded by
s 3B.
:::

## 3. Impact of the Civil Liability Act (Part 5)

The Ipp Committee's recommendations were aimed at reducing the liability of public authorities for negligence only
to their acts which were so unreasonable that no reasonable public authority would take them. This draws from the
Wednesbury unreasonableness principle used in administrative law — a much higher threshold than the ordinary
standard. Pretty much everything about the CLA is about limiting liability, and it should be interpreted with that
purpose in mind.

CLA Part 5 (ss 40–46) applies to civil liability in tort (s 40). It is not in the textbook; it is covered in the S&V
extract on the reading list. The CLA restricts the duty of care for public authorities — akin to the approach
adopted by the Australian courts.

### Who is a public authority (s 41)

:::statute
Section 41 defines a "public or other authority" — e.g., the Crown, a government department, a public health
organisation, a local council, and any public or local authority constituted by or under an Act. "Any person having
public official functions or acting in a public official capacity (whether or not employed as a public official)" is
also a public authority, but only in relation to those functions.
:::

This means that a private body or person may also be regarded as a public authority: psychiatrists operating under
the Mental Health Act 2007, people putting warning signs by roads, and prison guards.

### Resources (s 42)

:::statute
Section 42 stipulates principles that apply in determining whether a public authority has a duty of care or has
breached it: (a) "the functions required to be exercised by the authority are limited by the financial and other
resources that are reasonably available to the authority" for exercising those functions; (b) the general allocation
of those resources by the authority is not open to challenge; (c) "the functions required to be exercised by the
authority are to be determined by reference to the broad range of its activities (and not merely by reference to the
matter to which the proceedings relate)"; (d) the authority may rely on evidence of its compliance with general
procedures and applicable standards.
:::

Section 42 pulls the resources point into the statute: the courts cannot tell a public authority what it should be
spending money on. In *Romeo*, one argument was that the coastline should have been fenced; one reason that was not
reasonable is that the court cannot tell the authority to spend its money that way.

### The "so unreasonable" threshold (ss 43 and 43A)

:::statute
Section 43 provides that "an act or omission of the authority does not constitute a breach of statutory duty unless
the act or omission was in the circumstances so unreasonable that no authority having the functions of the authority
in question could properly consider the act or omission to be a reasonable exercise of its functions". Under s 43A,
the same principle applies to the exercise of special statutory powers by public authorities — a power conferred by
or under a statute "of a kind that persons generally are not authorised to exercise without specific statutory
authority".
:::

The lecturer's examples of special statutory powers are a fire service's power to back-burn and the issuing of
firearm licences: powers very limited in who can exercise them, not routine activity.

### Regulatory functions and exercising functions (ss 44 and 46)

:::statute
Section 44(1): "A public or other authority is not liable in proceedings for civil liability to which this Part
applies to the extent that the liability is based on the failure of the authority to exercise or to consider
exercising any function of the authority to prohibit or regulate an activity if the authority could not have been
required to exercise the function in proceedings instituted by the plaintiff." Under s 44(2), a function to issue a
licence, permit or other authority, or to register or otherwise authorise a person, is a function to regulate the
activity.
:::

:::statute
Section 46: "the fact that a public or other authority exercises or decides to exercise a function does not of itself
indicate that the authority is under a duty to exercise the function or that the function should be exercised in
particular circumstances or in a particular way."
:::

Sections 44 and 46 say the same thing as the common law already did: the power, or its past exercise, is not
enough — there needs to be more (control, knowledge, reliance). That is how the common law and the CLA intersect. The
lecturer also noted that potholes are covered by legislation removing the obligation; in the CLA, s 45 gives roads
authorities special non-feasance protection unless they had actual knowledge of the particular risk.

### Common law and the CLA together: RTA v Refrigerated Roadways

:::case
*RTA v Refrigerated Roadways* [2009] NSWCA 263 — while driving a truck on the F5 freeway near Sydney, Evans, an
employee of Refrigerated Roadways, was struck by a large piece of concrete dropped by third parties from an overhead
bridge; he died as a consequence. The RTA knew of the problem of people dropping objects from overhead bridges. The
question was whether the RTA owed a duty of care in respect of erecting a structure to prevent objects from being
thrown off the bridge. The RTA argued its duty did not extend that far, relying on *Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre v
Anzil*.
:::

The NSW Court of Appeal held that the RTA owed such a duty. It distinguished *Modbury Triangle*: in that case, the
type of harm suffered could only arise through criminal conduct. The RTA has "a unique role to play concerning any
dangers of objects falling from an overpass onto cars on a freeway below"; people driving on the freeway are
vulnerable, and many serious road accidents involve criminal conduct on the part of one party.

The Court of Appeal also ruled that s 43A would not have helped the RTA, because the erection of protective screens
would not be an exercise of a special statutory power — anybody can erect a screen. However, the Court held that the
RTA did not breach its duty: it had not been shown that the RTA failed to take reasonable steps to prevent the risk of
harm. (The lecturer added that the RTA had already begun putting screens up across overpasses.)

### Police

The starting point: no duty of care is owed to individual members of the public in the investigation and suppression
of crime — *Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* [1989] AC 53. Why: conflicting responsibilities, resource
allocation, and the risk of defensive policing. But it is not an immunity: negligent operational conduct can attract a
duty — *Zalewski v Turcarolo* [1994] Aust Torts Reports 81-280 (a police shooting during an operational response;
duty owed). Recall *Stuart v Kirkland-Veenstra* (Topic 6): a statutory power to intervene is not of itself a duty to
exercise it.

Complaints about police entering or searching a house usually raise intentional torts — did they have lawful
authority to be there? — rather than a duty of care.

## 4. Landlords

In the past, landlords were held not to have a duty of care — to the tenant or other users of the premises — in
respect of defective premises: *Cavalier v Pope* (1906). The reasoning was that the tenant, who is there and controls
the property, is the one who can see the defects. This rule was rejected by the High Court of Australia in *Northern
Sandblasting Pty Ltd v Harris* (1997) 188 CLR 313.

:::case
*Jones v Bartlett* (2000) 205 CLR 166 — the plaintiff, son of the tenant, was injured when he walked through a glass
door. The glass door complied with the Australian standards at the time the house was built but not when it was
leased; the landlord did not know that the glass door did not comply with the regulations. The High Court held that
the landlord owed a duty of care, but such duty was not breached in the circumstances of the case.
:::

Gummow and Hayne JJ noted that the key issue is not whether a duty existed but what was the nature and extent of such
duty: "the content of the landlord's duty to the tenant will be conterminous with a requirement that the premises be
reasonably fit for … habitation as a domestic residence". The "duty requires a landlord not to let premises that
suffer defects which the landlord knows or ought to know make the premises unsafe for the use".

The duty will be discharged "if the landlord takes reasonable steps to ascertain the existence of any such defects
and … takes reasonable steps to remove them or make the premises safe". What constitutes the taking of reasonable
steps will depend on all the circumstances of the case; in *Jones v Bartlett*, the glass door was not held to be a
dangerous defect. The lecturer linked the condition report a tenant fills in at the start of a lease to this duty to
ascertain defects.

The two judges also clarified that the landlord owes a duty of care to other entrants — though the duty to them will
be narrower than that owed by an occupier such as a tenant.

## 5. Occupiers

An occupier is a person or entity who controls the relevant land. Occupiers of land owe a duty of care to people who
enter lawfully onto their land. The extent and nature of the duty is subject to different factors, such as the
relationship between the occupier and entrant, the purpose of entry, and the type of premises. Examples: slipping in
supermarkets, injury caused by the poor condition of land, guests injured while staying in hotels, a commuter injured
while using a bus exchange — and *Romeo*, where the scope of the duty and the obviousness of the risk were in issue.

:::case
*Commissioner for Railways v Cardy* (1960) 104 CLR 274, 286: "A person trespasses at their own risk and the occupier
is under no duty to him except to refrain from intentional or wanton harm to him…."
:::

But see the discussion in the text (pp 328–329) of cases since then. The nature of the common law duty of care owed by
an occupier to a trespasser remains unresolved, but the current trends seem to indicate the High Court is increasingly
placing a higher level of responsibility on the plaintiff (that is, the trespasser).

## 6. Legal advocates (immune defendants)

As a matter of public policy, barristers are immune from liability for negligence in relation to the conduct of
cases in court, or in relation to such pre-trial matters as are intimately connected with the case in court. The
policy reasons for the immunity were outlined in *Giannarelli v Wraith*, including:

- the advocate's duty to the court may conflict with the duty to the client;
- the possible waste of court time if advocates present numerous arguments to protect against the risk of
  negligence allegations;
- considerations related to the importance of the finality of judgment — perhaps the most important, and raised in
  later cases as a key reason: a negligence case about how the first case was run disrupts the finality of the first
  judgment and undermines the court process.

The immunity is about the courtroom, not legal advice. Professionals who provide advice — lawyers included — fall
under pure economic loss caused by negligent misstatement (Topic 7). Solicitors can definitely be sued for negligence
in the way they act for a client: the lecturer's example is a solicitor who failed to diarise a limitation period, so
a $10 million claim became statute barred and the client could sue the solicitor for the lost claim.

## 7. Children

Parents may have a duty of care to others to control their child.

:::case
*Smith v Leurs* (1945) 70 CLR 256 — a 13-year-old boy fired a stone from a sling shot, injuring the plaintiff's eye.
The High Court held that a duty was owed but it was not breached, because the parents had taken reasonable steps by
telling their son not to use the sling shot outside their home.
:::

Parents generally owe no duty to a child in respect of feeding, clothing, raising and so on — parents can choose to
raise their child as they see fit. However, they have a duty to drive carefully so as not to injure a child (or even a
foetus), and they should not create any danger or risk for the child.

:::case
*Hahn v Conley* (1971) 126 CLR 276 — a grandfather was looking after a child, a 3-year-old girl; she called out from
across the road; the grandfather answered the call; the girl crossed the road and was struck by a car. The defendant
claimed an indemnity from the grandfather, assessed at 25% of the total damages, so the High Court had to consider
whether the girl had a cause of action against her grandfather. The Court noted that parents may become liable to the
child if the child is led into danger by their actions; but in the circumstances the grandfather, in loco parentis,
owed the girl no duty of care to protect her against the danger — e.g., he did not call the girl to come to him.
:::

### Complex situations: unborn children

Unborn children raise a particular difficulty, because until a child is born, she cannot bring an action. However,
where a child is injured while in the womb, an action for pre-natal injuries may be brought by the child provided the
child is later born alive.

Actions by parents have also been brought for wrongful conception (the mother would not have conceived if not for the
negligence — for example, a negligent sterilisation procedure) and wrongful birth (the pregnancy would have been
terminated but for negligence — for example, a test that negligently failed to report a genetic abnormality).
Actions have also been brought by the child for wrongful life (the pregnancy should have been terminated because of
handicap). These actions tend to be controversial for various reasons — how do you put a value on life? — yet
compensation can fund the care a severely disabled child needs. The detail belongs to health law and is not needed in
this unit.

## 8. Third parties

A duty of care may exist in relation to conduct by third parties causing injury or damage to the plaintiff. Picture a
triangle: the defendant owes a duty to the plaintiff, the plaintiff suffers loss or damage, but it is the third
party's conduct that causes it — for example, youth detainees (third party) under the control of a prison authority
(defendant) cause damage to the plaintiff. The plaintiff sues the defendant, usually because the third party has no
money to pay compensation.

:::rule
A duty of care owed by the defendant in relation to the conduct of a third party is only found in certain
circumstances, usually when there is a pre-existing relationship (between the defendant and the plaintiff, or between
the defendant and the third party), or a situation of control.
:::

:::case
*Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co Ltd* [1970] AC 1004 — boys escaped from detention operated by the Home Office and caused
significant damage to several yachts in the nearby harbour; the yacht owners sued the Home Office for negligence. The
House of Lords held that the Home Office owed them a duty of care: it was reasonably foreseeable that the boys might
cause damage if they escaped, and the detention officers had control over the boys. The court limited the scope of the
duty to damage caused in the vicinity immediately after escape; for reasons of policy (indeterminate liability), no
duty existed as regards further damage inflicted by the escapees while they remained at large.
:::

:::case
*Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre v Anzil* (2000) 205 CLR 254 — an employee of a video store was attacked by an unknown
assailant in the shopping centre car park after the centre's lights had been switched off; he argued that the centre
owner should be liable for failing to provide adequate lighting. The High Court held that the owner (occupier) did not
owe him a duty to protect him from the criminal conduct of a third party. The court distinguished between creating a
risk of harm and merely failing to prevent harm caused by someone else: the defendant had not created the risk of
criminal attack, and there was no special relationship giving rise to a duty to protect from that risk.
:::

There is a general reluctance of the common law to impose a duty requiring defendants to protect others from the
criminal or intentional acts of third parties, particularly where the defendant did not create or control that risk,
and where there is no protective relationship between defendant and plaintiff. In *Dorset Yacht* there was control of
the third party; in *Modbury Triangle* there was neither control of the attackers nor a protective relationship.
Compare *RTA v Refrigerated Roadways*, where *Modbury Triangle* was distinguished because the risk was not only
criminal.
