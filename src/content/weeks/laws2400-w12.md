## 1. Two defences built on the plaintiff's own conduct

This week's only source is the textbook's pair of flowcharts on defences to negligence (Tort Law Principles 3e,
pp 478–479): "Defences to Negligence 2 – Contributory Negligence" and "Defences to Negligence 3 – Voluntary
Assumption of Risk". The first flowchart in that series is not among this week's materials. Both defences look at
what the plaintiff did, but they work very differently.

:::examtip
Contributory negligence results in a *partial reduction* in damages. Voluntary assumption of risk operates as a
*complete defence*. When the plaintiff's own conduct is in issue, consider both branches.
:::

## 2. Contributory negligence

:::rule
A finding of contributory negligence on behalf of the plaintiff results in a *partial reduction* in the damages
otherwise recoverable by a plaintiff based on what is "just and equitable having regard to [their] share in the
responsibility for the damage" — see Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1965 (NSW) s 9(1) and the
equivalents in other Australian states and territories.
:::

As to the meaning of contributory negligence, the flowchart refers to *Davies v Swan Motor Co (Swansea) Ltd* and
*Jones v Livox Quarries Ltd*. The materials do not give the facts or holdings of these or any other case named this
week; each case card records only the proposition the flowchart cites it for.

### General principles: the core inquiry

The flowchart lists seven boxes under "General principles". The first three frame the core inquiry:

- **What is "just and equitable"?** Need to examine the whole conduct of each of the negligent parties and consider
  the relative blameworthiness of each — see *Pennington v Norris*.
- **Relevant standard of care.** The same principles that apply when determining whether a person has been negligent
  apply to considering whether a person has been contributorily negligent — see CLA s 5R and equivalents.
- **Causal requirement.** A causal link between the plaintiff's lack of care for their own safety and their injury
  must be established — see *Caswell v Powell Duffryn Associated Collieries*. Ordinary principles of causation apply
  in this context.

:::statute
Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) s 5R: "(1) The principles that are applicable in determining whether a person has
been negligent also apply in determining whether the person who suffered harm has been contributorily negligent in
failing to take precautions against the risk of that harm. (2) For that purpose— (a) the standard of care required
of the person who suffered harm is that of a reasonable person in the position of that person, and (b) the matter
is to be determined on the basis of what that person knew or ought to have known at the time."
:::

### General principles: particular situations

The other four boxes deal with particular situations:

- **Momentary thoughtlessness by employees.** In the employment context, inattention bred of familiarity and
  repetition, the urgency of the task, the plaintiff's preoccupation with the matter in hand, and other prevailing
  conditions may be taken into account when determining whether the plaintiff was contributorily negligent — see
  *McLean v Tedman*; *Staveley Iron and Chemical Co v Jones*.
- **Agony of the moment.** Action on the part of the plaintiff that may be considered to demonstrate a lack of
  reasonable care for the plaintiff's own safety may not amount to contributory negligence where the plaintiff's
  actions can be justified in a situation of perceived emergency — see *Avram v Gusakoski*; *Caterson v Commissioner
  for Railways*.
- **Rescuers.** Law in most jurisdictions now offers protection to those who act in good faith and without
  recklessness — see, eg, Civil Law (Wrongs) Act 2002 (ACT) s 5; Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) s 56.
- **Intoxication.** There is now a legislative presumption of contributory negligence where the plaintiff is
  intoxicated — see, eg, CLA s 50; Civil Law (Wrongs) Act 2002 (ACT) ss 95, 96.

:::statute
CLA s 56: "a good samaritan is a person who, in good faith and without expectation of payment or other reward, comes
to the assistance of a person who is apparently injured or at risk of being injured." Section 57(1): a good
samaritan "does not incur any personal civil liability in respect of any act or omission done or made by the good
samaritan in an emergency when assisting a person who is apparently injured or at risk of being injured."
:::

The flowchart files rescuers under contributory negligence, but the NSW sections it cites protect the good
samaritan from personal civil liability; the materials say nothing more about how a rescuer's own carelessness is
weighed. Note too that the flowchart's phrase "without recklessness" is not the wording of NSW s 56, which speaks of
acting in good faith and "without expectation of payment or other reward".

### Intoxication: CLA s 50 step by step

:::statute
CLA s 50 applies when the person whose death, injury or damage is the subject of the proceedings was, at the time of
the act or omission that caused it, "intoxicated to the extent that the person's capacity to exercise reasonable care
and skill was impaired" (s 50(1)). A court is not to award damages unless satisfied that the death, injury or damage
(or some other injury or damage) "is likely to have occurred even if the person had not been intoxicated" (s 50(2)).
If so satisfied, "it is to be presumed that the person was contributorily negligent unless the court is satisfied
that the person's intoxication did not contribute in any way to the cause" (s 50(3)). Where the presumption applies,
damages are reduced "by 25% or a greater percentage determined by the court to be appropriate" (s 50(4)). The section
does not apply where the court is satisfied that the intoxication was not self-induced (s 50(5)).
:::

## 3. Voluntary assumption of risk

:::rule
*Volenti non fit injuria* (voluntary assumption of risk) operates as a *complete defence* to negligence. The essence
of the defence lies in a plaintiff's willingness to assume a risk which the plaintiff knew existed in a particular
activity.
:::

The defendant must show that the plaintiff not only perceived the existence of the risk but that he or she fully
appreciated the nature and extent of that risk and voluntarily accepted that specific risk. The defence is narrowly
confined to the specific risk and does not extend to an awareness of the general nature of the risk.

### Knowledge of risk

The plaintiff must fully appreciate the nature and likely manifestation of the risk — *Banovic v Perkovic*; *Carey v
Lake Macquarie City Council*. Effective knowledge extends beyond the mere existence of a risk to an acceptance of
materialisation of that risk — see *Carey v Lake Macquarie City Council*; *ICI v Shatwell*.

### Intoxicated drivers and passengers

The courts are generally reluctant to allow the *volenti* defence against passengers who rely on an intoxicated
driver. Legislative intervention in some Australian jurisdictions specifically excludes the *volenti* defence in
these circumstances — see, eg, Motor Accidents Act 1988 (NSW) s 76 (cited in the flowchart; its text is not in this
week's materials).

### Legislative reform: CLA s 5L

Some Australian jurisdictions have enacted legislation which expands the voluntary assumption of risk defence
available to defendants — see, eg, CLA s 5L. For guidance as to the application of these provisions, see *Tapp v
Australian Bushmen's Campdraft & Rodeo Association Ltd* (2022) 273 CLR 454.

:::statute
CLA s 5L: "(1) A person (the defendant) is not liable in negligence for harm suffered by another person (the
plaintiff) as a result of the materialisation of an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity engaged in by
the plaintiff. (2) This section applies whether or not the plaintiff was aware of the risk."
:::

Set s 5L(2) beside the common law: *volenti* needs the plaintiff to have perceived, fully appreciated and voluntarily
accepted the specific risk, while s 5L applies whether or not the plaintiff was aware of the risk at all. (Section
5L was met in Topic 6, sport and recreational injury.)

### Illegality

Where the plaintiff has been injured and the plaintiff and defendant were involved in the criminal enterprise, the
courts have often found that it would not be possible or feasible to determine an appropriate standard of care. In
such cases, the courts have found that a defence of illegality is available to the defendant — see, eg, *Gala v
Preston*. However, the defence of illegality will not be available to a defendant where, on the facts, the court is
able to determine the relevant standard of care — see, eg, *Progress & Properties Ltd v Craft*.

:::
