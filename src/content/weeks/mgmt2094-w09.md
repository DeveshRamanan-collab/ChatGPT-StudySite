## About Dow Corning

The guest lecturer, Dr Vik Kortian, is a chemical engineer who spent most of his career at Dow Corning (much of it on
personal-care products such as silicone conditioners and two-in-one shampoos) before management consulting and academia.
His agenda: about Dow Corning; the history of product development; the case study (the Deck Protector); lessons learned.

- Dow Corning was formally established in **1943** as a joint venture between the American conglomerates **Dow
  Chemical** and **Corning Glass** to explore the potential of silicone, and made products for use by the U.S. military
  in World War II. Its first plant, in Midland, Michigan, began operating in 1945.
- On 11 December 2015 Dow announced it would merge with DuPont, and that it would acquire Corning's stake in Dow
  Corning for $4.8 billion in cash, with Corning gaining a roughly 40% stake in Hemlock Semiconductor Corporation. Dow
  Chemical assumed full ownership on 1 June 2016; the company became **Dow Silicones Corporation** in 2018.
- It markets over 7000 products (sealants, adhesives, rubbers, lubricants, silicone oils and solvents) across
  electronics, automotive, construction, healthcare and other industries. Around 2,100 are sold through
  **Xiameter**, its online-only distributor. The guest lecturer explained the logic: many products were really
  commodities, not specialties, so the company stripped out the services customers were not prepared to pay for and
  sold them online — an early digital transformation.

## Dow Corning's transformation program

In the early 1990s Dow Corning became embroiled in the silicone breast implant saga. Claims first centred on breast
cancer, then migrated to autoimmune diseases (lupus, rheumatoid arthritis) and neurological problems. Lawsuits began in
1984 and culminated in a 1998 multibillion-dollar class action settlement. The company spent **nine years in
bankruptcy protection**, ending in June 2004, during which it largely withdrew from clinical markets. The guest
lecturer noted that it came out stronger and bigger than it went in.

- Before the saga, Dow Corning had worked with IBM to develop its own software. In 1994 it started on a new Legal
  Entity Ordering Payments system (LEOP). Under the pressure of the litigation, and after 12 months and a significant
  amount of money spent unsuccessfully, it decided to **stop developing software and focus on its strengths:
  silicone products**.
- In 1995 it announced it would implement **SAP**, and announced its **program of transformation**.

The transformation aimed at a **'world class', customer-focused, process-oriented organisation** — "a four-year
journey, 1997–2000" pictured as a pyramid: communications hardware, then enabling software (SAP, Siebel, Outlook,
internet/intranet), then workflow re-engineering and the learning organisation, then customer distinction, supply
chains and commercialisation excellence, then culture, code of conduct and organisation structure, topped by a
customer-driven company, with Six Sigma applied throughout. There was an acknowledgment that **three areas of the
organisation needed 're-engineering'; commercialisation of new products was one of them.**

## Commercialisation issues

- Dow Corning was a heavy investor in R&D: it spent **10% of revenue** on R&D. Its analytical capabilities were among
  the best in the world: the University of Michigan and Michigan State University used its scanning electron
  microscope and other instruments.
- Before 1992 R&D was decentralised, with facilities at a number of locations around the world (including a small
  unit at the Australian plant). After 1992 management created **one central R&D location in Midland, Michigan**.
- Design and commercialisation of new products took on average **4 years** — from the idea, through laboratory
  testing, the pilot plant and market testing, to fine-tuning and selling.

:::definition
**New Product Market Share (NPS)**, one of Dow Corning's Key Performance Indicators: the percentage of revenue that
comes from newly commercialised products. It was around **25%** in the 1970s and 80s; by **1995** it had declined to
**13%**.
:::

The causes were argued over. Some blamed the centralisation of R&D; others pointed to the limits of the permutations
available on the siloxane (silicon–oxygen) backbone. Either way the number had gone down, and Dow Corning's leadership
determined that the way it commercialised products needed to improve.

- In 1997 it hired a consulting company to redesign its commercialisation process.
- Implementation was not successful: **poor change management practices led to poor take-up** of the new processes.
  The guest lecturer: every new idea was a change, and leadership was not skilled in making change stick; many people
  kept saying the new process would not work.
- In 1999 leadership abandoned the new commercialisation process to focus on the **Lean Six Sigma** implementation
  (its biggest competitor was a leading proponent of Six Sigma). In **2001**, as part of that program, it decided to
  implement **Design for Six Sigma (DFSS)**.

:::examtip
R&D spending is not innovation. Dow Corning spent 10% of revenue on R&D with world-class science, yet its share of
revenue from new products halved. The bottleneck was commercialisation, and a new process failed until people took
it up.
:::

## Six Sigma: three principal methods

The guest lecturer described Six Sigma as a quality-improvement methodology: it is about building and improving
processes, the efficiency and effectiveness of organisations, and discipline. Business leaders drive the **three
principal methods** used in implementing Six Sigma, pictured as a triangle with **leadership** at the centre:

- **DFSS** (Design for Six Sigma) generates **new** processes, products, services and/or plants.
- **DMAIC** (the Improvement Process) improves **existing** process performance.
- **Process Management** is the system that enables leverage and **sustains the gains** achieved by DFSS and the Sigma
  Improvement Process.
- **Leaders** drive and align the efforts strategically.

At Dow Corning DMAIC was the improvement process and DFSS the new-product development process; with process
management and committed leadership these were the three key elements of the transformation.

## The Six Sigma design process: DMEDI

| Step | Objective and main activities | Typical tools |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Define | Develop a charter, a multi-generational product/service plan and a project plan; business case, opportunity and goal statements, project scope, communication and change management plan | Charter, Gantt chart, in-and-out-of-scope tool, communications plan |
| 2 Measure | Research customers; identify customer, environmental/health/safety and business needs; translate them into critical-to-customer requirements (CCRs); benchmark competitor performance; set target sigma performance | Voice of the customer, affinity diagram, benchmarking, Quality Functional Deployment (QFD), CCR matrix |
| 3 Explore | Translate CCRs into functions; use creative techniques to develop alternative concepts; select the best-fit concept; develop high-level designs and assess their risk | Creativity tools, Pugh matrix, function/design QFDs, simulation, capability assessment |
| 4 Develop | Detailed designs; capability assessments; FMEA; process control plans; pilot plans | DOE, FMEA, Weibull, fault tree, simulation, process control plans |
| 5 Implement | Execute the pilot and validation plans; analyse results; scale-up decision; full-scale implementation plans; transition to process owners; lessons learned; rewards and recognition | Gantt chart, pilot validation assessment, process control plans, score card |

Each step ends in 'execute'. Running alongside every step are on-going activities: change management, customer
alignment, strategy alignment, project management, project case development and process management.

The guest lecturer stressed the focus on market research. The statistics are the same as in today's big-data
analytics; the difference is that they worked with samples, where big data works with whole populations. The aim was
not only a product the market wanted, but one made in the most efficient way possible.

## Case study: the Deck Protector

One of Dow Corning's major customers, **ArmorAll**, approached it with the idea of taking on **Thompson's WaterSeal**
(the market leader in deck protection) with a **silicone modified organic (SMO)** product. In the US many houses have
wooden decks that must be waterproofed: water absorbed by the wood expands when it freezes and breaks the wood up.

- As the number of permutations with the siloxane backbone was limited, **SMO was Dow Corning's strategy for growth**;
  this would have been the first SMO product commercialised.
- ArmorAll wanted the product developed and commercialised **within 18 months**. The project became **the pilot for
  the DfSS methodology**.

Product development. The chemist worked on a product that would meet or exceed the competitor's performance criteria,
namely its waterproofing, and came up with an SMO emulsion. The next challenge was the combination of **silane, wax and
surfactant** that would meet the price *and* performance requirements. A **six-factor, two-level Design of
Experiment** was run; samples were tested for waterproofing durability and the results compiled with the relevant
economic and profitability data.

**Process engineering commenced concurrently**: a new process was designed on the volumes ArmorAll provided. (The
guest lecturer: the chemist creates the recipe in the laboratory; the chemical engineer turns it into a large
production process. He was the process engineer and the economic evaluator.) Once all the information was complete, a
key meeting was held with ArmorAll to decide which formulation and price it wanted. ArmorAll was extremely impressed
with the detail; agreement was reached and production began. The guest lecturer's explanation: the team spent far
more time up front on experiments, financial analysis and process engineering, so the customer only had to choose.

:::definition
**Results of the ArmorAll Deck Protector.** In 12 months over 4,500 tonnes of product were produced, generating
US$40 million of revenue. The project won a Technical Achievement Award and a Manufacturing Achievement Award, and
became the basis for all new commercialisation projects at Dow Corning.
:::

## Post commercialisation

After 12 months of a very successful campaign **the project stopped**. ArmorAll had run TV commercials that Thompson's
challenged; a legal battle ensued and ArmorAll lost. The financial impact of the court case forced ArmorAll to be
taken over by **Clorox**. The guest lecturer: the marketing people ran the ads without checking with the legal
people; Dow Corning, as a B2B supplier, did everything its customer wanted, but the customer could still undo it.

**Lessons learned**

1. The new Lean (Six Sigma) approach to commercialisation was a complete success.
2. It opened up the use of complex statistical and mathematical modelling in the product development process.
3. Existing processes were challenged and, when identified as non-value-added, were removed.
4. No matter how good your commercialisation process, poor risk management by your customers can lead to project
   failure.

The host lecturer tied this to the unit's distinction: coming up with the idea or product is the **creativity**
part; bringing it to market — commercialising it — is the **innovation** part. Dow Corning's product succeeded; the
way it was brought to market failed. Every component of the organisation — marketing, legal, HR, culture and above
all strategy — has to come together.

## Creativity and discipline

Six Sigma is about discipline: disciplined people, processes and actions. The guest lecturer's point was that you can
still be disciplined and still be creative.

- **Space to play.** At the small Australian R&D unit, Friday afternoons were free: people could try whatever they
  wanted in the laboratory, amid laughter and noise. The new central R&D building in Midland was sterile — lab coats,
  no noise — and the creativity was missing. The host lecturer added that most such ideas go to waste, but the few that
  work may be a goldmine, and the time builds morale and the sharing of ideas.
- **Diversity.** The company brought people who think differently into its headquarters: with creativity you really
  need diversity.
- **Reuse.** Dr Kortian's own patented process for making silicone emulsions came from applying a technique he had
  already seen (emulsion polymerisation) in a new way. Many problems have been solved before.
- **Balance.** The host lecturer: creativity alone gives chaos and nothing useful; structure alone gives nothing new.
  Find the balance — and be well prepared, a real specialist in your area, so that ideas can come.
