## 1. Scope of liability in the causation inquiry

Causation under the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) has two elements. Section 5D(1) says that a determination that
negligence caused particular harm comprises (a) that the negligence was a necessary condition of the occurrence of the
harm (factual causation), and (b) that it is appropriate for the scope of the negligent person's liability to extend
to the harm so caused (scope of liability). This week's flowcharts — "Causation 4–6" in Tort Law Principles — deal with
element (b).

:::statute
CLA s 5D(1)(b): "that it is appropriate for the scope of the negligent person's liability to extend to the harm so
caused (scope of liability)". CLA s 5D(4): "For the purpose of determining the scope of liability, the court is to
consider (amongst other relevant things) whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed on the
negligent party."
:::

The flowchart reads s 5D(4) as asking whether and why responsibility for the harm should, or should not, be imposed on
the tortfeasor. It embraces policy-based considerations which the courts are required to articulate — see *Wallace v
Kam*. This raises *normative* questions, ie, whether the defendant *ought* to be held liable — see *Ruddock v Taylor*.

:::rule
Scope of liability raises two distinct common law concepts: new intervening acts (*novus actus interveniens*) and
remoteness of harm.
:::

## 2. New intervening acts (novus actus interveniens)

These are cases where 'but for' causation is established, but later events have some effect on the situation created
by the defendant's negligence. The court must determine whether subsequent events relieve the defendant of
responsibility for the injury caused, ie, whether the subsequent event takes the injury outside the scope of the
defendant's liability. So the court must ask whether the subsequent event breaks the chain of causation.

:::rule
Is the subsequent event ultroneous, something unwarrantable, a new cause which disturbs the sequence of events,
something which can be described as either unreasonable or extraneous or extrinsic? — *The Oropesa*.
:::

To sever the connection, the intervening act must ordinarily be:

- **voluntary human action** — voluntary action is conduct which is 'volitional'; or
- **a causally independent act which is a coincidence which is extremely unlikely** — see *Haber v Walker*.

Examples of 'volitional' conduct which severed the chain of causation: *Rickards v Lothian*; *Yates v Jones*; *CES v
Superclinics (Aust) Pty Ltd*. Examples of 'non-volitional' conduct which did not sever the chain of causation:
*Urbanski v Patel*; *Haynes v G Harwood & Son*; *Medlin v State Government Insurance Commission*; *Hirst v Nominal
Defendant*.

## 3. Negligent acts followed by negligent acts

The question of whether a subsequent negligent act severs the causal connection is a matter of 'fact and degree' — see
*Mahony v J Kruschich (Demolitions) Pty Ltd*. Negligence of a third party or even the plaintiff themselves does not
necessarily sever the chain of causation — see *Mahony v J Kruschich (Demolitions) Pty Ltd*; *Burns v Pearce*.

:::rule
This is especially so where the defendant's wrongful conduct has generated the very risk of injury resulting from the
negligence of the plaintiff or a third party and that injury occurs in the ordinary course of things — see *March v
E & MH Stramare Pty Ltd*. See, eg, *Club Italia (Geelong) Inc v Ritchie*; *Jausnik v Nominal Defendant (No 5)*;
*Gittani Stone Pty Ltd v Pavkovic*.
:::

## 4. Remoteness of harm

Remoteness of harm is concerned with the extent of the defendant's liability for losses. The leading case is
*Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v Morts Dock and Engineering Co Ltd (The Wagon Mound (No 1))*. The extent of the
defendant's liability for losses is determined by a reasonable foreseeability test.

It is a different test of reasonable foreseeability when compared to duty and breach — the court is concerned with what
damage is reasonably foreseeable as a consequence of the defendant's negligence. But 'reasonable foreseeability' means
the same thing as it does at the duty and breach stages.

:::definition
A risk is reasonably foreseeable when it is a 'real risk'. A real risk is one which would occur to the mind of a
reasonable man in the position of the defendant's servant and which he would not brush aside as far-fetched —
*Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v The Miller Steamship Co Pty Ltd (The Wagon Mound (No 2))*.
:::

- The extent of the harm and the precise manner of the harm's occurrence need not be reasonably foreseeable. See, eg,
  *Hughes v Lord Advocate*; *Jolley v Sutton London Borough Council*; *Mount Isa Mines Ltd v Pusey*; *Gittani Stone
  Pty Ltd v Pavkovic*; *Waller v James*.
- As to the 'kind' or 'type' of harm, see *Tremain v Pike*; *Nader v Urban Transit Authority of New South Wales*;
  *Commonwealth v McLean*; *Metrolink Victoria Pty Ltd v Inglis*.

This is the last of the three foreseeability questions from week 5 (Glass JA): at the remoteness stage, whether the
"kind of damage" suffered by the plaintiff was reasonably foreseeable as a possible outcome. The week 6 casebook extract
of *Tame v New South Wales* (Gummow and Kirby JJ) makes the same point for psychiatric harm: it is not necessary that
the particular type of disorder that eventuated be reasonably foreseeable; it is sufficient that the class of injury,
psychiatric illness, was foreseeable.

## 5. The egg-shell skull rule

:::rule
The defendant must take the plaintiff with all his weaknesses, beliefs and reactions as well as his capacities and
attributes, physical, social and economic — see *Nader v Urban Transit Authority of New South Wales*; see also
*Kavanagh v Akhtar*.
:::

The rule sits on the remoteness branch of the flowchart, beside the rule that the extent of the harm need not be
reasonably foreseeable. In *Tame* (casebook, week 6), Gummow and Kirby JJ noted Windeyer J's point in *Pusey* that a
"normal fortitude" condition of liability did not stand well with the egg-shell skull rule, and said that, if liability
is established, the egg-shell skull rule applies to the assessment of damages. For a NSW mental-harm claim, CLA s 32
still sets the normal-fortitude duty test (week 6).

## 6. Tutorial preparation: communication and trauma-informed practice

The Topic 11 tutorial preparation slides (a LAWS1100 skills deck) are not tort doctrine: they prepare for interviewing
a client.

:::case
*Roberts v Cashman* [2000] NSWSC 770 — the client's banana chair broke and she was injured; Depo Medrol injections made
things worse. She joined a class action against Depo Medrol by filling out the firm's questionnaire, but never had a
conference and was never asked about the original injury; when she found out she should have sued the chair's
manufacturer, her claim was time-barred. The duty of care was breached: the firm had expertise in personal injury and
product liability; assessing the original injury was inherent in the Depo Medrol claim; the questionnaire should have
put the firm on notice that a conference was called for; there is no different standard of conduct in class actions;
and a conference was not impracticable or uneconomic. A solicitor who does not interview the client in such a
situation "does so at his own peril" [64].
:::

- **Duties.** The duty to the court and the administration of justice is paramount (ASCR r 3.1). Key duties in an
  initial interview: explain confidentiality, establish the limits of the retainer, and disclose costs.
- **Client-focused interviewing.** The client is an actor and agent (ASCR rr 7 and 8): the lawyer controls the process,
  not the content, and clients make their own decisions — but adjust your level of scaffolding to the client in front
  of you.
- **Rapport and listening.** Rapport starts from the moment you see the client and depends on being non-judgmental.
  Active listening is LARS: Listening, Acknowledging (door openers, minimal encouragers), Reflecting (mirroring, for
  confirmation — reframing gives it back in new words, to change perspective) and Summarising (facts and client
  concerns). Hear the client before trying to solve their problems.
- **Questioning.** The T-funnel runs from broad open, to general open, to detailed, to nitty-gritty questions, with
  clarification throughout. Don't start questioning too soon and don't do too much of it; ask one plain, short question
  at a time; avoid leading, compound and double-negative questions.
- **Trauma-informed practice.** It is about avoiding re-traumatisation, not helping people solve their trauma. Ask
  "What happened to you?" instead of "What is wrong with you?". Trauma responses may look like fight (the 'rude'
  client), flight (the 'unresponsive' client), freeze (the 'undecisive' client) or fawn (the 'adoring' client), and
  shame leads people to hide things. Principles: safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration and empowerment —
  doing with, rather than for or to.
