## Defining tort law

"Tort" comes from the Latin *tortum*, meaning "something twisted, wrong or crooked". Tort law deals with civil
wrongs: it lays down rules and principles about how one should behave towards others — avoid causing harm to
others, and otherwise remedy the harm (e.g. by providing compensation). There are several prominent, separately
named torts, which raises a long-debated question: is it the *law of tort* or the *law of torts*?

:::definition
John Salmond held the view that the law of torts is merely a collection of miscellaneous causes of action. Pollock
and Winfield argued, on the other hand, that there is a set of fundamental principles underpinning the law of
tort — for example, duties imposed by law and owed to all.
:::

In common law countries, tort law is mostly judge-made law, so cases are critical. However, statutes have
increasingly become important, for example in responding to a societal challenge about the price and
availability of insurance — the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) is a case in point. Statutes have not completely
replaced or codified the common law: instead they modify or abolish some of the common law, and create new
causes of action. The co-existence of cases and statutes is the current life of tort law.

:::examtip
Before applying a common law rule, always check whether a statute such as the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) has
modified or abolished it for the jurisdiction in the problem.
:::

## Tort law and other laws

Tort law is part of a governance ecosystem that regulates human behaviour and protects rights and interests; it
should not be seen as a watertight compartment.

**Tort law and criminal law.** Civil wrongs are the focus of tort law, while criminal law deals with public
wrongs (offences). The distinction is not always sharp: a certain wrong may amount to both tort and crime (e.g.
assault), and compensation may be obtained for injuries from crimes. Broad distinctions: nature of wrong
(private/public), main purpose (compensation/punishment), primary principle of justice
(redress-correction/retribution-deterrence), action initiated by (individual/State), standard of proof (balance
of probabilities/beyond reasonable doubt).

**Tort law and contract law.** Both deal with civil wrongs, and certain wrongs may create a cause of action
under both (e.g. product liability, contracts for services). Key differences: source of duties (law/parties and
law), scope of duties (general "neighbour principle"/only on specific parties), relevance of motive
(yes in some cases/no), nature of damages (unliquidated/liquidated), privity (not relevant/generally required).

**Tort law and human rights law.** Tort law aims to protect the individual's bodily integrity, reputation and
property — similar to the purpose of human rights. In most liberal democracies a Bill of Rights may influence
interpretation of existing tort law or contribute to new tortious actions. Australia has no comprehensive human
rights law at the federal level (a human rights law exists in the ACT, Victoria and Queensland), so there has
been limited use of tort law to protect human rights.

## Theories about the role of tort law in society

Scholars and judges have invoked various theoretical justifications — descriptive or normative — for the role
of tort law in society; these theories also provide a framework to critically analyse judicial decisions.

:::rule
Compensatory function: tort law provides compensation to people injured by a wrong, aiming to put the victim
back, so far as money can achieve it, into the position she would have been in had the wrong not happened.
:::

The compensatory function faces challenges in practice — e.g. miscalculating loss of future earnings, high
legal fees, problems with lump-sum payment. The deterrence function postulates that if defendants (e.g.
employers made to pay for a workplace accident) bear the cost, they will try to avoid it in future; the same
logic applies to negligent manufacturing. The deterrence logic is also applied systemically: if X is held
liable, not only X but others will try to avoid committing that tort. Law-and-economics scholars such as
Calabresi and Posner argue an economic structure (cost and benefit) underpins tort law, aiming for the most
efficient allocation of the costs of accidents. Insurance relates to both deterrence and economics, but it may
reduce the deterrent value of tort law.

Corrective justice (Weinrib) sees tort law as concerned with allocating responsibility for harm caused:
individuals who breach the moral rights of others should correct the wrong by paying compensation, resting on
the idea that free human agency underlies responsibility. This emphasis on individuals can ignore other factors
(e.g. in a medical negligence case, the many systems involved) and the relevance of collective responsibility
and risk-sharing. Distributive justice sees tort law as a system to distribute risks and losses in society — a
no-fault liability mechanism (e.g. for road accidents), requiring proof of causation rather than fault, allows
distribution of risks and losses through insurance; losses may also be allocated to a public authority and, in
turn, to taxpayers. Feminist theory asks whether tort law takes into account the experiences of women, and has
focused on the concept of the reasonable person, the valuation of women's work for calculating damages, and the
recognised types of harm.

:::case
*Pabai v Commonwealth of Australia (No 2)* [2025] FCA 796 — Torres Strait Islanders sued the Commonwealth,
claiming its inadequate climate policies caused rising sea levels that damaged their islands and destroyed their
traditional way of life; unsuccessful at first instance, with an appeal lodged and heard before the Full Court of
the Federal Court. Used in lectures as an example of tort law's response to contemporary societal challenges.
:::

**Introducing vicarious liability.** Vicarious liability makes one person or entity liable for the conduct of
another — typically an employer's liability for a tort committed by an employee within the course of
employment. The detailed legal tests are covered later (Topic 12), but the concept recurs throughout the
semester and connects to the theories above.

:::rule
Professor Fleming's policy reasons for vicarious liability (quoted from Sappideen, Vines and Eldridge at
[13.45]): the employer advances its own economic interest through its employees and so should fairly bear a
corresponding liability; the employer is a more promising source of recompense than the employee, "a man of
straw without insurance"; the rule promotes wide distribution of tort losses through liability insurance and
higher prices; and it has admonitory value in accident prevention, since deterrent pressure works best on
employers, who can organise and supervise staff, while employees are rarely worth suing.
:::

## Elements of intentional torts

Two sets of causes of action developed historically: **trespass** (a direct interference with the person,
property or goods) and **action on the case** (indirect and consequential damage). Intentional torts are the
trespass family: trespass to the person (assault, battery and false imprisonment), trespass to land, and
trespass to chattels/goods. Related actions on the case include intentional interference with the person
(*Wilkinson v Downton*), malicious prosecution, conversion and detinue; beyond intentional torts, other actions
on the case include negligence, nuisance, misfeasance in public office and deceit.

The exact form of action was historically crucial, because it determined which court to approach and which form
of pleading to follow. The distinction between trespass and case remains critical in Australia today because
different elements must be proved for the two categories. All forms of trespass share four common elements:

:::rule
The four elements common to all trespass torts: (1) a voluntary act; (2) directness/direct interference; (3)
actionable per se, i.e. the plaintiff need not prove harm or damage; (4) fault.
:::

**Voluntary act.** The act must be a "positive act" — a merely passive act will not suffice (see p 45 of the
text). An epileptic fit is not a positive act; an act caused by the force of a third party is not a positive act;
an omission to act is not a positive act.

**Directness.** Trespass requires a direct act or interference, whereas an indirect/consequential act will
suffice for an action on the case.

:::case
*Hutchins v Maughan* [1947] VLR 131 is instructive on directness: the death of dogs caused by baits laid on the
ground a few days before the complainant arrived with them. The court held no trespass was made out, because
the baits were laid before the complainant arrived on the land; no injury would have occurred had he not taken
his dogs there. Directness is often "an issue of time" — had the defendant thrown the baits at the dogs,
trespass would have been made out.
:::

**Damage.** Trespass is actionable per se: no proof of damage is required, protecting bodily integrity, liberty
and property. For an action on the case, the plaintiff must establish that she suffered damage.

**Fault.** The direct interference must be accompanied by intention, recklessness or negligence. Intention and
recklessness are subjective states of mind; intention is required only as to the act itself (e.g. touching
another while mistakenly believing it is one's partner still suffices — the mistake is no defence; see p 46).
Recklessness arises when the consequences are so likely that the defendant is taken to have foreseen them, and
is generally rare. Negligent trespass (failure to exercise reasonable care resulting in interference) is an
objective state of mind, judged by the reasonable person standard, but is rare as such cases are now mostly
considered under negligence.

On the **onus of proof**, the plaintiff need only prove the direct interference occurred; the onus then shifts to
the defendant to disprove fault (e.g. by proving the interference was an "inevitable accident"). An exception
applies to harm arising out of use of the highway: there, the plaintiff must prove the defendant's fault, because
the public's right to use the highway means road users and adjoining property owners must accept some risk and
so bear the onus (see p 43). In an action on the case, the plaintiff must always prove fault.

:::case
*Williams v Milotin* (1957) 97 CLR 465 — a boy hit by a truck sued in negligence rather than trespass to the
person, to gain the benefit of the longer six-year limitation period. The High Court held he was entitled to
frame the claim as negligence: the same facts can found two distinct causes of action, with different elements
and different limitation periods, and the plaintiff may choose which to plead.
:::
