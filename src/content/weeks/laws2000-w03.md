## The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties

The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) (adopted 23 May 1969; entered into force 27 January 1980)
is "the treaty on treaties". It establishes rules, procedures and guidelines for how treaties are created,
drafted, amended, interpreted and operated, and only applies to treaties concluded between states (art 1
VCLT). "The law of treaties" refers to the body of legal rules and principles of international law applicable
to the making, operation and termination of a treaty. Where international organisations are parties, the 1986
Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties between International Organizations or between States and
International Organizations applies instead — a newer, separate convention not covered in this unit.

:::examtip
We have about 116 state parties to the VCLT, but even states that are not party to it will be bound by the
provisions of the Convention that are considered customary international law — so a non-party can still be
bound by, say, the interpretation rules in arts 31–32, if a court or tribunal has found that provision to
reflect customary international law. If there is a dispute as to whether a given provision applies to a
non-party, the best place to look is journal articles and textbooks, which can point towards the case law
that answers the question.
:::

## What is a treaty

Article 2(1)(a) VCLT defines a treaty. The key elements: concluded between states, in written form, and
governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or two or more related instruments,
and whatever its particular designation. It does not matter what the parties call the agreement — treaty,
convention, agreement and protocol are all common labels, and a protocol has the status of a treaty just the
same. A written treaty does not have to be one polished document: a series of resolutions can form a treaty
(this was the case with OPEC), and a treaty can have a protocol attached to it that is itself a treaty in
written form. "Governed by international law" means the law that applies to how the treaty is concluded,
interpreted and disputed is international law, not the domestic law of any state that concluded it — think of
art 38(1) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice, which lists treaties, customary international
law and general principles of law as sources a court applies to such a dispute.

:::rule
"'treaty' means an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by
international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or in two or more related instruments and whatever
its particular designation" (art 2(1)(a) VCLT).
:::

Intention to create rights or duties under international law does not require a polished, formal document.

:::case
*Aegean Sea Continental Shelf (Greece v Turkey)* — a press communiqué was found capable of being a legally
binding "international agreement".
:::

:::case
*Maritime Delimitation and Territorial Questions (Qatar v Bahrain)* — written minutes from a meeting
stipulated the commitments to which the parties had consented, and so created rights and obligations in
international law: the court found that all the elements of a treaty were present.
:::

:::case
*Maritime Delimitation in the Indian Ocean (Somalia v Kenya)* (Preliminary Objections) — a Memorandum of
Understanding (MoU) between Kenya and Somalia, signed, dated and registered with the United Nations, was held
to be a treaty under international law.
:::

A memorandum of understanding is an interesting case: under international law an MoU would usually qualify as
a treaty, but in Australia's own practice a memorandum of understanding is deliberately given a "less than a
treaty" status and is not treated as a treaty by DFAT — even though, as *Somalia v Kenya* shows, another
country's MoU could well be a treaty at the international level.

## Unilateral statements

Unilateral statements are "non-treaty" actions that can nonetheless give rise to legally binding obligations.
"A state may evidence a clear intention to accept obligations vis-à-vis certain other states by a public
declaration which is not an offer or otherwise dependent on reciprocal undertakings from its addressees"
(Crawford, *Brownlie's Principles*). Unlike a treaty, a unilateral statement is one state committing to do
something, with no reciprocity and no counterpart state agreeing to do something in return — you do not have
another party you have agreed to do something with. It also does not need to be in writing.

:::case
Unilateral statements can create internationally binding obligations: see the *Eastern Greenland Case* (1933)
and the *Nuclear Test Cases* (Australia v France, New Zealand v France) (1974).
:::

Exam tip: a genuine agreement between two heads of government, however informal ("over coffee"), that both
sides simply walk away from without more, is generally not a treaty — most students correctly say no here,
because nothing suggests either side intended to create a binding international agreement in written form.

## Authority to conclude a treaty

Which "official or entity" is competent under international law to give consent on behalf of a state and bind
it internationally is not resolved by international law itself — you have to look at the law of the state in
question, and each country approaches it differently.

- **UK**: treaties are concluded by the Crown — the government acting under the royal ("foreign relations")
  prerogative — but s 20 of the *Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010* (UK) requires a treaty to be
  laid before Parliament for ratification.
- **USA**: "treaties" require the advice and consent of at least two-thirds of the Senate, but the President
  may make "executive agreements" without consulting any other body; though not brought before the Senate,
  executive agreements are still binding on the parties under international law. The Paris Agreement shows
  how far this can swing: Obama joined it by executive order in 2016, Trump withdrew by executive order in
  2017, Biden rejoined by executive order in 2021, and Trump withdrew again by executive order in 2025 — all
  without the legislature. That would not happen in Australia.
- **Australia**: art 61 of the Constitution gives the executive power to conclude treaties; in practice this
  means the Minister for Foreign Affairs and the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade (DFAT), together with
  the Office of International Law in the Attorney-General's Department, manage treaties.

:::rule
Article 7 VCLT is divided into two rules. Under art 7(1), a person who produces "full powers" during
negotiation has authority to represent the state. Three officials do not need to produce full powers because
they are assumed to already have the authority: heads of state, heads of government, and ministers for foreign
affairs. Everyone else negotiating or signing a treaty on a state's behalf should produce a letter of full
powers.
:::

## Australia's treaty-making process

Australia's treaty-making process runs through DFAT's International Law: Advising and Treaties Section, in
six key steps.

1. **Mandate to negotiate.** The lead minister must seek a mandate to negotiate from the Minister for Foreign
   Affairs (which may involve consulting other Commonwealth agencies); the Prime Minister and any minister
   with a portfolio interest must be informed.
2. **Negotiation and finalisation of the text.** Lead agencies consult throughout with other interested
   Commonwealth, state and territory bodies and stakeholders.
3. **Ministerial and Executive Council (ExCo) approval.** All treaty actions — signing, ratifying, amending,
   terminating or withdrawing — must be approved by ExCo; the lead minister first seeks the agreement of the
   Minister for Foreign Affairs, the Attorney-General and other interested ministers, and the Prime Minister
   is informed.
4. **Signature.** Once ExCo approval is received, Australia's representative signs; if the signatory is not
   the Prime Minister or Minister for Foreign Affairs, the Minister for Foreign Affairs issues an instrument
   of full powers.
5. **Scrutiny by Parliament.** The treaty is tabled in both Houses for the Joint Standing Committee on
   Treaties (JSCOT), which reports within 15 or 20 joint sitting days on whether binding treaty action should
   be taken; a National Interest Analysis (NIA) accompanies the treaty, covering its social, economic and
   political impact, why it is in Australia's interest to become a party, how it will be implemented
   domestically, whether Australia could withdraw, and any consultation undertaken.
6. **Entry into force.** Once any implementing legislation has passed, arrangements are made for entry into
   force — for bilateral treaties usually an exchange of diplomatic notes; for multilateral treaties, ExCo
   approval to deposit the instrument of ratification. Ratification is an international act, usually a
   ratification letter that is deposited: for a treaty negotiated within the UN, it is often the UN
   Secretary-General who is the depositary, and that is where the letter of ratification is sent.

:::examtip
A National Interest Analysis (NIA) is not a rubber stamp: it sets out the treaty's purpose, the reasons
Australia should take the proposed action, the obligations it creates, how it will be implemented
domestically, whether Australia could later withdraw, and what consultation occurred — exactly the questions
the tutorial hypothetical about the Fisheries Cooperation Accord asks you to work through.
:::

## Case study: AUKUS and Australia's treaty-making process in action

The Agreement for the Exchange of Naval Nuclear Propulsion Information (ENNPIA) between Australia, the UK and
the US (Canberra, 22 November 2021) shows this process at work. Its NIA explains that ENNPIA "establish[es] a
legally-binding framework for the disclosure and use of information related to naval nuclear propulsion", is
necessary because the US and UK cannot disclose such restricted information to Australia without it, and
requires no changes to Australian law. It provides for termination on six months' written notice by any
Party, with continuing obligations on non-disclosure surviving termination.

A later, broader Agreement for Cooperation Related to Naval Nuclear Propulsion (which remains in force until
31 December 2075) went through the full JSCOT process: JSCOT Report 224 (November 2024) received 260
submissions, held four public hearings, and recommended binding treaty action be taken. At the hearings,
Professor Donald Rothwell argued the Agreement's withdrawal provisions gave the UK and US "an enormous amount
of flexibility" because only they can be an "Originating Party" able to withdraw if cooperation no longer
"promote[s]" and "will not constitute an unreasonable risk to" their "defence and security", while Australia's
own room to withdraw is comparatively narrow — an asymmetry the Australia Institute also raised, urging more
flexibility for future Australian governments.

## Consent to be bound, entry into force and provisional application

Consent to be bound (arts 11–17 VCLT) is not the same thing as entry into force (art 24), and the distinction
matters because a long gap can separate the two.

:::rule
Article 11 VCLT lists several ways of consenting to be bound — signature, exchange of instruments,
ratification, acceptance, approval or accession — but for this unit we focus on signature and ratification,
the two Australia actually uses.
:::

:::rule
Article 14 VCLT: ratification is required if it is specified in the treaty itself; the negotiating parties
agreed it was needed; the representative who signed did so subject to ratification; or it appeared from the
representative's full powers, or was expressed during negotiation, that this was the state's intention.
:::

How do you know whether a treaty needs ratification, or can simply be signed? Usually it is found in the
treaty's own provisions, but it also depends on how a state approaches consent to be bound. Common law
countries often follow a dualist system: before a treaty is integrated into national law it needs to be
transformed into national law, and that transformation is often done before the state ratifies — so in
Australia's case, ratification (rather than signature alone) is usually the way it happens, especially for
multilateral treaties. Bilateral treaties more often do not require ratification.

:::rule
"A treaty enters into force in such manner and upon such date as it may provide or as the negotiating States
may agree" (art 24 VCLT).
:::

The 1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) shows why the distinction matters: it was opened for
ratification in 1982 with around 100 signatures, but art 306 of the Convention says it enters into force 12
months after the 60th instrument of ratification is deposited — and the 60th ratification was not reached
until 1994, twelve years later. A state that signed and even ratified in the 1980s was not bound by all of
UNCLOS's provisions until it actually entered into force.

:::rule
Article 18 VCLT: a state is obliged to refrain from acts that would defeat the object and purpose of a treaty
it has signed subject to ratification (until it makes clear it will not become a party), or that it has
consented to be bound by, pending entry into force not being unduly delayed. Article 25 VCLT: a treaty (or
part of it) is applied provisionally, pending entry into force, if the treaty itself so provides or the
negotiating states have otherwise agreed.
:::

Article 18's obligation is deliberately limited — a signatory does not have to abide by "the nitty-gritty
details" of a treaty not yet in force, only refrain from defeating its object and purpose — whereas art 25
provisional application can mean applying part, or even all, of a treaty before it is formally in force,
whether the treaty itself says so or the parties agree so separately, and not every party to the eventual
treaty needs to sign up to provisional application.

## Invalidity of treaties

A treaty can seem perfectly concluded and still be invalid. The VCLT sorts grounds for invalidity into two
categories.

:::rule
**Relative invalidity** (issue with consent, arts 46–50): error (art 48), fraud (art 49), corruption of a
state's representative (art 50). **Absolute invalidity** (arts 51–53): coercion of a representative (art 51),
threat or use of force against the state (art 52), and conflict with a peremptory norm of general
international law — jus cogens (art 53).
:::

Error: if a factual situation a state relied on to become party to a treaty was actually wrong — the classic
(now rare) example is an error on a map material to the decision to join — the treaty can be void. Fraud: a
state or its representative fraudulently misleads another representative (for example, deliberately producing
a map with errors) to get them to sign. Corruption: a state offers a non-due advantage to another state's
negotiating representative to get them to sign, when they otherwise would not have. Coercion of a
representative: economic pressure, blackmail, or a threat to a representative's life. Threat or use of force
against the state itself is treated as absolute invalidity — the treaty is void ab initio.

:::rule
"A treaty is void if, at the time of its conclusion, it conflicts with a peremptory norm of general
international law. For the purposes of the present Convention, a peremptory norm of general international law
is a norm accepted and recognized by the international community of States as a whole as a norm from which no
derogation is permitted …" (art 53 VCLT). Examples given: the prohibition of torture and genocide, the
prohibition of aggression, the ban on crimes against humanity, the prohibition of piracy, and the right to
self-determination.
:::

:::case
*Case Concerning the [Land] and Maritime Boundary between Cameroon and Nigeria (Cameroon v Nigeria: Equatorial
Guinea intervening)* — a state cannot invoke the fact that its consent to be bound was expressed in violation
of its own internal law, unless the violation was "manifest and concerned a rule of its internal law of
fundamental importance" (art 46 VCLT). As a general rule this should not be possible unless it is very
obvious — a change of government whose new government simply does not want to be bound by what a previous
government agreed to is not, without more, a manifest violation.
:::

## Reservations

:::rule
"'reservation' means a unilateral statement, however phrased or named, made by a State, when signing,
ratifying, accepting, approving or acceding to a treaty, whereby it purports to exclude or to modify the legal
effect of certain provisions of the treaty in their application to that State" (art 2(1)(d) VCLT). A
reservation is not the same as an interpretative declaration — an expression of a state's view on what a
treaty means, not put forward as a condition of being bound, and only explanatory in character.
:::

Reservations exist because, in a multilateral negotiation with (sometimes) 160-plus states, it is much less
likely that every state will agree to every single provision than in a bilateral treaty — so international law
lets a state become party to a multilateral treaty without accepting every one of its provisions.

:::rule
Article 19 VCLT: a state may make a reservation to a multilateral treaty unless (a) the treaty prohibits
reservations altogether, (b) the treaty prohibits that specific reservation, or (c) the reservation is
incompatible with the object and purpose of the treaty. A reservation that a state will not commit to limit
its emissions under a treaty aimed at eliminating ozone-depleting substances, for example, would conflict with
the very objective of the treaty and so could not be relied on.
:::

:::rule
Article 20 VCLT sets the effect of a permissible reservation. Under art 20(4): other states may **accept** the
reservation (then both are parties to the treaty and the reservation together); other states may **object**
(then the treaty does not enter into force between the reserving and objecting states); and a reservation only
becomes effective once at least one other contracting state has accepted it — if none has, it has no effect at
all. A reservation expressly authorised by the treaty does not need any other party's acceptance. Under art
20(2), if the limited number of negotiating states and the treaty's object and purpose show the treaty must be
accepted in its totality by all parties, unanimous acceptance is required instead. Under art 20(3), a
reservation to the constituent instrument of an international organisation needs the acceptance of that
organisation's competent organ.
:::

What is the legal effect of an *impermissible* reservation? This remains genuinely unresolved in the academic
literature, and three solutions have been put forward: (1) the reserving state does not become a party to the
treaty at all, and no treaty obligations arise for it; (2) the "severance" option — ignore the invalid
reservation and treat the reserving state as a full party bound by the whole treaty, including the provision
it tried to reserve against (this sits uneasily with the rule that a state is only bound by what it consents
to); (3) the reserving state is presumed a full party without the benefit of the reservation, unless it has
expressly made clear it only wished to be bound on condition the reservation was accepted. Either way, an
invalid reservation is null and void, ab initio and as a matter of fact — as if it never existed — and no
other state is obliged to object to or accept it, though any state may still formulate a reasoned objection
explaining why it considers itself unbound.

## Treaty interpretation

Various rules for interpreting treaties have been proposed over the years — the plain-meaning, overall
context, teleological and effectiveness approaches — but the present regime is arts 31–32 VCLT, recognised by
the International Court of Justice as reflecting customary international law.

:::rule
"A treaty shall be interpreted in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning to be given to the terms
of the treaty in their context and in the light of its object and purpose" (art 31(1) VCLT). Context includes
the treaty's text, preamble and annexes, and any agreement or instrument relating to the treaty made in
connection with its conclusion — for example, a separate agreement the parties draw up specifically
concerning the interpretation of the treaty.
:::

:::rule
"Recourse may be had to supplementary means of interpretation, including the preparatory work of the treaty
and the circumstances of its conclusion … when the interpretation according to article 31: (a) leaves the
meaning ambiguous or obscure; or (b) leads to a result which is manifestly absurd or unreasonable" (art 32
VCLT).
:::

Interpretation is a holistic, sequential exercise, not a free-standing choice between methods: courts and
tribunals first seek the text's objective meaning under art 31, and only turn to the *travaux préparatoires*
under art 32 to confirm that meaning — they will not resort to preparatory work if the text is already
sufficiently clear, and they never try to rewrite the treaty or "legislate from scratch". Determining whether,
say, deploying armed forces along a border qualifies as a "threat of force" under art 2(4) of the UN Charter
is exactly this kind of interpretive, fact-specific exercise: it turns on assessing the meaning and scope of
undefined treaty terms and applying them to the circumstances at hand.

Subsequent practice of the parties, or a subsequent agreement between them, is an important mode of
interpretation provided in art 31, but caution is needed about what subsequent practice is relevant and how far
it can affect the ordinary meaning of the terms. An "agreement concerning the interpretation of Treaty X" is
obviously relevant; whether other, later treaties can be taken into account for the interpretation of a previous
treaty has to be ascertained on a case-by-case basis, always having regard to the object and purpose of the
treaty — maybe there is some connection in the main text, or some reference to the previous treaty in the
negotiations leading to the subsequent treaty.

:::case
*South China Sea Arbitration (The Philippines v China)* — the tribunal applied both the primary (art 31) and
supplementary (art 32) means of interpretation to art 121 UNCLOS's "regime of islands" ("an island is a
naturally formed area of land, surrounded by water, which is above water at high tide … rocks which cannot
sustain human habitation or economic life of their own shall have no exclusive economic zone or continental
shelf") and concluded that disputed features including the Spratly Islands did not have the legal status of
islands — a rock is entitled only to 12 nautical miles, an island to a full 200 nautical mile exclusive
economic zone, so how a feature is classified is critically important to the parties' maritime entitlements.
:::

## Termination, suspension and withdrawal

The materials treat withdrawal as the practical way out of a treaty rather than invalidity. In Australia, all
treaty actions — including terminating or withdrawing — must be approved by ExCo, and the National Interest
Analysis addresses whether it is easy for Australia to withdraw from the treaty. The ENNPIA, for example, can be
terminated on six months' written notice by any Party, and the withdrawal terms of the later AUKUS Agreement
were the main criticism raised before JSCOT. In the US, the Paris Agreement shows withdrawal (and rejoining) by
executive order alone.

:::examtip
A worked hypothetical from the live lecture: during a war, a state withdraws from a treaty guaranteeing safe
passage of food and grain cargo ships, citing a third-party threat (heavy sanctions imposed by another state).
Withdrawal is likely the state's easiest way out, but once armed conflict crosses the threshold into a
use-of-force situation, ordinary peacetime coercion/threat-of-force analysis for invalidity does not simply
transplant across — a war changes which body of law (the law of armed conflict) is really in play.
:::
