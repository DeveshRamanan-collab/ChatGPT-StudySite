## 1. Overview

This week we discuss the general principles governing breach of duty, how courts assess the reasonableness of the
defendant's response to a foreseeable risk, obvious versus inherent risks, and who the "reasonable person" is.

If a duty of care is established in the given circumstances, the next step is to prove that the duty was breached.
However, as noted in the duty weeks, there may be an overlap between inquiries related to the existence of the duty and
its breach.

:::examtip
Treat this area as common law modified by statute. A fully formed law of negligence existed before the Civil Liability
Act 2002 (NSW); the Act presupposes it and operates against its background, so the cases remain important even where
the Act applies. Where the Act applies, cite s 5B — and you can cite *Wyong* and *Benic* with it.
:::

## 2. General principles governing breach of duty

### The standard of care: reasonable care, judged objectively

- The breach should be established on the **balance of probabilities**.
- There are **no degrees of carelessness**: the defendant will be liable whether her conduct was marginally careless or
  completely reckless. In the flowchart's words, it makes no difference whether the conduct falls far short or just
  short of the required standard.
- Courts apply the **test of a reasonable person**. The defendant's conduct is judged against an objective standard,
  so a defendant may be in breach even though they did their personal best, if their personal best was not reasonable.

:::definition
*Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co* (1856), Alderson B: "Negligence is the omission to do something which a reasonable
man … would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do." (The flowchart cites the case as
[1856] 56 ER 1047 and attributes the words to "Anderson"; the slides name Alderson B.)
:::

The slides ask: if this principle was laid down in 1856, what is the significance of *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932)? The
lecture's answer: *Donoghue* established negligence as a separate, standalone cause of action.

:::rule
The duty is only a duty to take reasonable care; it is not an absolute duty to avoid injury. The measure is
reasonableness, not elimination of risk (*Swain v Waverley Municipal Council* (2005) 220 CLR 517, 520 [5], Gleeson CJ,
as cited in the flowchart). An obligation to exercise reasonable care must be contrasted with an obligation to prevent
harm occurring to others (*Dederer*, Gummow J).
:::

### The common law test: Wyong Shire Council v Shirt

At common law, the test governing breach had two limbs (*Wyong Shire Council v Shirt* (1980) 146 CLR 40):

1. reasonable foreseeability of a **real risk** of injury arising from the defendant's conduct — foreseeability being
   what the defendant knew or ought to have known at the time of the conduct; and
2. the reasonableness of the defendant's **response** to that risk.

A risk of injury which is remote in the sense that it is extremely unlikely to occur may nevertheless constitute a
foreseeable risk. A risk which is not far-fetched or fanciful is real and therefore foreseeable.

### The Civil Liability Act: ss 5B and 5C

- Breach is governed by ss 5B and 5C. The common law still applies in some areas (see s 3B).
- The Act has **three separate elements** — already represented in the common law but often conflated — which now must
  be separately addressed.
- The Act modified the probability of harm from "not far-fetched and fanciful" to **"not insignificant"**.
- The Act presupposes the existing law of negligence and operates against its background. The case law is still
  important.

:::case
*Benic v NSW* [2010] NSWSC 1039, Garling J: "The three separate elements in s 5B(1) represent the concepts of
foreseeability, probability and reasonableness of precautions … These concepts are each represented in the common law,
and are often conflated in the term 'reasonable foreseeability' but the statute now makes it clear that each must be
separately addressed."
:::

:::statute
Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) s 5B(1): A person is not negligent in failing to take precautions against a risk of harm
unless — (a) the risk was foreseeable (that is, it is a risk of which the person knew or ought to have known), and (b)
the risk was not insignificant, and (c) in the circumstances, a reasonable person in the person's position would have
taken those precautions.
:::

Section 5B sits in Division 2 of Part 1A, headed "Duty of care". The lecturer called this a trap: s 5B is the test for
breach, not for establishing a duty of care. All three paragraphs must be met — they are joined by "and".

### The flowchart's two questions

The textbook flowchart (written for the WA Act, whose s 5B has the same (1)(a)–(c) and (2)(a)–(d) structure) asks two
questions, which match *Wyong*'s two limbs:

1. **Was the RISK of injury to the plaintiff reasonably foreseeable?** — what standard of care does the defendant owe?
   The higher the degree of risk, the less reasonable it will be for the defendant to fail to take steps to prevent it
   occurring. Section 5B(1)(a) and (b) relate to this question.
2. **Was the RESPONSE of the defendant to this risk reasonable?** — has the standard been met? Section 5B(1)(c) and
   (2)(a)–(d) relate to this question.

:::examtip
Cite the NSW Act. The WA wording in the flowchart opens "A person is not liable for harm caused by that person's fault
unless"; NSW s 5B(1) reads "A person is not negligent in failing to take precautions against a risk of harm unless".
:::

### Benic: the steps for the breach question

Garling J in *Benic* laid down steps to be followed in determining the breach question under the Act:

1. **Identify a risk of harm.** It is sufficient "if the risk of harm is described as a class of injury, as distinct
   from the particular injury actually suffered by the plaintiff". You cannot think about "precautions against a risk
   of harm" until you know what the risk of harm is.
2. **Consider the three elements** in s 5B(1).

### Step 1: identifying the risk of harm — Dederer

:::case
*Roads and Traffic Authority of NSW v Dederer* (2007) 234 CLR 330 — a 14-year-old dived off a bridge into an estuary
with a shifting sandbar; there were pictograph "no diving" signs; he suffered partial paraplegia; jumping from the
bridge was a widespread practice that had never previously injured anyone. The Court of Appeal characterised the risk
as a "serious spinal injury flowing from the act of diving off the bridge". Gummow J: "such a characterisation of the
risk obscured the true source of potential injury [which arose] from impact upon jumping into the potentially shallow
water and shifting sands of the estuary" [60]. Held 3:2 (Gummow, Callinan and Heydon JJ): the obligation was to exercise
reasonable care, not to prevent harm; the risk was foreseeable but the probability low; the existing signs warning of,
and prohibiting, diving were a reasonable response. No breach.
:::

:::rule
When identifying the risk, be specific enough that the risk you identify corresponds to the type of precautions that
should have been taken. You cannot formulate the risk so broadly that it encompasses fundamentally different scenarios
requiring fundamentally different responses.
:::

### Element 1 — s 5B(1)(a): the risk was foreseeable

The first element concerns the foreseeability of the risk of harm. It corresponds to the first limb of the common law
test — but remember that the foreseeability inquiry is different at the breach stage.

- At the breach stage the question involves an examination of the **actual conduct** of the defendant. Compared with
  the duty stage (a more general test), it is a **more specific test**, which requires the court to consider the actual
  risk of injury in the circumstances of the case. The lecturer explained that the side that becomes specific is the
  defendant's conduct; the harm side stays general until remoteness.
- Either **actual or constructive knowledge** of the risk will suffice: what the defendant actually knew, or what a
  reasonable person in the position of the defendant ought to have known — judged at the time of the conduct, not with
  hindsight.
- "Reasonable foreseeability is a fact-value complex … with inherent questions of fairness, policy, practicality,
  proportion, expense, and justice": *Swain* (2005) 220 CLR 517, 547 [79] (McHugh J), as cited in the flowchart.
- The plaintiff does not have to show that the precise manner of the injury, or the particular injury, was reasonably
  foreseeable — just a reasonably foreseeable risk of injury of some kind to someone such as the plaintiff (the lecture
  pointed back to *Chapman v Hearse* on the sequence of events). The defendant must also foresee and guard against
  inadvertent, negligent, or even extremely foolhardy conduct by the plaintiff, even though this is unlikely: *Nagle v
  Rottnest Island Board* (1993) 177 CLR 423; *McLean v Tedman* (1984) 155 CR 306 (flowchart).

:::case
*Wyong Shire Council v Shirt* (1980) 146 CLR 40 — while water skiing, Shirt struck his head on the bottom of a shallow
lake and became quadriplegic; the water was only about 3.5–4 feet deep at that point. He alleged he was misled by a
"deep water" sign the Council had erected to warn swimmers about deeper channels alongside a jetty. A jury found a
breach; the High Court held that finding was not unreasonable. Mason J: "foreseeability of the risk of injury and the
likelihood of that risk occurring are two different things … A risk of injury which is quite unlikely to occur … may
nevertheless be plainly foreseeable." A reasonable man might well have concluded that the sign was ambiguous and might
induce a water skier to ski in that zone, mistakenly believing it to be deep.
:::

### Element 2 — s 5B(1)(b): the risk was not insignificant

The second element reflects the **probability** of the risk materialising: it may not be satisfied if there was a very
low probability of the occurrence of injury. As noted in the Ipp Report, the choice of double negative is intentional:

:::definition
Ipp Report: "The phrase 'not insignificant' is intended to indicate a risk that is of a higher probability than is
indicated by the phrase 'not far fetched and fanciful', but not so high as might be indicated by a phrase such as 'a
substantial risk'."
:::

So, on a scale from insignificant to substantial, "not far-fetched or fanciful" (the common law) sits low; "not
insignificant" sits a little higher; "substantial" sits well above both. The lecturer called "not insignificant" a
slightly more demanding test than "not far-fetched or fanciful".

The NSW Court of Appeal in *Rail Corporation NSW v Donald* [2018] NSWCA 82 (not in the text; the slide is the source)
noted that the significance of a risk depends on several factors, such as **obviousness**, **likelihood of
occurrence** and **seriousness of consequence**. They are weighed together; not every factor needs to point the same way.

The flowchart says it is not yet completely clear what s 5B(1)(b) requires, and offers as arguable markers a "real risk"
(*Bolton v Stone*) that is not far-fetched or fanciful (*Wyong*). Read that with the slides: "not far-fetched or
fanciful" is the common law threshold the Act raised, so showing only that a risk clears it is not enough on its own.

:::examtip
The marks are in the application. Whether you conclude a risk is not insignificant matters less than showing how the
law applies to the facts — name the risk, then weigh obviousness, likelihood and seriousness.
:::

## 3. Responding to the foreseeable risk: s 5B(1)(c) and (2)

If the harm to the plaintiff was foreseeable and the risk was "not insignificant", the focus shifts to what precautions
a reasonable person in the position of the defendant would have taken. The concept of the standard of care is implicit
here: s 5B(1)(c) asks what standard of care — what precautions — a reasonable person would have taken in the
circumstances. Courts consider several factors as part of the **"calculus of negligence"**.

:::statute
Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) s 5B(2): In determining whether a reasonable person would have taken precautions against
a risk of harm, the court is to consider the following (amongst other relevant things) — (a) the probability that the
harm would occur if care were not taken, (b) the likely seriousness of the harm, (c) the burden of taking precautions to
avoid the risk of harm, (d) the social utility of the activity that creates the risk of harm.
:::

The factors are weighed together. The lecturers stressed that this is not a checklist and there is no algorithm: no
factor is more important than another, and not every factor arises in every case.

### Probability of harm — Dederer

In *Dederer* the RTA knew people jumped from the bridge and that riverbed levels were continually altered; 1992 safety
standards required vertical rather than horizontal railing bars, but no modification was made. The plaintiff argued the
signs should have warned of the shifting sands and the railings should have been replaced. Gummow J: the probability
should be judged **prospectively** (at the time of the alleged negligence), not retrospectively; while the magnitude of
risk was grave, the probability of injury was low despite the frequency of jumping (Dederer was the first to be
injured); and considering the expense, difficulty and inconvenience, a reasonable response did not demand the measures
suggested. The warning signs were reasonable steps. "If the [R]TA exercised reasonable care, it would not be liable even
if the risk-taking conduct continued."

### Seriousness of the harm — Paris

The standard of care will increase with the seriousness of harm. If the defendant knows of the plaintiff's particular
vulnerability to greater injury, a higher standard of care will be expected.

:::case
*Paris v Stepney Borough Council* [1951] AC 367 — a garage worker with only one good eye injured it when hit by a piece
of metal; the employer knew of his condition but did not provide goggles, although supplying them was not ordinary
industry practice at the time. The House of Lords held by majority that the duty was breached. Lord Macdermott: "the
known circumstance that a particular workman is likely to suffer a graver injury than his fellows from the happening of
a given event is one which must be taken into consideration in assessing the nature of the employer's obligation to
that workman."
:::

The standard of care is not affected by the plaintiff's intoxication: s 49(1) — the fact that a person is or may be
intoxicated does not of itself increase or otherwise affect the standard of care owed to the person.

### Burden of taking precautions — Woods

The burden may relate to cost or to other practical considerations, such as the availability of safety equipment and
convenience.

:::case
*Woods v Multi-Sport Holdings* (2002) 208 CLR 460 — Woods suffered serious injury to one eye playing indoor cricket; the
organiser supplied no helmets. There were no specific helmets for indoor cricket and the rules did not require them. The
High Court held by majority that the duty was not breached. Gleeson CJ noted the plaintiff was an adult and the risk of
some injury was likely, though only two serious eye injuries a year were reported among 12,500 players in WA. The burden
limb is not confined to financial cost: headgear had not been designed for indoor cricket, was not worn elsewhere, was
not required by the rules and raised its own convenience and safety concerns.
:::

### Social utility of the risk-taking activity — Red Cross

Courts consider the social utility and public benefits of activities that create a risk of harm; emergency vehicles are
a case in point.

:::case
*E v Australian Red Cross Society* (1991) 27 FCR 310 — the plaintiff was infected with AIDS through a blood transfusion.
The issue was whether the Society took adequate steps (including introducing Hepatitis B surrogate testing) to screen
donors. The Federal Court held there was no breach in not introducing the test earlier than 1984: while the test might
not reduce the risk of infection, it would have meant the likely loss of 5% of all blood donations.
:::

### Weighing the calculus: Romeo and Refrigerated Roadways

- *Romeo v Conservation Commission* (1998) 192 CLR 431 (week 6): Kirby J — whether the defendant met the standard "must
  be assessed on the facts of each case with reference to consideration such as those collected by Mason J in Shirt",
  which "provide a framework for determining which risks the defendant should guard against and which it can safely
  ignore". No breach: the perceived magnitude of risk, the remote possibility of an accident, the expense, difficulty
  and inconvenience of alleviating conduct and the Commission's other priorities. Decided before the Act, it still
  stands as sound principle.
- *RTA of NSW v Refrigerated Roadways* [2009] NSWCA 263 (week 8): Campbell JA — "Section 5B presupposes the existence of
  the law of negligence, and operates against its background." It is always predictable that someone might drop an
  object from a bridge, and that is not far-fetched or fanciful; but before a roads authority is negligent for failing
  to screen the bridge, it must have failed to take a reasonable step that a reasonable roads authority would have
  taken. No breach.

### Section 5C: other principles

:::statute
Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) s 5C: In proceedings relating to liability for negligence — (a) the burden of taking
precautions to avoid a risk of harm includes the burden of taking precautions to avoid similar risks of harm for which
the person may be responsible, and (b) the fact that a risk of harm could have been avoided by doing something in a
different way does not of itself give rise to or affect liability for the way in which the thing was done, and (c) the
subsequent taking of action that would (had the action been taken earlier) have avoided a risk of harm does not of
itself give rise to or affect liability in respect of the risk and does not of itself constitute an admission of
liability in connection with the risk.
:::

The lecturer also noted that **industry standards** are information a court can take into account in deciding what was
reasonable, but they are not the legal test — and only standards released before the accident count, because the
question is judged at the time.

## 4. Obvious versus inherent risks

At common law, courts considered whether the risk was inherent or obvious in deciding breach. **Obvious risks** are
risks which should be obvious to any reasonable person (see s 5F). **Inherent risks** are those which cannot be avoided
by taking reasonable care (see s 5I). If a risk is obvious or inherent, it changes how a reasonable person would respond
to it.

When the risk is obvious, courts may treat it as relevant to the scope of the duty of care (Kirby J in *Romeo*); later
courts treated it as relevant to breach — recreational activities are a typical example. Now the Act bears directly on
the issue:

- **s 5G**: a person who suffers harm is presumed to have been aware of an obvious risk unless they prove, on the balance
  of probabilities, that they were not aware of it; awareness of the type or kind of risk is enough.
- **s 5H**: there is no proactive duty to warn of an obvious risk unless an exception applies — for example, the plaintiff
  requested advice or information about the risk from the defendant.
- **s 5L**: no liability for harm from the materialisation of an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity engaged
  in by the plaintiff.
- **s 5I**: a person "is not liable in negligence for harm suffered by another person as a result of the materialisation
  of an inherent risk". Whether something is an inherent risk is a question of fact — but the duty to warn of a risk
  remains (s 5I(3)).

These provisions work much like defences and are covered in detail in the defences week.

## 5. Unpacking the reasonable person

Courts assess the defendant's conduct against the standard of the "reasonable person" in s 5B(1)(c). The reasonable
person is a legal fiction created by judges to apply the objective test, because there are too many subjective
variables. The concept first appeared in *Vaughan v Menlove* (1837) as "the man of ordinary prudence".

Is the test entirely objective, or is it influenced by the attributes of a given judge? The earlier formulation of the
"reasonable man" has been heavily criticised by feminist scholars: judges invariably had a man in mind when applying the
test; the judiciary was predominantly male; and male attributes were picked (for example, emotions were ignored).

The judicial construction of the reasonable person is not completely devoid of particular characteristics: courts
consider, for example, age, mental ability and professional expertise.

### Age — McHale v Watson

:::case
*McHale v Watson* (1966) 115 CLR 199 — Watson, a 12-year-old boy, threw a sharpened piece of scrap metal at a tree; it
bounced off and injured McHale's eye. The High Court held by majority that the standard of care for children is that of
a normal child of comparable age (McTiernan ACJ: it is appropriate to consider the defendant's age). Kitto J: "The
standard of care being objective, it is no answer for him, any more than it is for an adult, to say that the harm he
caused was due to his being abnormally slow-witted, quick-tempered, absent-minded, or inexperienced. But it does not
follow that he cannot rely in his defence upon a limitation upon the capacity for foresight or prudence … being
characteristic of humanity at his stage of development."
:::

### Mental illness or disability — Carrier v Bonham

:::case
*Carrier v Bonham* [2002] 1 Qd R 474 — Bonham, a psychiatric patient with schizophrenia, escaped from hospital and
intentionally jumped in front of a bus driven by Carrier, who braked but could not avoid him and suffered nervous shock.
The Queensland Court of Appeal held that the defendant's mental condition had no effect on the standard of care
expected. McPherson JA: "it would be impossible to devise a standard by which the tortious liability of such persons
could be judged as a class"; "Unsoundness of mind is not a normal condition in most people, and it is not a stage of
development through which all humanity is destined to pass."
:::

### Inexperience — learner drivers

The standard of care for a learner driver is the same as for a licensed driver: *Imbree v McNeilly* [2008] HCA 40. The
standard is objective and impersonal: it does not vary with the particular driver's experience, and it would be
unworkable to apply different standards to drivers based on their experience level.

### Professionals — Rogers v Whitaker and s 5O

:::case
*Rogers v Whitaker* (1992) 175 CLR 479 — an eye surgeon failed to warn of a complication arising in one in 14,000 cases.
The High Court held that the standard of care "is that of ordinary skilled person exercising and professing to have that
special skill", and rejected the *Bolam* principle (that the standard of care is determined in line with medical
practice).
:::

Section 5O of the Act incorporates a modified version of the *Bolam* principle.

:::statute
Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) s 5O: (1) A person practising a profession (a professional) does not incur a liability in
negligence arising from the provision of a professional service if it is established that the professional acted in a
manner that (at the time the service was provided) was widely accepted in Australia by peer professional opinion as
competent professional practice. (2) However, peer professional opinion cannot be relied on … if the court considers
that the opinion is irrational. (3) Differing peer professional opinions widely accepted in Australia do not prevent any
one or more (or all) of them being relied on. (4) Peer professional opinion does not have to be universally accepted to
be considered widely accepted.
:::

Do we use s 5B, s 5O or both? *Peterkin v Tadros* [2026] NSWSC 1185 [151]–[154] gives the answer: **s 5O is a defence.**

- The defendant bears the onus of establishing its preconditions: that they were practising in a profession, and that
  they were doing so in a manner widely accepted in Australia by peer professional opinion as competent professional
  practice — subject to the rationality requirement in s 5O(2).
- Where s 5O is pleaded, apply it first. If it is made out, it **supplants** the analysis otherwise required by s 5B:
  the defendant has not breached their duty (citing *South-Western Sydney Local Health District v Gould* (2018) 97 NSWLR
  513; [2018] NSWCA 69).
- If the s 5O defence fails, the plaintiff still needs to satisfy ss 5B and 5C to establish breach.

## 6. Answer order for breach

:::examtip
1. State the standard: reasonable care, judged by the test of a reasonable person (adjusted for age, not for mental
   illness or inexperience). If the defendant is a professional who pleads s 5O, deal with s 5O first.
2. Benic step 1: identify the risk of harm — a class of injury, specific enough to match the precautions in issue
   (*Dederer*).
3. Benic step 2: s 5B(1)(a) foreseeable (knew or ought to have known; not far-fetched or fanciful — *Wyong*); (b) not
   insignificant (higher than not far-fetched — Ipp; obviousness, likelihood, seriousness — *Donald*); (c) the
   precautions a reasonable person in the defendant's position would have taken, weighing s 5B(2)(a)–(d) with the cases
   and s 5C. All three must be met.
:::
