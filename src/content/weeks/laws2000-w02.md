## Article 38(1) and the sources of international law

Last week we talked about what international law is and how it affects our lives. International law is not
something we can easily compare with domestic law — we can't completely transpose the domestic legal regime
into international law because we don't have a legislator, we don't have those compulsory courts. Because we
don't have a legislator, the sources of international law are quite different to what we find in domestic law.

When we are identifying sources of international law, we will always start with this article — this is the
first place of reference, Article 38 of the ICJ Statute. The ICJ Statute, at this article, identified the
sources: international conventions (treaties), international custom, general principles of law, and — as a
subsidiary means — judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists.

:::definition
Article 38(1) ICJ Statute distinguishes primary sources — treaties, customary international law and general
principles of law — the real, true sources of binding obligations by states, from secondary sources — judicial
decisions and scholarly writings — which are not law-creating in themselves; they help us interpret the
international legal principles found in the primary sources.
:::

This article was not designed and drafted to give us a clear reference to what are the sources of
international law — that is not the purpose of the article, so there are things we can add to that list
later on. Article 38 doesn't provide an exhaustive list of sources.

:::examtip
"There is a clear hierarchy between the sources of international law as laid out in Article 38(1)" is a claim
to analyse critically, not accept. The ordering in Article 38(1)(a)-(d) tracks the primary/secondary,
law-creating/law-interpreting distinction — it does not rank treaty above custom, or custom above general
principles. A treaty and a customary rule can bind the same states on the same question simultaneously.
:::

## Treaties

A treaty is "an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by
international law" (Article 2(1)(a), Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties) — an agreement between
sovereign States, and in some cases international organisations (e.g. the EU), which is binding at
international law. Treaties are also known as 'agreements', 'conventions', 'protocols' or 'covenants', and
less commonly 'exchanges of letters': the name of the treaty as such doesn't really have an impact on whether
we have a treaty in front of us — what really counts when assessing whether a document is a treaty is whether
the parties have consented to be bound. Treaties can be bilateral (between two States) or multilateral
(between three or more States).

:::rule
Pacta sunt servanda: states are bound to honour their treaty-based obligations. If a state has become a party
to an international treaty, that implies consent to be bound by the legal obligations established by the
treaty.
:::

Treaties are the first source listed at Article 38 — probably the most important source of international law
that we have, and the most direct evidence of the existence of a legal obligation: it is really easy, when you
have a treaty, to see if the state has accepted the obligation, because if the state has ratified the treaty,
if it has become a party to the treaty, clearly it has accepted to be bound by that obligation. A State party
to an international treaty must ensure that its own domestic law and practice are consistent with what is
required by the treaty; the treaty may give general guidance on the measures to be taken at the domestic law
level, and a state shouldn't act or create laws that would be contrary to the obligation it has undertaken.

Treaties are also usually how international organisations are created: the UN Charter is the treaty that
created the UN and explains the powers of that organisation, and NATO was created the same way — that's
something that cannot be done otherwise than by treaty. In this unit we focus on treaties covered by the
Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties of 1969, though a treaty, generally understood, can also be concluded
between international organisations and states, and doesn't need to be in writing.

## Customary international law

Customary international law arises when a particular conduct (way of behaving) is (a) followed as a general
practice among states — consistent repetition of a particular behaviour, for a considerable period of time,
when confronted with the same facts (the objective element) — and (b) accepted by those states as legally
binding, opinio juris sive necessitatis: acts motivated by a desire to honour, or create, a legal obligation
(the subjective-psychological element).

:::definition
The objective element (practice) is comparatively easy: you look at what states do, or omit to do. Opinio
juris is more tricky, because states won't necessarily tell you why they are acting in a certain way — you
have to infer, from other actions and statements, that they believe the practice is legally required, not
just a matter of courtesy, convenience or tradition (like rules of diplomatic protocol, or sending humanitarian
aid after a disaster).
:::

Neither element needs to be perfect. Practice needs to be consistent and uniform, but that doesn't require
absolute uniformity — a practice can be imperfect and still crystallise into a rule.

:::case
Military and Paramilitary Activities in and against Nicaragua (Nicaragua v United States of America) [1986]
ICJ Rep 14: "The Court does not consider that, for a rule to be established as customary, the corresponding
practice must be in absolutely rigorous conformity with the rule. In order to deduce the existence of
customary rules, the Court deems it sufficient that the conduct of States should, in general, be consistent
with such rules, and that instances of State conduct inconsistent with a given rule should generally have been
treated as breaches of that rule, not as indications of the recognition of a new rule." A state that defends
inconsistent conduct by invoking an exception within the rule itself confirms, rather than weakens, the rule.
:::

:::case
North Sea Continental Shelf Cases (Federal Republic of Germany v Denmark; Federal Republic of Germany v
Netherlands) [1969] ICJ Rep 3: "an indispensable requirement would be that within the period in question,
short though it might be, State practice, including that of States whose interests are specially affected,
should have been both extensive and virtually uniform." A short passage of time is not itself a bar to a new
rule forming, provided practice is sufficiently intense — but "the frequency, or even habitual character, of
the acts is not in itself enough" to prove opinio juris; acts of "ceremonial and protocol", performed almost
invariably, are motivated by courtesy, not law.
:::

:::case
Asylum Case (Colombia v Peru) [1950] ICJ Rep 266, 277: Colombia could not establish a regional custom of
diplomatic asylum in Latin America, because the practice disclosed "so much uncertainty and contradiction, so
much fluctuation and discrepancy... that it is not possible to discern in all this any constant and uniform
usage, accepted as law."
:::

Practice can take a wide range of forms — physical and verbal acts, and in some circumstances inaction —
including public statements, official publications, government legal opinions, diplomatic correspondence,
decisions of national courts, treaty provisions, and conduct in connection with resolutions of international
organisations; there is no predetermined hierarchy among them. A failure to react over time to a practice can
itself be evidence of acceptance as law, provided states were in a position to react and the circumstances
called for a reaction.

### Persistent objector and particular custom

Once a rule of customary international law is established, it generally binds every state — but there are two
exceptions. First, custom can be particular rather than universal: a regional rule binding only a group of
states, or even a custom that exists between only two states.

:::case
Right of Passage over Indian Territory (Portugal v India) [1960] ICJ Rep 6: the ICJ found that, even though
there was no treaty between the states establishing this right of passage, Portugal's centuries-long practice
of accessing its enclave through Indian territory had crystallised into a customary right of passage — a rule
of customary international law that can exist as between only two states.
:::

Second, a state that has persistently and consistently objected to a rule from the very beginning of its
formation is not bound by it — the persistent objector rule.

:::case
Fisheries (United Kingdom v Norway) [1951] ICJ Rep 116: the Court found there wasn't enough state practice to
support the UK's claimed 10-mile rule for closing bays as a rule of customary international law at all — but
even if it had achieved that status, Norway had consistently rejected it and always calculated its territorial
sea using straight baselines instead, and so would not have been bound by it regardless.
:::

:::rule
The objection must be raised before the rule crystallises and maintained consistently — a state can't decide
partway through, once a rule turns out not to be in its interest, that it is a persistent objector. This
reconciles customary law with the principle that international law is based on consent: states are only bound
by what they agree to be bound with.
:::

## The relationship between treaties and customary international law

A rule of international law may be contained in a treaty and be a rule of custom simultaneously. The Nicaragua
case (at paras 175-178) and the North Sea Continental Shelf cases identify three ways a treaty and a rule of
custom can relate: (a) a treaty may codify a pre-existing rule of custom; (b) a treaty may crystallise a
developing rule of custom, because it will prompt an intense practice by states; or (c) a treaty may give rise
to a completely new rule of customary international law, on an issue that is relatively newly emerged, because
the treaty will generate the practice.

The clearest example is the right to self-defence: it is a rule of customary international law, but it has
also been added to the UN Charter at Article 51, which recognises a right of individual or collective
self-defence. Because the two exist in parallel, states that are not party to the UN Charter would also have
the right to self-defence, because it is also a rule of customary international law — and the rule that you
find in a treaty will have certain specification, while the rule that evolves through custom may have a
different specification.

## General principles of law

Article 38(1) ICJ Statute also directs the Court to "the general principles of law recognized by civilized
nations". International courts and tribunals often rely on these principles when they cannot find authority
in other sources of international law, e.g. when a dispute cannot be effectively resolved on the basis of a
treaty or customary international law. Examples of these general principles of law are equity, good faith,
res judicata, and the impartiality of judges.

:::definition
The criteria for a general principle are, broadly: recognition by different states with a decent geographical
representation — different civil law, common law or other legal systems — and that it be possible to
transpose it usefully to international law; not everything relevant domestically is relevant to international
law.
:::

"Equity" has also been applied in various ways and contexts at the international level. It refers to what is
fair and reasonable in the administration of justice; the International Court of Justice often refers to
equity and equitable principles when delimiting the maritime boundaries between coastal states with opposite
or adjacent coasts.

## Judicial decisions and scholarly writings

As a matter of doctrine, judicial decisions are not, in and of themselves, binding international law.

:::statute
Article 59 ICJ Statute: "The decision of the Court has no binding force except between the parties and in
respect of that particular case."
:::

While in common law systems case law is a primary source of law because of the doctrine of precedent, in
international law that's not the case: even if Australia and France are before the Court and the ICJ's
decision is binding on the two states, if Australia brings another case against a different party with similar
facts and similar relevant law, the previous decision will not be binding in relation to the new case — this
respects the principle of consent, given the Court's jurisdiction is not compulsory.

However, judicial decisions carry substantial interpretative weight; international judges and legal
practitioners constantly refer to (and base their reasoning on) previous decisions, and the ICJ rarely departs
from its jurisprudence. International judges try to maintain consistency for reasons of legitimacy —
inconsistent jurisprudence would undermine the legitimacy of the international adjudicatory infrastructure —
predictability — without consistent jurisprudence, states would be reluctant to bring cases to international
courts because of the uncertainty about the outcome — and the peaceful settlement of disputes: states also
invoke ICJ decisions in negotiating settlements, sometimes as a bargaining chip.

Scholarly writings — "the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists of the various nations" — are
academic interpretations and commentaries of esteemed academics, useful to better understand the law or
clarify it where there is ambiguity. The International Law Commission (ILC), a UN body comprised of elected
academics with a geographically representative membership, has an important role in the codification and
progressive development of international law; its draft articles (e.g. on state responsibility, or on sea
level rise) are often referred to in ICJ judgments, and can eventually become treaties once states negotiate
and adopt them.

## Other sources and normative categories: what is missing from Article 38(1)

:::definition
Four further categories are commonly identified beyond Article 38(1): unilateral statements/acts, jus cogens,
decisions of international organisations, and soft law (non-legally-binding commitments).
:::

Unilateral statements by state representatives can create obligations under international law: "A state may
evidence a clear intention to accept obligations vis-à-vis certain other states by a public declaration which
is not an offer or otherwise dependent on reciprocal undertakings from its addressees" (Crawford, Brownlie's
Principles). "Nothing in the nature of a quid pro quo, nor any subsequent acceptance of the declaration, nor
even any reply or reaction from other States, is required" for a unilateral statement to be legally binding —
the binding character of unilateral statements is based on the principle of good faith, and courts look at the
words used and the context (e.g. a press conference) to interpret them.

Jus cogens refers to peremptory norms from which no derogation is permitted; there is debate as to whether it
is a subset of customary international law. Article 53 VCLT defines it as "a norm accepted and recognized by
the international community of States as a whole as a norm from which no derogation is permitted and which can
be modified only by a subsequent norm of general international law having the same character." The content of
jus cogens is controversial, though the ILC has identified genocide, crimes against humanity, aggression,
slavery, torture, and the right to self-determination among others. No treaty can contain a provision that
violates a jus cogens norm — not even one provision in an entire treaty — and the treaty will be terminated if
it does.

Decisions and resolutions of international organisations can bind states: rules concerning the regulation of
flights over the high seas adopted by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) become binding on
contracting states unless they register their disapproval within three months. Article 25 UN Charter: "The
Members of the United Nations agree to accept and carry out the decisions of the Security Council in
accordance with the present Charter" — for example UNSC Resolution 1373 (2001) on counter-terrorism, adopted
weeks after 9/11, required all member states to implement counter-terrorism measures in domestic law.

Soft law (non-legally binding commitments): political compromises versus legally-binding undertakings — the
key is to identify the intention of the parties. Declarations adopted by the UN General Assembly are not
legally binding, but can shape international practice and can lead to the formation of custom, or even result
in the adoption of new treaties (e.g. the 1972 Stockholm Declaration and the 1992 Rio Declaration are
non-binding soft law documents that have progressively resulted in binding legal commitments; the Universal
Declaration of Human Rights led to the drafting and adoption of the ICCPR and the International Covenant on
Economic, Social and Cultural Rights). Soft law instruments do not constitute independent sources of
international law, but may assist in interpreting one of the recognised legal sources.
