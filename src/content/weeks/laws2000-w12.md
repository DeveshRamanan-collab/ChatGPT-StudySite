## Overview

The lecture covers five things: the history and foundations of international human rights law (IHRL); the UN
international human rights framework, including the International Bill of Rights; categories of rights; the scope of,
and restrictions on, international human rights; and international monitoring mechanisms.

## History and foundations

Early instruments: Magna Carta (1215); the English Bill of Rights (1689); the French Declaration of the Rights of Man
and of the Citizen (1789); the US Constitution (1789) and the US Bill of Rights (1791).

Enlightenment period. **John Locke**, in the *Two Treatises of Government* (1689), defended the claim that men are by
nature free and equal against claims that God had made all people naturally subject to a monarch. He argued that people
have rights, such as the right to life, liberty and property, that have a foundation independent of the laws of any
particular society. **Immanuel Kant**, *Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785): all persons have an inherent
value, or dignity. This commands a distinct kind of moral respect and limits our treatment of others — human beings must
be treated "as an end" and "never merely as a means".

19th–20th century: the beginning of international human rights, but issue-specific. The abolition of slavery (Britain
in 1807, then other European countries, and the 1926 Slavery Convention); women's rights (limited) — trafficking in
women for prostitution. Then the Second World War and the Nazi regime.

## The UN Charter and human rights

:::statute
**Art 1(3).** The Purposes of the United Nations are: … promoting and encouraging respect for human rights and for
fundamental freedoms for all without distinction as to race, sex, language, or religion.

**Art 55(c).** With a view to the creation of conditions of stability and well-being which are necessary for peaceful
and friendly relations among nations based on respect for the principle of equal rights and self-determination of
peoples, the United Nations shall promote: … universal respect for, and observance of, human rights and fundamental
freedoms for all without distinction as to race, sex, language, or religion.

**Art 56.** All Members pledge themselves to take joint and separate action in co-operation with the Organization for
the achievement of the purposes set forth in Article 55.
:::

## The International Bill of Rights

The International Bill of Rights is made up of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), the International
Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights
(ICESCR).

**UDHR, 10 December 1948.** Art 2: "Everyone is entitled to all the rights and freedoms set forth in this Declaration,
without distinction of any kind, such as race, colour, sex, language, religion, political or other opinion, national
or social origin, property, birth or other status." Furthermore, no distinction is to be made on the basis of the
political, jurisdictional or international status of the country or territory to which a person belongs, whether it be
independent, trust, non-self-governing or under any other limitation of sovereignty. Women delegates from various
countries played a key role in getting women's rights included in the Declaration: Hansa Mehta of India is widely
credited with changing "All men are born free and equal" to "All human beings are born free and equal" in art 1.

UDHR rights listed on the slides: art 3 (life, liberty and security of person); art 5 (prohibition on torture); art 6
(recognition as a person before the law); art 7 (equality before the law); art 9 (prohibition on arbitrary arrest and
detention); art 12 (privacy, family and home); art 13 (freedom of movement); art 20 (freedom of peaceful assembly and
association); art 23 (right to work); art 26 (right to education).

- **ICCPR:** art 6 right to life; art 7 prohibition on torture; art 8 prohibition on slavery; art 9 right to liberty
  and security; art 10 rights in detention; art 11 no imprisonment for breach of contract; art 12 freedom of movement;
  art 13 rights of aliens.
- **ICESCR:** art 6 right to work; art 7 just and favourable conditions of work; art 8 right to form trade unions;
  art 9 right to social security; art 10 protection of the family; art 11 right to "adequate food, clothing and
  housing"; art 12 right to the "highest attainable standard of physical and mental health".

## Core international human rights instruments

- International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (ICERD) — 21 Dec 1965
- International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights — 16 Dec 1966
- International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights — 16 Dec 1966
- Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) — 18 Dec 1979
- Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment (CAT) — 10 Dec 1984
- Convention on the Rights of the Child — 20 Nov 1989
- International Convention on the Protection of the Rights of All Migrant Workers and Members of Their Families —
  18 Dec 1990
- International Convention for the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearance — 20 Dec 2006
- Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities — 13 Dec 2006

## Sources of human rights obligations

- **Treaty law:** the core instruments above; the European Convention on Human Rights; the American Convention on
  Human Rights.
- **Customary international law:** e.g. torture, genocide, racial discrimination. Some are considered jus cogens.

## Generations of rights

- **First generation — civil and political rights:** e.g. the right to life, freedom from torture, freedom of
  expression, the right to a fair trial, political participation rights.
- **Second generation — economic, social and cultural rights:** e.g. the right to education, the right to health, the
  right to an adequate standard of living, the right to work, cultural rights.
- **Third generation — collective rights:** e.g. self-determination (potentially, the right to development and the
  right to a healthy environment).

## Key principles: universality

Universality = rights apply to all humans everywhere. The debate is cultural relativism v universalism.

- **Cultural relativists:** different societies have different values and conceptions of rights; Western individualism
  doesn't reflect all worldviews, and universal rights represent cultural imperialism (in other words, IHRL is
  ethnocentric); rights must be understood in context.
- **Universalists:** basic rights like life and freedom from torture cannot depend on culture; cultural relativism
  often masks oppression, particularly of women and minorities; culture isn't static — it changes and evolves.

## Key principles: indivisibility and interdependence

All rights are interconnected and equally important. All human rights have equal status and cannot be positioned in a
hierarchical order. Denial of one right invariably impedes the enjoyment of other rights — e.g. the right of everyone to
an adequate standard of living cannot be compromised at the expense of other rights, such as the right to health or the
right to education. Being able to benefit from one right may depend upon the fulfilment of others.

## Rights holders and duty bearers

Right bearers: the individual, and groups (collective rights). Duty bearers: states (primarily).

- **Vertical obligations:** the state's duties toward individuals under its jurisdiction.
- **Horizontal obligations:** the state's duty to protect individuals from violations by other private actors — e.g.
  torture, non-discrimination in housing.

:::rule
Human Rights Committee, General Comment No 31, para 8: "The Covenant cannot be viewed as a substitute for domestic
criminal or civil law. However the positive obligations on States Parties to ensure Covenant rights will only be fully
discharged if individuals are protected by the State, not just against violations of Covenant rights by its agents, but
also against acts committed by private persons or entities … in so far as they are amenable to application between
private persons or entities".
:::

International organisations are also duty bearers (e.g. peacekeeping).

:::case
*Interpretation of the Agreement of 25 March 1951 between the WHO and Egypt* (Advisory Opinion) ICJ, 1980, para 37 —
"International organizations are subjects of international law and, as such, are bound by any obligations incumbent upon
them under general rules of international law, under their constitutions or under international agreements to which
they are parties…" (The slide prints "WTO"; the 1951 agreement was with the World Health Organization.)
:::

## Scope of IHRL: territory, jurisdiction and extraterritoriality

:::rule
States must respect the rights of people within their territory and those subject to their jurisdiction (ICCPR art
2(1)), irrespective of nationality or legal status.
:::

:::case
*Legal Consequences of the Construction of a Wall in the Occupied Palestinian Territory* (Advisory Opinion) ICJ, 2004,
para 109 — "while the jurisdiction of States is primarily territorial, it may sometimes be exercised outside the
national territory. Considering the object and purpose of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, it
would seem natural that, even when such is the case, States parties to the Covenant should be bound to comply with its
provisions … the drafters of the Covenant did not intend to allow States to escape from their obligations when they
exercise jurisdiction outside their national territory".
:::

:::case
*Armed Activities on the Territory of the Congo (Democratic Republic of the Congo v Uganda)* (Judgment) ICJ Reports 2005
— having concluded that Uganda was an occupying Power in Ituri at the relevant time, the Court found that Uganda's
responsibility was engaged both for any acts of its military that violated its international obligations and for any
lack of vigilance in preventing violations of human rights and international humanitarian law by other actors present in
the occupied territory, including rebel groups acting on their own account (para 179). International human rights
instruments are applicable "in respect of acts done by a State in the exercise of its jurisdiction outside its own
territory", particularly in occupied territories (para 216).
:::

Even sometimes when the state has no effective control: see also Human Rights Committee, *Sergio Euben Lopez Burgos v
Uruguay*, Communication No R.12/52 (6 June 1979), UN Doc Supp No 40 (A/36/40) at 176 (1981), para 12.3.

## Derogations in time of emergency

:::statute
**ICCPR art 4.** 1. In time of public emergency which threatens the life of the nation and the existence of which is
officially proclaimed, the States Parties to the present Covenant may take measures derogating from their obligations
under the present Covenant to the extent strictly required by the exigencies of the situation, provided that such
measures are not inconsistent with their other obligations under international law and do not involve discrimination
solely on the ground of race, colour, sex, language, religion or social origin. 2. No derogation from articles 6, 7, 8
(paragraphs 1 and 2), 11, 15, 16 and 18 may be made under this provision.
:::

:::statute
**American Convention on Human Rights art 27.** 1. In time of war, public danger, or other emergency that threatens the
independence or security of a State Party, it may take measures derogating from its obligations under the present
Convention to the extent and for the period of time strictly required by the exigencies of the situation, provided that
such measures are not inconsistent with its other obligations under international law and do not involve
discrimination on the ground of race, color, sex, language, religion, or social origin. 2. The foregoing provision does
not authorize any suspension of the following articles: Article 3 (Right to Juridical Personality), Article 4 (Right
to Life), Article 5 (Right to Humane Treatment), Article 6 (Freedom from Slavery), Article 9 (Freedom from Ex Post Facto
Laws), Article 12 (Freedom of Conscience and Religion), etc.
:::

Absolute and limited rights. Four substantive requirements for a derogation:

1. Necessity and proportionality (nature, duration, impact).
2. Consistent with the state's other obligations under international law, especially jus cogens.
3. Cannot affect absolute or non-derogable rights.
4. Notification requirement.

:::case
*Aksoy v Turkey* (Application No 21987/93) (ECtHR), para 78 — "Although the Court is of the view … that the
investigation of terrorist offences undoubtedly presents the authorities with special problems, it cannot accept that it
is necessary to hold a suspect for fourteen days without judicial intervention. This period is exceptionally long and
left the applicant vulnerable not only to arbitrary interference with his right to liberty but also to torture …
Moreover, the Government have not adduced any detailed reasons before the Court as to why the fight against terrorism
in South-East Turkey rendered judicial intervention impracticable."
:::

## Restrictions outside an emergency

Conditions:

1. Provided by law and legal (e.g. the Constitution).
2. Serve a legitimate aim described in the limitation clause, e.g. protection of rights and freedoms.
3. Necessary in a democratic society — that is, responding to a public need, proportionate, and the aim pursued is
   legitimate.

## Charter-based mechanisms

**Human Rights Council.** Replaced the UN Commission on Human Rights in 2006 (UNGA resolution 60/251). 47 (country)
members, elected by the UNGA for 3-year terms (maximum 2 consecutive terms), with regional distribution of members. Its
working methods fall into four categories: regular sessions, special sessions, the Universal Periodic Review and the
special procedures.

**Universal Periodic Review (UPR).** Each member state is reviewed every 4.5 years. States (and NGOs) submit reports; a
working group (47 members) reviews the reports and issues an "Outcome Report" including recommendations to the member
state under review; states accept or note the recommendations. Australia was reviewed by the UPR working group in
January 2021: it received 344 recommendations and supported 177 when its outcome was adopted at the Council's 47th
session in July 2021 (in its 2015 cycle it received 290 and supported 150). The UPR's 2024 facts and figures report 100%
participation and a 76% acceptance rate.

**Special procedures.** A system of independent experts — special rapporteurs, independent experts and working groups:
46 thematic mandates (e.g. the right to privacy, the right to food, human rights and climate change) and 14 country
mandates that investigate violations in specific countries (e.g. CAR, Belarus, Russia, Cambodia). Methods of work:
country visits (with state consent); receiving individual complaints; issuing reports on international human rights
within their mandate to foster a better understanding of these norms and their implementation.

## Treaty-based mechanisms

Each core treaty has a treaty body: ICERD — Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination; ICESCR — Committee
on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights; ICCPR — Human Rights Committee; CEDAW — Committee on the Elimination of
Discrimination against Women; CAT — Committee against Torture; Convention on the Rights of the Child — Committee on the
Rights of the Child; Migrant Workers Convention — Committee on Migrant Workers; Convention on the Rights of Persons with
Disabilities — its Committee; Enforced Disappearance Convention — Committee on Enforced Disappearances; and the
Optional Protocol to the Convention against Torture (2002) — Subcommittee on Prevention of Torture.

- **State reporting:** states must submit initial reports after ratifying a treaty, followed by periodic reports;
  dialogue with the state is followed by concluding observations and recommendations.
- **Individual complaints procedures:** written, confidential proceedings modelled on judicial practice, with full
  opportunity for both parties to respond to pleadings; the committee adopts "views", and the published jurisprudence
  is used for authoritative treaty interpretation.
- **Inter-state complaints:** extremely rare — only three have ever been brought, all under ICERD. Only ICERD does not
  require reciprocal declarations.
- **General comments:** committees issue authoritative interpretations of treaty provisions called "general comments"
  or "general recommendations".

## Legal nature of treaty body outputs

Treaty body decisions are not binding but carry significant weight.

:::case
*Ahmadou Sadio Diallo (Republic of Guinea v Democratic Republic of the Congo)* (Merits) ICJ, 2010, para 66 — "Although
the Court is in no way obliged, in the exercise of its judicial functions, to model its own interpretation of the
Covenant on that of the Committee, it believes that it should ascribe great weight to the interpretation adopted by this
independent body that was established specifically to supervise the application of that treaty. The point here is to
achieve the necessary clarity and the essential consistency of international law, as well as legal security…" See also
*Application of the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (Qatar v United
Arab Emirates)* (Preliminary Objections) ICJ, 2021, para 101.
:::
