## Week 3 – Legal Positivism

- **H.L.A. Hart's theory of law** — law as the union of primary and secondary rules.
- **Hans Kelsen's theory of law** — law as a coercive, normative order that regulates its own creation, application
  and execution.
- **What is legal positivism?** — legal positivism as a general theory of law; as a methodology; as a political theory
  of law.

Hart and Kelsen are the most influential legal positivists of the 20th century. They developed two different theories
of law, but there are also commonalities between them, and the problems we discuss in this unit still use the language
and concepts their theories introduced. Legal positivism is best understood as a family of theories. For this week's
tutorial, focus on chapter 6 of *The Concept of Law* (the rule of recognition); chapter 5 is the background you need.

## A fresh start

"In the last three chapters we have seen that, at various crucial points, the simple model of law as the sovereign's
coercive orders failed to reproduce some of the salient features of a legal system." (H.L.A. Hart, *The Concept of
Law* (OUP, 3rd ed, 2012) 79)

"The last three chapters are therefore the record of a failure and there is plainly need for a fresh start. Yet the
failure is an instructive one, worth the detailed consideration we have given it, because at each point where the
theory failed to fit the facts it was possible to see at least in outline why it was bound to fail and what is required
for a better account." (Ibid, 80)

Chapters 2, 3 and 4 criticise a model that resembles Austin's theory; in chapters 5 and 6 Hart develops his own theory,
building on those criticisms. Keep the idea of a *better account* in mind: when we compare theories we need the criteria
on which one theory can be said to be better than another.

## The idea of obligation

"…the theory of law as coercive orders, notwithstanding its errors, started from the perfectly correct appreciation of
the fact that where there is law, there human conduct is made in some sense non-optional or obligatory." (CL 82)

Revisit the "gunman situation". According to Hart, "[t]here is a difference, yet to be explained, between the assertion
that someone was obliged to do something and the assertion that he had an obligation to do it." (CL 82) From a factual
standpoint the gunman and the tax officer look the same — someone with power over me demands money, with negative
consequences if I do not comply — but we have the intuition that they differ. Even a person who does not pay their
taxes is still under an obligation to pay them.

## The idea of a rule

Habits and rules share one element: we can observe a pattern/convergence of behaviour… However, according to Hart,
there are three salient differences:

1. Deviations from a rule are met with criticisms.
2. The rule is regarded as a good reason for making such criticism.
3. (Social) rules have an internal aspect ("a critical reflective attitude to certain patterns of behaviour as a common
   standard" – see CL 57).

"…habits are not 'normative': they cannot confer rights or authority on anyone." (CL 60)

The lecture's examples: someone who jumps the queue is called out, and the people in the queue cite the rule as the
reason ("that's not how you queue"); if you move chess pieces as you wish, you are no longer playing chess. From the
external point of view — an alien, or a first-time spectator who does not know the game — you can only register
patterns of behaviour. The internal point of view is the point of view of the participants who buy into the rules; you
can understand it even when you are only watching. For Hart the internal aspect is a critical reflective attitude, not
feelings of compulsion: he criticises legal realism (especially the Scandinavian realists) for reducing it to emotions,
and for explaining obligation as a prediction of negative consequences. Rules give reasons — to criticise others and to
act ourselves — an idea Joseph Raz later develops as "reasons for action".

## A set of primary rules of obligation

According to Hart, a simple social structure of primary rules has three main defects:

- It is uncertain.
- It has a static character.
- It is inefficient.

"The remedy for each of these three main defects in this simplest form of social structure consists in supplementing
the primary rules of obligation with secondary rules which are rules of a different kind." (CL 94)

This is not a sociological investigation but more like a thought experiment. The lecture's illustrations: the elders of
a community who know the customs fix the content of an uncertain rule; a community that discovers it can fish calls a
general assembly (or empowers a smaller group) to create a new rule; parties to a disagreement call in a third party to
adjudicate. The content of the secondary rules varies (a jury in one jurisdiction, a judge in another), but both
systems have rules of adjudication.

## Secondary rules as remedies

- Uncertainty → rule of recognition
- Static quality → rules of change
- Inefficiency → rules of adjudication

"If we stand back and consider the structure which has resulted from the combination of primary rules of obligation
with the secondary rules of recognition, change and adjudication, it is plain that we have here not only the heart of a
legal system, but a most powerful tool for the analysis of much that has puzzled both the jurist and the political
theorist." (CL 98)

:::definition
For Hart, law (in its central case, a legal system) is **the union of primary and secondary rules**: primary rules of
obligation combined with secondary rules of recognition, change and adjudication.
:::

The union is at the centre of a legal system, "but it is not the whole": moving away from the centre we meet elements of
a different character. For Hart the paradigm case of law is the law of the modern state. Austin focused on the
individual law (a law is a command of the sovereign); Hart and Kelsen look at the system as a whole. Bear the picture of
centre and periphery in mind for next week (Finnis).

## The foundations of a legal system

"Wherever such a rule of recognition is accepted, both private persons and officials are provided with authoritative
criteria for identifying primary rules of obligation. The criteria so provided may, as we have seen, take any one or
more of a variety of forms: these include reference to an authoritative text; to legislative enactment; to customary
practice; to general declarations of specified persons, or to past judicial decisions in particular cases." (CL 100)

- The existence of a rule of recognition is shown in the way in which primary rules are identified (the RoR is manifest
  in a general practice).

For Austin sovereignty comes first and then a legal system; for Hart (and in a sense Kelsen) it is the other way round —
first the rule of recognition, then an authority identifiable as sovereign. The rule is often implicit; it is kept alive
by private persons and officials, especially judges. Chapter 6 also speaks of rules of recognition in the plural, with
one ultimate rule of recognition.

## Hart on legal validity

"To say that a given rule is valid is to recognize it as passing all the tests provided by the rule of recognition and
so as a rule of the system. We can indeed simply say that the statement that a particular rule is valid means that it
satisfies all the criteria provided by the rule of recognition." (CL 103)

- Validity and membership (vis-à-vis a system of rules).
- Ultimacy and supremacy of the rule of recognition (CL 105–107).
- Is the rule of recognition (legally) valid?

## The "validity" of the rule of recognition

"No such question can arise as to the validity of the very rule of recognition which provides the criteria; it can
neither be valid nor invalid but is simply accepted as appropriate for use in this way. To express this simple fact by
saying darkly that its validity is 'assumed but cannot be demonstrated', is like saying that we assume, but can never
demonstrate, that the standard metre bar in Paris which is the ultimate test of the correctness of all measurement in
metres, is itself correct." (CL 109)

## Hart on the existence of a legal system

"There are therefore two minimum conditions necessary and sufficient for the existence of a legal system. On the one
hand, those rules of behaviour which are valid according to the system's ultimate criteria of validity must be
generally obeyed, and, on the other hand, its rules of recognition specifying the criteria of legal validity and its
rules of change and adjudication must be effectively accepted as common public standards of official behaviour by its
officials." (CL 109)

The first condition captures a factual element; the second is what Hart adds to Austin. Hart is concerned with the
existence of a legal system, not its legitimacy.

:::case
The *Miller* case (UK Supreme Court, 2017), recalled from week 1: the judges used Hart's rule of recognition to make
sense of the UK legal system and its interaction with the European Union — these concepts are part of legal reasoning,
not only legal theory.
:::

## Hart on international law

"Kelsen and many modern theorists insist that, like municipal law, international law possesses and indeed must possess
a 'basic norm', or what we have termed a rule of recognition […] The opposed view is that this analogy of structure is
false: international law simply consists of a set of separate primary rules of obligation which are not united in this
manner." (CL 233)

For Hart, there are analogies between international law and municipal law, in terms of function and content
(principles, concepts, and methods). However, their form/structure is different. International law is law, but not a
legal system: a non-central case of law, next to municipal law as the central case. International law in 1961 was
different from international law today, and some criticisms of the chapter are not completely fair; the lecture also
notes the demarcation problem — complex organisations (FIFA, a mafia) have something like secondary rules too.

## Hans Kelsen (1881–1973)

Born in Prague in 1881; studied law at the University of Vienna; professor at the University of Vienna (1919); worked on
the Austrian Constitution (1920); professor at the University of Cologne (1930); forced to move to Switzerland in 1933
and to the US in 1940; professor at the University of California, Berkeley (1945). He published more on public,
constitutional and international law than on legal theory — *Das Problem der Souveränität und die Theorie des
Völkerrechts* (1920), *Peace Through Law* (1944), *General Theory of Law and State* (1945), *The Law of the United
Nations* (1950), *General Theory of Norms* (1979; English 1990) — and many works remain untranslated from German.

## Kelsen's Reine Rechtslehre

Stanley Paulson distinguishes four phases in the development of Kelsen's pure theory of law: a constructivist phase,
from 1911 to about 1920; a strong neo-Kantian phase, from about 1920 to the mid-1930s; a weak neo-Kantian phase, from the
late 1930s to 1960; and a skeptical, or empiricist, phase, from 1960 onwards. The Pure Theory of Law was first published
in 1934 as *Reine Rechtslehre*; a revised edition was published in 1960 and translated into English in 1967 as *The Pure
Theory of Law*. Its chapters include the static aspect and the dynamic aspect of law: the static aspect (the content of
norms) matters more in morality; to understand law we need its dynamic aspect. Kelsen speaks of legal *orders* and
*norms* where Hart speaks of legal systems and rules; for now treat the terms as synonyms.

## Kelsen on the nature of law

- Law is – according to the Pure Theory of Law – by its very nature a definite type of norm. (p 1)
- The law is a coercive order. (p 1) Coercion is the distinguishing feature of law vis-à-vis other normative orders.
- It is a peculiarity of the law that it regulates its own creation, application and execution. (p 5)
- What is the difference between the command of a gangster to give him a certain sum of money and the command of a tax
  officer which has the same subjective meaning? "The answer is, that the command of the tax officer is authorized by an
  objectively valid legal norm, whereas the command of the gangster is not." (p 6)
- The search for the reason for the validity of the legal norms thus leads to the insight into the hierarchal structure
  of a legal order. (p 6)

The gangster is Kelsen's version of the gunman: Hart and Kelsen see the same problem and give different explanations.
Focus on the idea of authorisation. The Vienna school calls the hierarchical structure the *Stufenbau*.

## Kelsen on legal validity

- What constitutes the unity of a multitude of norms?
- Why does a certain norm belong to a certain order?
- Why is a norm valid – what is the reason for its validity?

For Kelsen: if a norm is valid, then it is binding; the validity of a norm cannot be a fact; only a competent authority
can create a valid norm; there is a chain of validity back to the historically first constitution; and the basic norm is
a transcendental-logical presupposition.

The lecture's walk-through: a council regulation is valid because a higher norm authorised the council to issue it;
that norm is valid because a still higher norm authorised it; and so on up to the constitution. Then we go back in time
through previous constitutions to the historically first constitution. To say why that is valid we must presuppose a
norm — you ought to obey the historically first constitution — the basic norm (*Grundnorm*). In Kelsen's theory the
basic norm performs the same function as the rule of recognition in Hart's. For Hart validity is membership; for Kelsen
it is binding force. (In Australia the chain leads back to a statute of the Imperial Parliament, which some authors use
to test Kelsen's theory.)

## Hart's rule of recognition vs Kelsen's Grundnorm

- For Hart the existence of a rule of recognition is a complex, empirical question of fact. For Kelsen, the basic norm
  is a "juristic hypothesis", a "postulated ultimate rule", a "fiction".
- For Kelsen, a jurist/theorist must presuppose the validity of the basic norm. For Hart, the rule of recognition is
  neither valid, nor invalid.
- Kelsen's basic norm has always the same content ("who laid down the first constitution ought to be obeyed"). For Hart,
  the rules of recognition in different jurisdictions may well have different content.

"One reason for using the expression 'rule of recognition' instead of a 'basic norm' is to avoid any commitment to
Kelsen's view of the conflict between law and morals." (CL 293) On Kelsen's view a legal norm and a moral norm cannot be
valid at the same time, so there can be no meaningful conflict between law and morality; Hart can accept such
conflicts.

## Kelsen's pure theory of law as a theory of positive law

"The pure theory of law is a theory of positive law; a general theory of law, not a presentation or interpretation of a
special legal order. From a comparison of all the phenomena which go under the name of law, it seeks to discover the
nature of law itself, to determine its structure and its typical forms, independent of the changing content which it
exhibits at different times and among different peoples. In this manner it derives the fundamental principles by means
of which any legal order can be comprehended. As a theory, its sole purpose is to know its subject. It answers the
question of what the law is, not what it ought to be. The latter question is one of politics, while the pure theory of
law is science." (Hans Kelsen, 'The Pure Theory of Law and Analytical Jurisprudence' (1941) 55(1) *Harvard Law Review*
44)

It is "the most consistent version of that school of jurisprudence which is called legal positivism because it
considers as 'law' only positive law and refuses to recognize as law any other normative social order even if, in the
usage of language, it is called 'law', as e.g. so-called 'natural law'" (p 1). As far as the relationship between law
and justice, and formalism, is concerned, "there is no essential difference between analytical jurisprudence (the
founder of which is John Austin …) and the Pure Theory of Law" (p 4).

## What is legal positivism?

According to Leslie Green and Tom Adams ('Legal Positivism', *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*): legal positivism is
the thesis that the existence and content of law depends on social facts and not on its merits.

According to John Austin (1790–1859): "The existence of law is one thing; its merit and demerit another. Whether it be
or be not is one enquiry; whether it be or be not conformable to an assumed standard, is a different enquiry." (J
Austin, *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (Wilfrid E Rumble ed, Cambridge University Press, 1995) 157)

## Hart on legal positivism

"Here we shall take Legal Positivism to mean the simple contention that it is in no sense a necessary truth that laws
reproduce or satisfy certain demands of morality, though in fact they have often done so." (CL 185–186)

"My account is descriptive in that it is morally neutral and has no justificatory aims: it does not seek to justify or
commend on moral or other grounds the forms and structures which appear in my general account of law, though a clear
understanding of these is, I think, an important preliminary to any useful moral criticism of law." (CL 240)

The first is a claim about law (the relationship between law and morality); the second is a claim about legal theory.
Distinguish the two levels.

## Jeremy Bentham and John Austin

"…Bentham and Austin were not dry analysts fiddling with verbal distinctions while cities burn, but were the vanguard of
a movement which laboured with passionate intensity and much success to bring about a better society and better laws."
(Hart, 'Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals', 596)

According to Hart the Utilitarians insisted on the separation of law and morals, but…

- They never denied that many legal rules mirror moral rules/principles (as a matter of historical fact).
- Bentham never denied that moral principles might become part of a legal system thanks to explicit legal provisions
  (see, for example, some constitutional provisions).

## Two simple things

"What both Bentham and Austin were anxious to assert were the following two simple things: first, in the absence of an
expressed constitutional or legal provision, it could not follow from the mere fact that a rule violated standards of
morality that it was not a rule of law; and, conversely, it could not follow from the mere fact that a rule was morally
desirable that it was a rule of law." (Hart, 'Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals', 599)

:::rule
The separation between law as it is and law as it ought to be is the central claim in the positivist tradition.
:::

## The utilitarian tradition in jurisprudence

According to Hart, the utilitarian tradition in jurisprudence has defended three doctrines:

1. the separation between law and morals;
2. the important truth that a purely analytical study of legal concepts, a study of the meaning of the distinctive
   vocabulary of the law, was as vital to our understanding of the nature of law as historical or sociological studies
   (though of course it could not supplant them);
3. the famous imperative theory of law.

(1), (2), and (3) are separate doctrines/claims – they are not a package.

## Is vs. ought

- Law as it is vs. law as it ought to be — a key distinction defended by Bentham, Austin, and Hart.
- Yet, for some legal philosophers this sharp distinction is intellectually misleading and/or practically dangerous.

Hart identifies three lines of criticism: law and morality – in relation to laws and/or a legal system; legal
positivism and formalism in adjudication; and the practical problem of the existence of morally evil laws (see, eg, the
Nazi regime).

## Five meanings of 'positivism'

Hart identifies five meanings of the term 'positivism' in the context of jurisprudence:

1. the contention that laws are commands of human beings;
2. the contention that there is no necessary connection between law and morals, or law as it is and law as it ought to
   be;
3. the contention that the analysis (or study of the meaning) of legal concepts is (a) worth pursuing and (b) to be
   distinguished from historical inquiries, sociological inquiries, and from the criticism/appraisal of law;
4. the contention that a legal system is a "closed logical system" in which correct legal decisions can be deduced from
   predetermined legal rules by logical means alone;
5. the contention that moral judgments cannot be established or defended, as statements of facts can, by rational
   argument, evidence, or proof ("noncognitivism" in ethics).

## Different positivist theories of law

- Bentham and Austin held the views expressed in (1), (2), and (3), but not in (4) and (5).
- Kelsen held the views expressed in (2), (3), and (5), but not those in (1) and (4).

(Hart, 'Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals', n 25 at 601–602; CL 302.) What about H.L.A. Hart? Work out
which of the five claims Hart would defend and which he would reject: if you can do that, you understand both Hart's
theory and what legal positivism means.

## HLA Hart's theory of law — what to revise

- The idea of a social rule — habits vs rules.
- The idea of legal obligation — being obliged vs having an obligation.
- Law as the union of primary and secondary rules — legal systems vs sets of rules.
- The rule(s) of recognition — Hart's rule of recognition vs Kelsen's basic norm.
- The idea of legal validity.
- The existence of a legal system.

## Legal positivism

- **Legal positivism as a general theory of law**
  - Law is grounded in social facts (social facts thesis).
  - The existence and content of law depend on its sources and not on its merits (sources thesis).
  - Law and morality are separable, in the sense that we can conceptualise law without any reference to morality
    (separability thesis).
  - These are descriptive and conceptual claims about the nature of law.
- **Legal positivism as a methodology**
  - Legal theory is a morally neutral, descriptive enterprise.
  - This is a meta-theoretical claim about the nature of legal theory.
- **Legal positivism as a political theory of law**
  - Moral criteria of legal validity are not morally desirable (even though they are conceptually possible), so
    morality and law should be kept separate.
  - This is a prescriptive/normative version of the separability thesis.

as a methodology — so show early that you are testing a claim about legal theory, not a claim about law.
