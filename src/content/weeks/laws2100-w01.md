## Jurisprudence

The word 'jurisprudence' has more than one meaning. *Iurisprudentia* = *iuris* (of right, of law) + *prudentia*
(wisdom, foresight, knowledge).

- Jurisprudence as a body of judicial decisions: consider, for example, the expression 'the constitutional
  jurisprudence of the High Court'.
- Jurisprudence in relation to religious law (eg Hindu law, Jewish halakha, Christian canon law, Islamic sharia).
- Medical jurisprudence (legal medicine).

The lecture does not engage with these uses; they show the complexity of the subject, mirrored in the different
uses of the term. For our purposes, jurisprudence should be understood as legal theory.

## Jurisprudence and legal theory

Jurisprudence as a general theory of law. Brian Bix uses the terms 'jurisprudence', 'legal theory' and 'legal
philosophy' interchangeably throughout his textbook (Preface to the ninth edition, p vii). Not everyone agrees: the
further readings include Roger Cotterrell, 'Why Jurisprudence Is Not Legal Philosophy'.

- On what basis can we speak of a general theory of law?
- General jurisprudence as a philosophical inquiry into the nature of law?

:::definition
**General jurisprudence** deals with philosophical puzzles that are thrown up by all legal systems (eg what is law?
what is a legal system? what is legal reasoning? is there a general obligation to obey the law?).
**Particular jurisprudence** deals with puzzles that emerge in some legal systems or traditions but not necessarily
all (eg what is common about the common law? in what sense is Parliament sovereign?).
:::

This is a very rough distinction, and the two are not completely disconnected; a number of theories use it and make
different methodological claims about the two areas.

## Jurisprudence and conceptual analysis

- Analytical jurisprudence and analytical philosophy. Hart's *The Concept of Law* (1961) is widely seen as the
  beginning of modern analytical jurisprudence, though the tradition is older (Bentham, Austin). Hart combined it
  with the Oxford philosophy of his time, which focused on the analysis of (ordinary) language.
- Conceptual analysis has been a key element/method of analytical philosophy.
- Conceptual theories generally define terms by necessary and sufficient conditions.
- "…conceptual theories and claims set the boundaries of categories. The drawing of such boundaries can be helpful
  in establishing a common ground for investigation and discussion, but the placement of the boundaries is often
  contested." (Bix, p 18)
- According to Bix, purely conceptual theories can be contrasted with two different kinds of theories:
  non-conceptual theories in the natural sciences, and non-conceptual theories in the social sciences. Those
  theories use concepts, but they are not conceptual theories.

When people disagree about a concept, ask what kind of disagreement it is: semantic (about the meaning of a term)
or conceptual (not sharing the same concept). The lecture's example: 'football' in the UK and in Australia; once I
say "I meant soccer", we share the concept, and context (pragmatics) does the rest.

## According to William Twining (1934–2025)…

- "…there is a lot of work to be done on usable concepts in cross-cultural and transnational legal discourse and
  that this is a task for analytical jurisprudence" (p 34)
- "It is part of my general thesis that conceptual analysis has an important part to play in reviving general
  jurisprudence, but that what is needed is a much broader conception of what is involved than those of Hart or Raz
  or even Stone" (p 39)
- "…conceptual elucidation is as important for social scientific investigation as it is for legal exposition and
  the methods of conceptual analysis developed by analytical philosophy are applicable to important concepts in
  empirical legal studies and the social sciences generally" (p 57)

Twining pays much more attention to the social side of things, and to the empirical and social sciences, than Hart.
Stone is Julius Stone, long professor of jurisprudence at Sydney, who defended a sociological jurisprudence.

## Legal theory in the real world

:::case
*Milirrpum v Nabalco Pty Ltd* [1971] 17 FLR 141 (the Gove land rights case) — an Australian case in which Aboriginal
claims to land were questioned on the basis that the customary rules of Aboriginal peoples could not be described
by the court as legal rules. The lecture: the court was buying into assumptions about the nature of law that came
from Austin's theory, and you cannot fully understand the reasoning in Mabo without it.
:::

:::case
*Madzimbamuto v Lardner-Burke* [1969] 1 AC 645 — a decision of the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council on United
Kingdom constitutional law and the constitutional law of Rhodesia. The case considers the legality of the Unilateral
Declaration of Independence made by Rhodesia in 1965, in light of Hans Kelsen's theory of law.
:::

:::case
The *Miller* case — the UK's first case on Brexit (decided by the UK Supreme Court in 2017), considering whether the
British Government could withdraw from the European Union without an Act of Parliament giving permission to do so.
The judges used Hart's concept of the rule of recognition: "we would not accept that the so-called fundamental rule
of recognition (i.e. the fundamental rule by reference to which all other rules are validated) underlying UK laws has
been varied by the 1972 Act or would be varied by its repeal" [60]; and one argument was "that the UK's withdrawal
from the EU will alter the UK's rule of recognition: that is to say, the rule which identifies the sources of law in
our legal system and imposes a duty to give effect to laws emanating from those sources" [173].
:::

Concepts developed within theories of law filter through into the real world, and make a difference when officials
decide on the basis of them. Look for these theories in the rest of your legal studies, and use your legal knowledge
to put pressure on the theories.

## Six kinds of questions

1. **Doctrinal questions** — eg questions about domestic law (what the law is on a specific issue). Every
   hypothetical is a cluster of doctrinal questions.
2. **Normative questions** — descriptive vs normative claims (is/ought distinction). Normative: justificatory vs
   critical claims (and theories).
3. **Empirical questions** — explanatory/causal vs effects claims (and theories).
4. **Metatheoretical questions** — what are the success criteria for a given theory?
5. **Methodological questions** — how should we conduct research in this field?
6. **Conceptual questions** — eg what is a contract? what is law? what is justice?

Picking one theory over another is not like picking an ice-cream flavour: a metatheoretical question looks for
criteria to say a theory is superior, at least in respect of X, Y, Z. Some claims in the readings are theoretical,
others metatheoretical; it matters to see at which level a claim operates.

## Jurisprudence and the nature of law

(1) What is (the nature of) jurisprudence? (2) What is (the nature of) law? These are connected but different
problems. When you read, ask: is this an argument or claim about jurisprudence — the subject, the theory — or a
claim about law, about the nature of law?

:::examtip
Confusing the two levels is the commonest way to be off topic: an essay about whether law is connected to morality
answers a question about the nature of law, not one about the nature of jurisprudence.
:::

"In law as elsewhere, we can know and yet not understand" (H.L.A. Hart, *Definition and Theory in Jurisprudence: An
Inaugural Lecture*, 1953). "My aim in this book has been to further the understanding of law, coercion, and morality
as different but related social phenomena" (H.L.A. Hart, *The Concept of Law*, 6). Remember this and you already
have a map of the book: its chapters mirror it.

## H.L.A. Hart (1907–1992)

Professor of Jurisprudence at the University of Oxford and Fellow of University College, Oxford (1952–1973 on the
slide; the lecture said he took the chair in 1951). Inaugural lecture at Oxford: *Definition and Theory in
Jurisprudence* (1953). *The Concept of Law* (1961; 2nd ed 1994; 3rd ed 2012). The 2nd edition, published after
Hart's death, adds the unfinished Postscript, largely a response to critics, especially Ronald Dworkin; its curators
included Joseph Raz. The 3rd edition adds a new introduction by Leslie Green. Use the book as a tool, not the gospel.

## Hart's theoretical project

The purpose of the book "…is not to provide a definition of law, in the sense of a rule by reference to which the
correctness of the use of the word can be tested; it is to advance legal theory by providing an improved analysis of
the distinctive structure of a municipal legal system and a better understanding of the resemblances and differences
between law, coercion, and morality, as types of social phenomena." (CL, 17)

"Notwithstanding its concern with analysis the book may also be regarded as an essay in descriptive sociology;…"
(Preface). Whether it really is, and what that means, is debated (see Twining's chapter).

## Hart on the question 'What is law?'

- Unending theoretical debate in books vs the ability of most people to cite examples of law.
- Consider the "salient features" listed at page 3 of *The Concept of Law*.
- The question "what is law?" expresses perplexities about the general nature of law.
- Clear/standard/paradigm cases of law (for Hart, "the legal systems of modern states") vs
  questionable/challengeable/borderline cases (for Hart, "primitive law and international law").
- Speculation about the nature of law has historically centred on a few principal issues.
- Confusion about these issues may coexist with a firm knowledge of the law: I can be an expert in Italian contract
  law and still struggle to understand law at the general level.

## Three persistent questions

"We shall distinguish here three [...] recurrent issues, and show later why they come together in the form of a
request for a definition of law or an answer to the question 'What is law?', or in more obscurely framed questions
such as 'What is the nature (or the essence) of law?'" (CL, 6)

1. How does law differ from and how is it related to orders backed by threats? (law and coercion)
2. How does legal obligation differ from, and how is it related to, moral obligation? (law and morality)
3. What are rules and to what extent is law an affair of rules? (law, like games and sports, is a rule-based social
   practice — but playing law is not playing chess)

The rest of the book is organised around these three issues.

## Searching for a definition…

"…even skilled lawyers have felt that, though they know the law, there is much about law and its relations to other
things that they cannot explain and do not fully understand. Like a man who can get from one point to another in a
familiar town but cannot explain or show others how to do it, those who press for a definition need a map
exhibiting clearly the relationships dimly felt to exist between the law they know and other things. Sometimes in
such cases a definition of a word can supply such a map: at one and the same time it may make explicit the latent
principle which guides our use of a word, and may exhibit relationships between the type of phenomena to which we
apply the word and other phenomena." (CL, 14)

The map has two dimensions: particular law and law as a general idea; and law and the other things we know
(morality, religion, games, sports, etiquette).

## Triangles, elephants, and…law

- Triangle (def.) = three-sided rectilinear figure.
- Elephant (def.) = quadruped distinguished from others by its possession of a thick skin, tusks, and trunk.

:::definition
These are definitions **per genus et differentiam**: general kind + distinguishing features. This type of
definition does two things at once: it provides a code or formula translating the word into other well-understood
terms, and it locates the kind of thing the word refers to, by indicating the features it shares with a wider
family of things and those which mark it off from others of that same family.
:::

Can we define law per genus et differentiam? Some theorists (Austin, Kelsen) tried. Hart says this type of
definition does not work for law. That is a big claim, and many disagree. There are in fact two different claims:
one, that it is impossible to define law per genus et differentiam; another, that it may be possible, but there are
better definitions of law. Hart claims it is not possible, so law must be defined by another method (the end of
chapter 1; discussed in the week 2 tutorial).

## Hart on the nature of legal theory

"My aim in this book was to provide a theory of what law is which is both general and descriptive." (CL, 239 —
the Postscript)

- General in the sense that it is not tied to any particular legal system or legal culture.
- Descriptive in that it is morally neutral and has no justificatory aims.

"As a means of carrying out this descriptive enterprise my book makes repeated use of a number of concepts such as
duty-imposing rules, power-conferring rules, rules of recognition, rules of change, acceptance of rules, internal
and external points of view, internal and external statements, and legal validity" (CL, 240).

:::examtip
These two claims can be used as criteria to assess the theory on its own terms. Is it really general — not tied to
any particular legal system? Is it really descriptive — morally neutral, with no justificatory aims? Open questions
you should be able to write an essay on in a few weeks.
:::

## John Finnis on law and philosophy

"Making, acknowledging, and complying with law involves acts of rational judgment. The reasonableness and
justification of these acts cannot be assessed without premises about true human goods, the nature of persons and
their acts, and the contours of the common good and human rights. […] legal studies are really attractive and
worthwhile because law, and juristic argumentation, is an arena where themes and theses in ethics, political theory,
and related philosophical domains all come to bear on – and crystallize out in – legislating and adjudicating to make
a difference to human persons." (J. Finnis, *Philosophy of Law: Collected Essays Volume IV*, OUP, 2011)

Finnis, a natural law thinker (week 4), connects analytical jurisprudence with a rich understanding of human nature
and of what we ought to do — not at all what Raz or Hart try to do. Yet he uses concepts Hart developed, which makes
the comparison meaningful.

## Dickson on the problem of the nature of law

"[…] recent years have seen many thought-provoking challenges emerge which cast doubt on the very possibility of,
and/or call into question the current manner of engaging in, that part of the philosophy of law which attempts to
identify and understand law's nature." (J. Dickson, *Elucidating Law*, OUP, 2022, 11–12)

"Some […] theorists are wary of the idea that a human-made social construction such as law can have a nature which
legal philosophers can attempt to identify and explain. Others mount challenges in respect of what they regard as the
key method employed by legal philosophers attempting to ascertain law's nature, namely conceptual analysis…" (p 12)

## Gardner on the value of studying jurisprudence

- As a philosophical subject, jurisprudence can assist in our development as human beings.
- Two practical implications: jurisprudence contributes to excellence in argument; and it can help us recognise
  different perspectives and different aspects of a given legal issue.
- "A philosophical education encourages us to look […] for timeless problems underlying topical problems" (John
  Gardner, 'Why study jurisprudence?', p 8).

## Leslie Green on legal philosophy

"Legal philosophy is here to remind you that you are not a slave to your own (legal) head, that there is an escape
from the 'default setting' of your own little legal system, however profitable and powerful it may be. It will
explain how something that is true 'legally speaking' may be utterly false. It will show you the difference between
the way things are around here and the way things ought to be. It may even help you envision the way things could
well be, if only we cared enough."

## Julie Dickson's philosophy of legal philosophy

Understanding law by elucidating it.

- The task of legal philosophy is to illuminate and explain aspects of something that exists in our social reality
  and that we call 'law'.
- Law is a complex phenomenon, and the task of legal philosophy is never-ending.
- Elucidation is an active process which requires one to identify law's most important and significant features.
- A proper understanding of law can help us appreciate how law bears upon our practical reasoning — for example, it
  can help us decide if we should take law as having authority over us (the question of week 12: is there an
  obligation to obey the law?).

## How to approach this subject…

**Curiosity** — follow the readings, but also your own questions. **Charity** — make the argument you are reading
the strongest version of the argument, as Hart does with Austin before trying to knock it down. **Clarity** — even
when you make a mistake, be clear. Don't memorise theories: use them to develop your own understanding of law.
