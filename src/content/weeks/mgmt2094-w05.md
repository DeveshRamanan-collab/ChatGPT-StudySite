## Theories of creative thinking and the creative problem-solving process

The week covers the Whole Brain Model, Wallas' model of the creative process, divergent and convergent thinking,
conditions of creative thinking, theories of creativity, and creative problem solving.

The lecture is an overview of the theories that are out there, not an exhaustive list. Find the ones that are useful to
you — for business, for work and for your own creative projects — and study them in greater depth.

## Whole Brain Model

| Quadrant | Thinking |
| --- | --- |
| A | Logical, analytical, fact based, quantitative |
| B | Organised, sequential, planned, detailed |
| C | Interpersonal, feeling based, kinaesthetic, emotional |
| D | Holistic, intuitive, integrating, synthesising |

- Herrmann's model is based on learning / thinking / doing.
- A and B sides are the preparation and verification; C and D sides of the brain are the incubation and illumination.
- This model looks at how you first look at a problem: it creates a thinking profile of you and how you respond to
  each quadrant.
- Preferred mode is 1 and competent mode is 2 (this is the way you function, but may not be your first choice);
  avoidance is given a 3. You may have more than one quadrant with the same number.

In the lecture: A is how scientists, mathematicians and chemists think — logically, mostly with numbers. B is planning,
organisation and following a structure. D is intuitive and holistic: integrating and synthesising different ideas,
which is where fresh thinking comes from, and this side is often ignored. C is interpersonal and feeling based:
noticing the emotions of others and how other people will think about your ideas.

Based on your personality you will prefer some quadrants and avoid others, but the problem decides which quadrant you
need: a maths problem needs the logical, quantitative side. If that is a side you avoid, be aware of it and
consciously push yourself towards it. The lecturer also cautioned that the quadrants do not necessarily correspond to
how your brain actually works.

:::examtip
Applying the model: name the quadrant the task needs, the person's code for it (1 preferred, 2 competent, 3 avoided),
and what follows — push towards an avoided quadrant, or draw on a teammate who prefers it.
:::

## Wallas' model of creative thinking

1. **Preparation** – focuses on the problem and explores the problem's dimensions
2. **Incubation** – subconscious mulling of the problem
3. **Intimation** – inkling that a solution may well be on its way
4. **Illumination** – discovery, "eureka": the idea bursts through…
5. **Verification** – focus on the practicality, effectiveness and appropriateness

It is a structured approach to creative problem solving – it needs time and a variety of people.

In the lecture: Intimation is the feeling that you are very close to finding a solution; Illumination is the moment
you jump in the air and say "yes, I found it". The fifth step still matters: you must logically check whether the
solution actually fits and solves the problem. That is especially important for overly creative people who generate
lots of ideas, because the problem is often matching and fitting the solutions to the problem. A team whose members
have different preferences can complement each other and draw on each other's strengths.

:::definition
**Intimation** — the feeling, in the fringe of consciousness, that a solution is coming before you know what it is:
"I often know that the solution is coming, though I don't know what the solution will be" (a civil servant quoted
by Wallas, in Sadler-Smith 2015).
:::

### The reading: Sadler-Smith (2015)

- Graham Wallas set out the model in *The Art of Thought* (1926). It is usually cited as four stages: Preparation,
  Incubation, Illumination (and its accompaniments), Verification. Wallas devoted 12 pages to **Intimation**, the
  fringe consciousness linking Incubation and Illumination, so a five-stage model is a more authentic representation.
- Wallas built the model from the physicist Helmholtz (a problem "investigated in all directions", then happy ideas
  arriving unexpectedly, without effort, on a slow walk up wooded hills) and the mathematician Poincaré, from whom he
  borrowed the term *verification*.
- Three levels of proximity to consciousness: Preparation and Verification are voluntary, effortful, conscious work;
  Incubation is involuntary, effortless, nonconscious work, not open to introspection; Intimation occurs in the
  fringe of consciousness and is only partly open to attention; Illumination is the instantaneous "flash" or "click".
- Incubation takes two forms: **distraction** (conscious work on other problems — more effective for less difficult
  creative thought) and **mental relaxation** (needed for more difficult creative thought, such as a scientific
  discovery, a poem or a political decision). Physical exercise may help. Posing the problem early gives Incubation
  more time.
- Preparation depends on domain-specific knowledge ("chance favours the prepared mind"). Verification is conscious and
  logical, and also social: the field — critics, peer reviewers, investors, venture capitalists — act as gatekeepers.
  Poincaré warned that an inspiration can feel certain and still fail "the test of verification".

## Theories of creativity

The lecture frames the first theories around normality. The "normal" state is stable and secure — well fed, healthy,
in a steady job, not venturing into unknown territory. To be creative you have to move away from that state in one
way or another. Many forces in society push us towards normality while organisations need more creativity, which makes
creativity a scarcer and more valuable resource.

### Psychoanalytical theory of creativity — Jung; Freud, Adler and Hammer

- Main tenet: people become more creative as a reaction to difficult circumstances and/or repressed emotions.
- When a situation becomes difficult or they go through a traumatic event, people pull back from their surroundings.
  They then rely on their creative side to find a solution to the difficult situation, as an outlet for their now
  repressed emotions.
- A sense of inferiority can contribute to creativity, and the creativity can make them feel superior and move forward.
- Freud suggests that "unsatisfied wishes are the driving power behind fantasies".

In the lecture: for Freud, desires and fantasies sit in the subconscious and often cannot be acted on, so they are
channelled and expressed through creative work — painting, writing, even new products or organisations. For Jung, the
unconscious (dreams among it) is a source of ideas that creative people interpret and translate into works for their
own culture.

### Mental illness theory of creativity — Briggs; Eisemann; Goodman

- For people to become creative, some form of mental illness needs to be present. It doesn't have to be severe, as
  this can actually impede.
- Common disorders associated are bipolar, manic-depressive and severe mood swings.
- Verhaeghen and colleagues, in a recent study, show that when a person is in a reflective mode they become more
  creative, but it can also lead to depression — indicative of artists and writers. It is suggested this is when
  people become sensitive to colours, sounds and emotions.
- It is said that stress actually impairs creativity by distracting us. The lecture added a qualification: not all
  stress is always bad, and some stressful situations can spur creativity.

### Addiction theory of creativity — Lapp, Collins, Norlander and Wallas

- Addiction to substances such as alcohol and drugs contributes to creativity and may even cause it in some people.
- One tenet: recreational drugs and alcohol remove the stress element that inhibits our creative ability.
- Under the influence, many people have a train of thought that others would view as loose and unpredictable — traits
  we relate to creativity.
- Against the theory: addiction doesn't cause the creativity; those with creative tendencies are more prone to
  addiction.

The lecturer was clear this is not an invitation to use substances: for people who are already creative, drugs and
stimulants can impede creativity and worsen anxiety and restlessness, and there are other ways to spur creativity.

### Creative theory of psychoticism — Eysenck

- All creative people have a disposition for psychotic tendencies, which form the basis for creative personalities.
- Psychoticism is seen as halfway between a "normal" person and the state of psychosis.
- A psychotic generally rejects social, cultural or authoritarian norms — an anti-convention form of rebellion.
- They are risk takers, impulsive, disregard common sense and are spontaneous in nature.

In the lecture: creating something new means going against social norms at least to a small degree, so the theory
intuitively makes some sense.

### Humanistic theory of creativity — Maslow; Rogers and Fromm

- Looking at Maslow's hierarchy of needs: once we get to self-actualisation, we are free and comfortable enough to
  express ourselves in a creative manner.
- The theory argues that environment is not a factor in creativity: a person who is able to meet all their needs can
  then choose to be creative.
- Creativity is central to our growth and learning processes.

Maslow's hierarchy, from the base: **physiological** (air, water, food, shelter, sleep, clothing, reproduction) →
**safety** (personal security, employment, resources, health, property) → **love and belonging** (friendship,
intimacy, family, sense of connection) → **esteem** (respect, self-esteem, status, recognition, strength, freedom) →
**self-actualisation** (the desire to become the most that one can be). The lecture: we cannot be creative unless, at
least partly, the other needs are met.

### Maslow's three types of creativity

- **Primary**: creativity that allows us to escape the daily grind / stress of life, channelled into painting, drawing,
  writing and so on. (The lecture: it need not be any good.)
- **Secondary**: a higher level of thought to achieve; more thought out and involved than primary creativity.
- **Integrated**: combines both. It may be spontaneous when we commence the creative work, but a lot more thought goes
  into what we hope to achieve before we begin — e.g. philosophy and scientific discoveries.

### Theories of creative grace / accident

- **Creativity by grace**: the idea of genius — people endowed with a superhuman potential; creativity seen as a divine
  gift. (The lecture: on this view you are either super creative or you are not, and nothing can be done about it.)
- **Creativity by accident**: it rises by chance. Accidental discoveries, e.g. immunisation arising from an
  interruption in work, radioactivity from a wrong hypothesis.

### Theories of creative association / cognitive

- **Creative association**: applying procedures from one area to another. It underlies the justification for many
  divergent thinking techniques such as lateral thinking and brainstorming. (The lecture: you notice patterns in one
  field and bring them to another — an interdisciplinary approach.)
- **Cognitive creativity**: it is a normal human activity; it is about reasoning, recognition and understanding.

:::examtip
Theory questions turn on the trigger each theory names: hardship or repressed emotion (psychoanalytical), a mild
disorder (mental illness), substances (addiction), an anti-convention disposition (psychoticism), needs met
(humanistic), a gift (grace), chance (accident), a transfer between areas (association), ordinary reasoning
(cognitive).
:::

## Divergent and convergent thinking

- Divergent thinking is about coming up with many possible solutions to the one problem.
- Convergent thinking is solving a problem with only one single correct solution.

Both were covered in more detail in Weeks 2 and 3. In creative problem solving the two alternate: diverge to list many
options, converge to choose.

## Creative problem solving

- **Objective finding** – defines the problem area
- **Fact finding** – gather the information
- **Problem finding** – defines the problem correctly
- **Idea finding** – generates many solutions to the problem
- **Solution finding** – evaluates and chooses between possible solutions
- **Acceptance finding** – implements the chosen ideas correctly

**Objective finding** essentially involves divergent thinking to generate a list of problems; convergence is then used
to identify the most relevant problem areas for further exploration. Hits and hotspots are identified by questioning:
(1) ownership — is one motivated to solve it; (2) priority — how important is the problem; (3) critical nature — how
urgent is it to be solved.

**Fact finding**: overall comprehension of the problem is increased by collecting relevant information. This also
helps new ideas to be generated; hits and hotspots can assist convergence; and the previously identified problems may
now be seen from a new perspective.

**Problem finding** essentially uses the previous stage's "hits" to identify the most productive problem definition
possible — the lecture calls this an extremely important part, where many problems are narrowed down to one defined
precisely. **Idea finding** helps to structure the search for potential solutions: mainly divergent activity
generates many ideas using a variety of idea generation aids. **Solution finding** is basically the choice of ideas
that can be transformed into workable solutions.

**Acceptance finding** is a divergent activity that helps to implement solutions successfully: (1) listing potential
implementation obstacles and ways to overcome them; (2) developing both preventive actions and contingency plans;
(3) generating an action plan to implement a solution.

Creative problem solving is intuitive and natural: it is not designed to replace our natural creative processes but to
build upon them. It is research based (much research has tried to come up with ONE best way). It is very
generalisable — it can be used in all aspects of life, where other models are more domain specific. It requires
different mental operations, so it is a higher order thinking skill with sub-skills.

## Four preferences to thinking creatively

1. **The Clarifier** asks "are we really solving the right problem?". They want to be quick to move to the solution,
   like doing research and gathering information, attend to the detail and are methodical; they ask lots of questions
   because they crave information.
2. **The Ideator** looks at the big picture and dreams of possibilities. They like to toy with ideas, stretch their
   imagination with out-of-the-box ideas and jump from A to Z. They are adventurous and dreamers.
3. **The Developer** takes an idea and tinkers with it, improving on others' ideas. They put together workable
   solutions and analyse competing solutions, looking at the pros and cons. They are perfectionists who can get stuck
   developing the perfect solution, and others may see them as nit-pickers finding flaws in others' solutions.
4. **The Implementer** is quick to action, always moving forward. They need to see ideas hit fruition and want things
   to happen; they are decisive and determined. They can leap into action too soon, appear too pushy and try to
   oversell their idea.

Understanding your preference can improve your creative performance. By working in teams you may well have others who
think differently to you, which should lead to complementary ideas and to solving a problem more effectively by taking
into account the different paths to the solution. The lecture tied this to emotional intelligence: if you understand
your own strengths and weaknesses and can see them in others, you can match traits in a team.

## Readings

### Markman (2017), How You Define the Problem Determines Whether You Solve It

- Stories of invention focus on the novel solution — Dyson adapting the industrial cyclone to eliminate the vacuum
  bag, Picasso and Braque's cubism, the Xerox PARC desktop's spatial user interface — which makes creativity seem a
  mystery.
- Most people who come up with creative solutions find them inside the collective memory of the people working on the
  problem. Tapping memory is one of the most cost-effective and repeatable approaches, compared with luck (Archimedes
  in the bath) or decades of research and development.
- Memory works by cues (a birthday party brings hats, cake and singing to mind; salad brings lettuce, tomatoes and
  dressing). The statement of the problem is the cue to memory, so changing the description draws out new information.
- Dyson: "separating dirt from air" does not mention the bag at all, and industrial cyclones are one way to separate
  particles from air. A radically new solution requires a new problem statement.
- There is no ideal problem statement. The most consistently creative people and groups find many descriptions: some
  specific (objects acted on), others abstract (relationships among objects, or the goal). Not "think outside the box",
  but find more descriptions of the box.

### Claman (2017), How to Spark Creativity When You're in a Rut

- On your first day at work you asked questions, compared, suggested and evaluated: as a new person you felt entitled
  to look at things differently — a sign of your creativity.
- Everyone has creativity when confronted with new problems or new ideas; it's not just for the talented few or for
  people in artistic roles. But creativity can fade when you get bored or discouraged, and a work groove can turn into
  a rut.
- Five questions to spot a rut: a recurring pattern to your workdays; feeling you must agree with colleagues and
  bosses to get along; seeing obstacles everywhere to new ideas; "That won't work. It's been tried too many times
  before"; "It doesn't matter what I do, really. They don't care."
- Four suggestions: **think new** (new people, clients, assignments, skills); **look for intersections** (creativity
  occurs at the crossroads of different people and ideas; volunteer for cross-functional work); **capitalise on
  obstacles** (every obstacle is an opportunity for research and analysis); **share what you know** (blogging,
  training, mentoring).
- Habits that sap creativity leave you just "doing stuff" — only transactions; habits that enhance it make the job your
  own. The lecturer especially recommended this reading: apply it to yourself and your career.
