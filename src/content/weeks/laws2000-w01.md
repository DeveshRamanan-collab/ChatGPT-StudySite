## What is international law?

Three definitions of public international law recur in the lecture.

:::definition
"Public international law consists of rules and principles which facilitate the conduct of states and
international organisations in their relations with one another and, in some cases, with individuals,
groups and transnational companies." — Office of International Law, Attorney-General's Department,
Australian Government
:::

:::definition
"...international law by definition is universally applicable and authoritative for all States.
States recognise international law as 'law' by voluntarily consenting and adhering to agreed
standards. ... Australian lawyers practice international law before Australian courts [and before
international courts]. Their practice can include holding Australia to account for its international
obligations [or promoting Australia's interests in the international fora]..." — The Practitioner's
Guide to International Law (2nd Edition, 2014)
:::

:::case
S.S. Lotus (France v Turkey), 1927 PCIJ (ser A) No 10, para 44 — "International law governs
relations between independent States. The rules of law binding upon States therefore emanate from
their own free will as expressed in conventions or by usages generally accepted as expressing
principles of law and established in order to regulate the relations between these co-existing
independent communities or with a view to the achievement of common aims. Restrictions upon the
independence of States cannot therefore be presumed."
:::

Each definition insists on a different aspect of what international law is. What the unit covers is
basically general public international law — the key issues, the key subjects of public
international law.

## International law is everywhere

International law regulates a lot of activities in our everyday life, even where this looks purely
domestic: the way we tell the time (the Final Act of the International Meridian Conference, 1884,
fixed Greenwich as the primary meridian), the cars we drive and their functions (Global Technical
Regulations for Wheeled Vehicles 1998), products and food, and the internet and cloud services this
lecture itself depends on (Constitution of the International Telecommunications Union, 1865; Space
Treaty, 1967; Law of the Sea Convention; Convention for the Protection of Submarine Telegraph
Cables; Uruguay Round Agreement, World Trade Organization Treaty System and related instruments).

## General and specialised international law

"General" international law — sources, treaty law, statehood and recognition, jurisdiction and
immunities, diplomatic protection, state responsibility, peaceful settlement of disputes — is
pretty much what this unit covers this semester. "Special" international law covers use of force
and the law of armed conflict, international criminal law, international human rights law,
international law of the sea, international air and space law, international environmental and
climate change law, international energy law, international trade law, international investment
law, and international health law.

These regimes do not operate in silos: when you talk about an issue in an exam question, sometimes
you will have to borrow and draw from different regimes, not necessarily just one. A vessel that
saves migrants at sea and wants to dock in a country different from the country where it is
registered raises migration law, the law of the sea, and human rights law — you can even have
perhaps public health law, international health law. A dispute between two states on the use of an
agrochemical product for growing food, where the product has an impact on the environment of one or
more states, draws on international health law, trade law, environmental law and state
responsibility together.

:::examtip
Classify which regime(s) an exam fact pattern actually engages before choosing a rule — most
realistic problems borrow from more than one.
:::

## Issues of shared concern, and the two routes

International law is importantly interested in issues of shared or common concern — issues of a
global or transboundary nature, not limited to a certain state, that will have an impact that
crosses over to other states: pandemics and epidemics, access to medicines and vaccines, global
trade, the high seas, and climate change. There are two potential routes: "the nationalist route"
(addressing an issue domestically only) and "the internationalist route" (bringing states together
to adopt standards and rules), the internationalist route increasingly the more efficient of the two.

The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 showed that public international health law was a necessary
regulatory framework, and that coordination between states — and even within states — was, at the
time, inadequate. Following 2020 the World Health Organization worked on a framework with its
member states to try to mitigate the risk of a pandemic: preparedness, and tools for what to do when
there is a pandemic, especially the fair and equitable distribution of vaccines. The 2025 pandemic
agreement's regime for vaccine distribution is "a little bit weak", but there is nonetheless an
attempt at regulating how the world will address pandemics in future.

Global commons — the high seas, outer space, Antarctica — don't belong to one state in particular;
no state has jurisdiction over these areas, and even a land-bound country always has freedom of
navigation on, and access to, the high seas.

## International law as a living process

:::rule
International law as a living process is "a process of continuous interaction between States
grounded into practices or expectations of sovereign governments. It changes and evolves as those
practices and/or expectations are changed by the existence of new interests and technologies." It is
also "a process in which certain rights are asserted, and duties relied on by reference to norms
based on express agreements or custom."
:::

International law evolves in different ways — through the adoption of new treaties, and through the
modification of existing treaties: treaties can be modified by adding new provisions, amending
provisions of a treaty, or by adopting protocols that will be added to the treaty but that form a
whole with it. Customary international law, developed through state practice, is perhaps the best
way of illustrating international law as a living thing: state practice, which needs to be general,
together with a belief that this is law (or at least how the law should be), is how the law develops
through time and practice.

International environmental law illustrates how one regime of international law has developed almost
from scratch. There was not much of an international legal framework before the 1960s. The United
Nations was key: it hosted UN conferences (the UN Conference on the Human Environment, 1972),
created UN programs and agencies (the UN Environment Program, alongside other specialised agencies
not dedicated to the environment, such as the Food and Agriculture Organization), and, through the UN
General Assembly — whose resolutions and declarations are not treaties and are not binding, because
the General Assembly cannot create binding obligations on states — built documents that created
momentum for a framework further down the track (the Rio Declaration on the Human Environment, 1992).
States eventually transformed that momentum into binding treaties: the United Nations Framework
Convention on Climate Change (1992), the Kyoto Protocol (1997), and the Paris Agreement (2015), and
the ICJ's 2025 Advisory Opinion on Obligations of States in respect of Climate Change followed from
that same body of work.

:::case
Ozone depletion is a striking numerical example: the 1985 Vienna Convention for the Protection of the
Ozone Layer (entered into force 1988) and the 1987 Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the
Ozone Layer (entered into force 1990) were followed by a sharp decline in emissions of ozone-depleting
substances; the UN reported in 2023 that "the ozone layer is on track to recover by 2040."
:::

## How international law differs from domestic law

Unlike a domestic legal system, international law has no universally compulsory procedure for the
settlement of disputes, and neither a legislative nor an executive branch. International law rules
become legally binding when there is sufficient consensus and practice among states to generate a
rule of customary international law, or when states agree to be bound by a treaty. Sovereign states
are the makers of international law and its "enforcers" if, when, they choose so — but enforcement
is irrelevant to the binding quality of international law as a system of law. International law
doesn't have a legislator: there is no parliament that adopts the law, and no police to enforce it.
In international law, states are everything — they are creating the law, they are interpreting the
law, and then the law applies to them; they are the creator, but also the subject of the law, which
is different from a domestic legal system. The International Court of Justice, often called "the
world court", is not compulsory: states need to agree to be bound by the jurisdiction of the court to
actually be brought before it by another state.

:::examtip
"No one in this Great Hall of Justice is starry-eyed about international law, but it is what we
have." — Philippe Sands. Violations of international law still occur (occupation of territories, use
of force, targeted killings, disrespect of treaties), but just as crimes occur within national legal
systems without destroying them, frequent assaults on international law do not invalidate its
legitimacy: the vast majority of states abide by their obligations, and there has been huge progress
in shared responsibilities and regimes on environmental protection, natural resources and public
health.
:::

## Why do states comply?

States choose to operate within the system of international law not necessarily because the system
itself is peremptory, but because it is necessary if anything meaningful is to be achieved.

:::rule
"...the basis of obligation is the same in every legal order: a consciousness among the subjects that
this order is needed if one is to reach a common end" (Hoffman, 1987). Compliance comes from fear not
of sanction but of "loss of reputation", which leads other states to exclude that state from future
opportunities to cooperate (Chayes & Chayes, 1995).
:::

There is also quasi-enforcement: membership and participation in international organizations and
agencies is contingent on compliance and trust (Articles 5 and 6, UN Charter, allow suspension or
expulsion from membership; the International Monetary Fund and World Bank can exert some economic
leverage over members' conduct). More broadly, a state that always or often doesn't respect its
international commitments risks other states becoming more reluctant to trade with it, or develop
alliances with it, in future — illustrated by the tariff regime the US has imposed on other
countries, and by European and Canadian moves to develop trade partnerships they see as more secure.

The realist view in international relations theory holds that what matters most is power, especially
military power, and that when law and power conflict, it is almost inevitably law that will lose --
the militarily powerful state will only obey international law when it is in its perceived interest
to do so. Although enforcement is seen as pretty much key to the respect of rules in domestic law,
there is debate as to whether enforcement and sanctions are necessary in a legal system at all.

## A brief history: from the polis to Westphalia

International law's early traces are found in ancient cultures and communities that had traded for
millennia and held mutual expectations as to how their members would behave among themselves, and
with the outside world. Scholars have identified the concept of the polis — the Greek city-state, an
urban centre surrounded by countryside and protected by walls, a public space where decisions about
governing the polis were taken — and the idea of "nomos" in ancient Greece: restricting natural
freedoms for the sake of expediency and self-interest. Thucydides, in the History of the
Peloponnesian War, wrote that "right, as the world goes, is only in question between equals in
power, while the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must" — an idea that founding
figures of international law such as Grotius and Vattel drew on directly, and one still echoed
(inverted) in the modern principle of "Sovereign Equality" (Art 2, UN Charter). Ancient Roman law
developed "Jus naturale...Jus civile...", and these ancient practices reappeared — without a natural
continuity — in the Roman Empire and later the Catholic Church, which enjoyed spiritual authority
across the Western world and whose Pope could exercise considerable influence over local emperors and
kings.

Most textbooks consider international law to have developed as a system of law in Europe in the late
Middle Ages — the 15th and 16th centuries — a period of overlapping allegiances and different, often
juxtaposed, political and religious forces (the Holy Roman Empire and the Catholic Church). Theories
of natural law (Jus Naturale) held that there were intrinsic values, universal and applicable across
time and place, governing relations between individuals and their social surroundings; jus gentium
was, by contrast, the rules of law common to all nations — the law created by humans, variable in
time and place. Hugo Grotius (1583-1645) first secularised the idea of natural law and then
distinguished it from jus gentium; jus gentium's source was the consent of states, not some higher
ground based on natural reason. Grotius's major contributions concerned the freedom of the seas and
the law of war and peace (The Freedom of the Seas; On the Law of War and Peace), and it is from jus
gentium that international law, as we know it today, seems to have subsequently developed as a
distinct discipline of law.

This period — the early 14th to 17th centuries — was also when colonialism began, with early
confrontations between Spanish explorers and native populations in what was then called the New
World. This caused problems for natural-law scholars: the Spanish Dominican professor Francisco de
Vitoria (1483-1546), in lectures given in 1532, concluded that native populations of the New World
were part of the human race and that natural law therefore applied to them; he rejected the claim
that Spanish forces could conquer new territories on the basis of "discovery", because there was no
discovery — there were already local people living on those territories. For Vitoria, conquest, like
any other war of territorial expansion, was not justified under natural law. The Jesuit Francisco
Suarez (1548-1617), whose 1612 treatise on laws and God the lawgiver profoundly shaped early thinking
about international relations, and Emmerich de Vattel (1714-1767), whose Law of Nations underlined
the idea of state equality irrespective of power (at least within Europe) and distinguished natural
law from "laws of action" (positive law) — arguing that only positive law, decided by states or
people, is relevant — were the other key figures of this period.

:::rule
The Peace of Westphalia (1648) — the Peace Treaties of Munster and Osnabruck, brought about at the
end of the Thirty Years' War that had ravaged continental Europe — is the "beginning" of the
international state system: the idea was to organise territory and individuals into sovereign
states, out of the need for a system with a clear source of authority — the birth of the notion of
"sovereignty", an authority with absolute and indivisible power over people and territory. To this
day, the Peace of Westphalia, and the system that emerged from it, remains a key building block of
the international legal system, and the whole UN framework is based on Westphalian principles,
especially respect for equal rights and self-determination of peoples and sovereign equality of all
UN member states.
:::

## The nineteenth century to the United Nations

In the nineteenth century, positivism became the dominant theory: the only true source of law was
the will of states — not fairness, not divine justice, but consent to be bound by a legal
obligation — so that validity flowed from state consent, and an obligation was imposed only on
states that had consented to be bound by it. States could choose which legal obligations they were
bound to: certain states ratify a treaty, other states decide not to, so states end up with different
rights and obligations at the international level. This was understandable, as it was the period of
the Enlightenment, which really prioritised rationality and empiricism over divine order. Positivism
led to a number of formal institutions and treaties: the 1865 International Telegraph Union, the 1874
Universal Postal Union, the 1856 Declaration of Paris (setting legal limits on the capture of private
property at sea), the 1868 Declaration of St Petersburg (banning the use of explosive bullets), and
the Hague Conferences of 1899 and 1907, which led to the adoption of conventions on the conduct of war
and established a Permanent Court of Arbitration. Colonialism continued throughout: at the outbreak
of the First World War in 1914, 90 per cent of Africa was under European control.

Despite this progress, the First World War raged from 1914 to 1918. In the interwar period, states
established the League of Nations (1919) to try to avoid a second major war; it did not itself
prohibit war, but it imposed a legal obligation on member states to submit disputes likely to lead to
conflict to adjudication, or inquiry. The 1928 Treaty of Paris (the Kellogg-Briand Pact) further
established an obligation that states should refrain from going to war as a means of settling their
international controversies and as an instrument of national policy. The Permanent Court of
International Justice (PCIJ), created in 1920, dissolved in 1946, but the precursor of today's
International Court of Justice, developed a solid body of case law still referred to today for
guidance and for the identification of legal principles, despite the PCIJ itself no longer existing.

The Second World War followed (1939-1945). The world reacted very strongly to the atrocities that
happened during it: the 1945-1946 Nuremberg Trials prosecuted top German officials, including leading
figures of the Nazi regime, for international crimes — the beginning of what we call international
criminal law (today's International Criminal Court is its successor, though the field became more
mainstream only with the creation of the ICC in the 1990s). The League of Nations was replaced by the
United Nations (1946), whose constitutive instrument, the UN Charter, entrusted it with the
maintenance of international peace and security — peace, security, stability and justice --
respecting the principle of equal rights and self-determination of peoples, and the sovereign
equality of all UN member states, and banning the use of force. The UN Security Council has the
primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security; the UN General
Assembly, in which all member states are represented and each has one vote of equal weight, has only
a consultative role — it does not have the ability to create binding law the way the Security
Council does — but it offers the organisational structure where all states can meet, negotiate,
voice their opinions and make declarations. The Assembly has been instrumental in the promotion of a
range of goals of global interest, notably decolonisation: when the United Nations was created,
almost one-third of the world's population lived in a territory dependent, in some form, on a
colonial power; in 1960 the Assembly adopted the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to
Colonial Countries and Peoples, affirming the right of all peoples to self-determination and
proclaiming that colonialism should be brought to a speedy and unconditional end. Today more than 80
former colonies have gained their independence. The International Law Commission, created in 1946,
promotes "the progressive development of international law and its codification."
