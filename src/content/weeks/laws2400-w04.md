## 1. Overview

This week we focus on various defences that may be pleaded in cases related to intentional torts. One
conceptual question is about the meaning of defences: are these part of the elements of a tort, or
external to those elements? What do you think? And why does it matter? As the consent material below
shows, the answer changes who bears the onus of proof at trial.

## 2. Consent

If a person freely gives consent to a tortious conduct, no action is generally available. This defence is
underpinned by deference to the autonomy of individuals. Consent is available as a defence to battery,
assault, false imprisonment, trespass to land and interference with chattels.

### Defence or element? The onus debate

Courts in England and New Zealand have taken the view that consent is an element of trespassory conduct —
so the onus is on the plaintiff to prove lack of consent. However, courts in Canada and Australia have
taken a different view.

:::case
McHugh J in *Marion's Case* (1992) 175 CLR 218 reasoned in obiter that consent is a defence and therefore
the onus is on the defendant to prove that there was consent. Leeming JA in *White v Johnston* [2015]
NSWCA 18 (unnecessary treatment by a dentist) considered in obiter that the better view is that consent is
an essential element of assault and battery.
:::

This means there is some uncertainty in Australia regarding whether consent is a defence or part of the
elements. Does it matter much if consent were to be regarded as part of the elements? It changes who loses
when the evidence on consent is unclear.

### Validity of consent

Consent may be express or implied. In order to be valid, consent must: be voluntary/free; come from a
competent person; and be in relation to the act complained of. It is critical that consent is given
freely: the individual should have sufficient knowledge as to what she is consenting to, and there is no
force, fraud, threat or intimidation.

However, not every misrepresentation will nullify consent. If the plaintiff understands the "nature and
character" of the interference, consent will be valid even if there were false inducements — e.g., a false
promise of marriage to induce sexual relations.

:::case
*Dean v Phung* [2012] NSWCA 223 — Dr Phung treated Dean for minor chipping of his front teeth but conducted
more than 53 consultations to perform unnecessary procedures, costing Dean $73,640. The court found that
the consent was not validly given.
:::

Consent must also not be exceeded: because consent is often specific to a specific act, it will not be a
good defence if the defendant's conduct exceeds the consent given.

:::case
*McNamara v Duncan* (1971) 26 ALR 584 — the defendant deliberately struck the plaintiff on the head,
fracturing his skull, during a game of Australian Rules Football. The court rejected the argument that the
plaintiff must have consented to receive such a blow and thus accepted the risk of such injury: while
forcible bodily contact is part of Australian Rules Football, it will not cover deliberately inflicted
injuries.
:::

### Capacity and minors

The consent should also come from a competent person — there should be legal capacity to give consent,
meaning sufficient understanding to comprehend the act as well as its consequences. Adults are presumed to
have this capacity. But what if an adult was drunk, asleep or unconscious?

:::case
*Brightwater Care Group v Rossiter* (2009) 40 WAR 84 — a completely paralysed person, unable to consume by
mouth any nutrition or fluids, directed the care home staff to discontinue nutrition and fluids. Noting the
important right to autonomy or self-determination, the court ruled that a medical practitioner or service
provider will commit a trespass against the patient if this was done against the wishes of a medically
competent patient.
:::

The issue of consent may raise complex questions: what if an adult woman declines treatment which will
save the life of her unborn child? What about persons with intellectual disability? What about a minor
with a sufficient understanding?

:::case
The House of Lords in *Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech Area Health Authority* [1986] 1 AC 112 held that
a 15-year-old girl could give valid consent to receiving medical advice about a prescription for the
contraceptive pill without her parents' consent. It reasoned that a minor could give informed consent when
she achieves a sufficient understanding and intelligence to enable her to "understand fully what is
proposed".
:::

The High Court of Australia approved the *Gillick* principle in *Marion's Case* (1992) 175 CLR 218 (an
application authorising sterilisation of an intellectually disabled teenager, Marion, brought by her
parents). The High Court observed that the fact that a child is disabled does not of itself mean that she
cannot give informed consent. After considering the nature of sterilisation, it ruled that parents cannot
provide consent to authorise non-therapeutic treatment of a child that is not in the child's interests.

The parents' consent has been held to be insufficient where the medical procedure is not for the treatment
of the child's illness — e.g., sterilisation of mentally disabled minors, gender reassignment surgery.
Moreover, for certain treatments, consent of courts may be required by statutes — e.g., the *Children and
Young Persons (Care and Protection) Act 1998* (NSW).

:::case
*X v Sydney Children's Hospital Network* (2013) 85 NSWLR 294 — a 17-year-old Jehovah's Witness, competent,
refused a blood transfusion; his parents supported the refusal. Held: the parens patriae jurisdiction
extends to any person under 18, even a competent "mature minor". Section 174 of the Care and Protection Act
does not exclude it absent clear words. (See also *H v AC* [2024] NSWSC 40.)
:::

## 3. Self-defence

If the defendant acted in self-defence, it is a defence to a claim for trespass to the person. To avail
this defence, the defendant must prove two conditions at common law:

:::rule
*Zecevic v DPP* (1987) 162 CLR 645 — (1) there was a reasonable apprehension of physical force to the
defendant or others; and (2) the force used by the defendant was proportionate to the threat.
:::

:::case
*Fontin v Katapodis* (1962) 108 CLR 177 — Fontin incorrectly accused Katapodis of not paying for goods,
which resulted in heated arguments; Katapodis picked up a wooden T-square and broke it on Fontin's
shoulder; Fontin threw an off-cut of glass at Katapodis's face, injuring his hand. The High Court held that
while Fontin had the right to defend himself, throwing a piece of glass at Katapodis's face "was out of all
reasonable proportion to the emergency confronting Fontin".
:::

The defence is available for the protection of third parties such as family members or even strangers. It
may also be invoked to protect property (both land and chattels) against unlawful interference by
trespassers. The defence to protect land is generally only available to the person who has possession —
moreover, a request to leave the land should first be made and the opportunity given to comply with the
request.

### Effect of the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW)

The common law of self-defence has been codified and modified by statutory provisions drawn from the
criminal law. Sections 52–54 of the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) (CLA) are a case in point. Self-defence
operates under both regimes in parallel (see ss 3A(1), 3B(1)(a)(iii) CLA) with different tests.

:::statute
Section 52 CLA provides that self-defence will absolve the defendant from civil liability if used in
response to "unlawful conduct" and a two-limb test is satisfied — the person believed the conduct was
necessary for a listed purpose, and the conduct was a reasonable response in the circumstances as they
perceived them. The legislation does not define "unlawful conduct", though this has been held to include
both criminal and tortious conduct. The common law test does not have the unlawful conduct requirement.
:::

:::case
*NSW v McMaster* [2015] NSWCA 228 — Justin's partner, sister and mother suffered a violent home invasion
including physical and sexual assault; police arrived after a call from Justin's partner. Justin — holding
a metal curtain rod — ran out yelling "Where's my sister"; as he ran towards Constable K he was shot by
Constable F. The trial court held there was battery and that the constable was not entitled to
self-defence. The Court of Appeal ruled that in the circumstances both the subjective and objective
requirements of the self-defence test were satisfied. The Court of Appeal also found that Justin's own
conduct was unlawful, because even though not intentional, it was itself a negligent trespass to the
person.
:::

## 4. Legal process

Lawful arrest is a defence to trespass to the person. Under the *Law Enforcement (Powers and
Responsibilities) Act 2002* (NSW) (LEPRA), s 99(1) provides that arrest without warrant requires BOTH (a)
suspicion on reasonable grounds that the person is committing or has committed an offence AND (b)
satisfaction that arrest is reasonably necessary for a listed reason. Suspicion is more than a possibility,
less than a belief. Sections 230 and 231 confine the force used to such force as is reasonably necessary.
(See *Owlstara v State of NSW* [2020] NSWCA 217.)

The defence also covers entering and remaining on land. Recall *Roy v O'Neill* (2020) 273 CLR 91 (from
Topic 3): police entered the unit's curtilage for a proactive domestic violence order compliance check. The
High Court held, 3:2, that this was within the implied licence (*Halliday v Nevill*). Their observations of
Roy's state of intoxication then enlivened the power to remain on the land for a breath test (*Police
Administration Act 1978* (NT) s 126(2A)), so the officer had independent authority to remain.

## 5. Provocation

Provocation is a defence to a criminal charge, but it is generally not available as a defence to a tort
action.

:::rule
See *Fontin v Katapodis* (Katapodis injured Fontin's shoulder in an argument, and Fontin argued he was
provoked): provocation cannot reduce an award of compensatory damages. It may, though, assist to reduce or
prevent an award of exemplary damages. Queensland and Western Australia are an exception — provocation may
be pleaded as a defence there.
:::

## 6. Contributory negligence

Contributory negligence has been rejected several times as a defence to intentional torts. Consider the
policy reasons behind this: typically this is a situation where the plaintiff has been careless in some
way — but should that allow the defendant, who has intentionally interfered with another, to escape
consequences?

:::case
*Horkin v North Melbourne Football Club* [1993] VR 153 — Horkin was asked to leave the club as he had had
too much to drink; when he did not leave, club employees threw him out with more force than necessary,
injuring him. The trial judge reduced the defendant's liability by 30% due to contributory negligence
(intoxication, failure to leave). The Victorian Supreme Court held that it will be unfair and unjust to
reduce liability for intentional torts: intentional battery vs the plaintiff's carelessness — "the
difference is not merely in degree but in kind, and the social condemnation attached to the fault differs
markedly".
:::

Should contributory negligence apply as a defence to conversion — for example, if a car left unlocked with
keys in the ignition is stolen? You could argue that if a plaintiff has been careless with their vehicle,
they created an opportunity for theft, and allowing the defence would encourage more personal
responsibility. Alternatively, a plaintiff's carelessness doesn't justify theft.

## 7. Necessity

The defence of necessity may arise in varied situations: public necessity (e.g., if a ship is sinking, you
may throw luggage out of the ship to save lives); necessity to protect one's property (e.g., trespassing on
a neighbour's land to rescue your cat); and action to preserve the life or health of a person who is unable
to consent (e.g., a doctor treats an unconscious patient without consent to save their life).

:::rule
The defence of necessity may be available if (1) the action was necessary to prevent serious harm to
person or property, and (2) the action was reasonable in the circumstances and proportionate to the risk
posed.
:::

The reasonableness of the defendant's act is assessed on the facts of each case.

:::case
*Southwark London Borough Council v Williams* [1971] Ch 734 — the defendants, who were squatters, pleaded
necessity when the Council sought a court order for immediate possession. Lord Denning rejected the
defence: if homelessness is admitted as a defence to trespass, no one's house would be safe! "The doctrine
[must] be carefully circumscribed."
:::

The defence of necessity is available to a defendant who acts to protect property and, by doing so,
inflicts damage to the plaintiff's property.

:::case
*Proudman v Allen* [1954] SASR 336 — the defendant saw a rogue car running driverless down a slope towards
another car and turned the rogue car's steering wheel to avoid a collision; this caused the rogue car to
roll into the sea. The car's owner sued. The defendant successfully used the defence of necessity. Even
volunteers or strangers who act to protect the property of others can use this defence.
:::

Necessity may be pleaded as a defence in trespass to the person, as it was (alongside self-defence) in
*NSW v McMaster* above, where there was a situation of imminent danger and the steps taken were reasonably
necessary to ward off the danger.

The defence of necessity may also arise in the medical context: can a doctor plead necessity where the
treatment is for the benefit of a patient who is unable to give consent (e.g., an unconscious patient, or a
patient who is a child or under another incapacity)?

:::case
*In re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation)* [1990] 2 AC 1 — F, a 36-year-old woman with a serious mental
disability, developed a sexual relationship with a fellow patient; the doctors felt F would not be able to
cope with pregnancy and childbirth and there were no other viable alternatives, so F's mother sought a
court declaration that sterilisation would be lawful. Lord Goff granted the declaration.
:::

Lord Goff laid down two requirements to apply the necessity principle: (1) there must be a necessity to act
when it is not practicable to communicate with the assisted person; and (2) the action taken must be such
as a reasonable person would, in all the circumstances, take, acting in the best interests of the assisted
person. The necessity principle applies both in cases of emergency (e.g., treatment of an unconscious
person) and in cases with a permanent state of affairs (e.g., treatment of a mentally disabled patient).
However, Lord Goff drew a distinction between these: if a patient is temporarily incapacitated, the doctor
should do no more than is necessary before the patient recovers consciousness; if the patient is
permanently incapacitated, there is no point in waiting to obtain the patient's consent — the doctor must
act in the best interests of the patient.

:::examtip
Legislation often governs specific medical situations (e.g., treatment of children, or of intellectually
disabled adults) and should be checked before falling back on the common law necessity principle.
:::
