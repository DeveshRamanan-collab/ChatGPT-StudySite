## What is international criminal law?

International criminal law (ICL) is a branch of public international law and consists of:

- **Substantive law**: (i) governs the four core international crimes and (ii) demands that states prosecute these
  crimes; and
- **Procedural law**: regulates the various stages of international criminal proceedings.

:::definition
International crimes are breaches of international rules entailing the personal criminal liability of the
individual(s) concerned.
:::

First limb — substantive law: the acts amounting to core international crimes; the subjective elements required for
such acts to be prohibited; the circumstances under which persons accused of international crimes may nevertheless
not be held criminally liable; and the conditions under which states may prosecute or bring to trial accused persons.

Second limb — procedural law: the sets of rules governing the various stages of international criminal prosecutions —
pre-trial processes, trial processes, appeals processes, rights of the accused, rules of evidence, sentencing, and
victims' rights (victims have a prominent part in proceedings). These rules are found in the statutes and rules of the
various international(ised) criminal tribunals.

## A new, rudimentary and hybrid branch

- ICL is a relatively new branch of public international law. It has gradually expanded to cover new crimes.
- ICL is a rudimentary branch: the broadening of the substance of ICL has been a complex process; determining the
  definitions of the actus reus and mens rea of these crimes has not always been clear; and the penalties for these
  crimes are not laid down precisely.
- ICL is a hybrid branch: it presents unique characteristics deriving from international humanitarian law (IHL) and
  international human rights law (IHRL), as well as from national criminal laws.

Relevant international(ised) criminal tribunals: the International Military Tribunal (IMT); the International Military
Tribunal for the Far East (IMTFE); the International Criminal Tribunal for the Former Yugoslavia (ICTY); the
International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda (ICTR); the International Residual Mechanism for Criminal Tribunals (MICT);
the International Criminal Court (ICC); the Special Court for Sierra Leone (SCSL); the Extraordinary Chambers in the
Courts of Cambodia (ECCC); the Special Tribunal for Lebanon (STL); the Special Panels for Serious Crimes (SPSC); the
Iraqi High Tribunal (IHT); and the Kosovo Specialist Chambers (KSC).

## The Nuremberg International Military Tribunal

Despite their shared intention to punish Axis war criminals, the Allied leadership was divided over how to do so:
differences existed over the purpose and the procedure to be applied. The London Agreement, with the IMT Charter
annexed, created the IMT. The Charter provided for the individual criminal responsibility of leaders, organisers,
instigators and accomplices.

:::statute
Article 6, IMT Charter: "The following acts […] are crimes coming within the jurisdiction of the Tribunal for which
there shall be individual responsibility: (A) Crimes against peace; (B) War crimes; (C) Crimes against humanity."
:::

- **Crimes against peace** — planning, preparation, initiation or waging of a war of aggression, or a war in violation
  of international treaties, agreements or assurances, or participation in a common plan or conspiracy for the
  accomplishment of any of the foregoing.
- **War crimes** — violations of the laws or customs of war, including, but not limited to, murder, ill-treatment or
  deportation to slave labour or for any other purpose of civilian population of or in occupied territory, murder or
  ill-treatment of prisoners of war or persons on the seas, killing of hostages, plunder of public or private
  property, wanton destruction of cities, towns or villages, or devastation not justified by military necessity.
- **Crimes against humanity** — murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation, and other inhumane acts committed
  against any civilian population, before or during the war; or persecutions on political, racial or religious
  grounds in execution of or in connection with any crime within the jurisdiction of the Tribunal, whether or not in
  violation of the domestic law of the country where perpetrated.

## The Nuremberg Principles

The UN General Assembly affirmed the "Principles of International Law Recognized in the Charter of the Nürnberg
Tribunal and in the Judgment of the Tribunal" in Resolution 95(I) (11 December 1946).

:::rule
Principle I: "Any person who commits an act which constitutes a crime under international law is responsible therefor
and liable to punishment."
:::

- Principle II: "The fact that internal law does not impose a penalty for an act which constitutes a crime under
  international law does not relieve the person who committed the act from responsibility under international law."
- Principle III: "The fact that a person who committed an act which constitutes a crime under international law acted
  as Head of State or responsible Government official does not relieve him from responsibility under international
  law."
- Principle IV: "The fact that a person acted pursuant to order of his Government or of a superior does not relieve
  him from responsibility under international law, provided a moral choice was in fact possible to him."

## The International Military Tribunal for the Far East and other post-WWII tribunals

The IMTFE was established by proclamation by US General Douglas MacArthur, in implementation of the Potsdam
Declaration, to try Japanese officials involved with perpetuating World War II. 28 defendants were charged with 55
counts: crimes against peace (art 5(a), IMTFE Charter), conventional war crimes (art 5(b)) and crimes against humanity
(art 5(c)). All defendants were found guilty, though not on all counts charged; there were seven death sentences, and
the others were sentenced to imprisonment. The IMTFE largely agreed with the Nuremberg IMT on the application of the
law.

Control Council Law No 10 provided for domestic prosecutions in the Allied powers' zones of occupation. The most well
known are the twelve trials conducted in the US zone of occupation by the Nuremberg Military Tribunals (NMTs). The
Allied powers also conducted thousands of trials in the Asia-Pacific region and beyond — China, the United States, the
Netherlands, the United Kingdom, Australia, the Philippines and France.

## The ad hoc tribunals: the ICTY

The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia was established by UN Security Council Resolution 827
(25 May 1993), acting under Chapter VII of the UN Charter, entitled "Action with respect to Threats to the Peace,
Breaches of the Peace, and Acts of Aggression".

:::rule
Resolution 827: "[A]ll States shall cooperate fully with the International Tribunal and its organs in accordance with
the present resolution and the Statute of the International Tribunal and that consequently all States shall take any
measures necessary under their domestic law to implement the provisions of the present resolution and the Statute,
including the obligation of States to comply with requests for assistance or orders issued by a Trial Chamber under
Article 29 of the Statute".
:::

:::statute
Article 1, ICTY Statute: "The International Tribunal shall have the power to prosecute persons responsible for serious
violations of international humanitarian law committed in the territory of the former Yugoslavia since 1991 in
accordance with the provisions of the present Statute."
:::

161 individuals were charged (with 90 convictions and 19 acquittals), which demonstrates the possibility of
conducting international(ised) criminal prosecutions. In 2010 the International Residual Mechanism for Criminal
Tribunals (MICT) was established, and the ICTY closed on 31 December 2017. It undoubtedly contributed to the
development of ICL, the historical record and combating denial. Criticisms relate to cost, delays, lack of victim
participation, and distance from affected communities.

Milestones: in 1995 the *Tadić* Interlocutory Appeal Decision addressed the legality of the creation of the ICTY; in
1999 Slobodan Milošević, then President of the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia, was indicted.

## The ad hoc tribunals: the ICTR

The International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda was established by a UN Security Council resolution of 8 November 1994,
under Chapter VII of the UN Charter (the slide gives "Resolution 855"; the number is flagged to verify against the
textbook, Rose et al, Ch 13). Its headquarters were in Arusha, Tanzania (Security Council Resolution 977).

:::statute
Article 1, ICTR Statute: "The International Tribunal for Rwanda shall have the power to prosecute persons responsible
for serious violations of international humanitarian law committed in the territory of Rwanda and Rwandan citizens
responsible for such violations committed in the territory of neighbouring States between 1 January 1994 and 31
December 1994, in accordance with the provisions of the present Statute." Article 2: Genocide. Article 3: Crimes
against humanity. Article 4: Violations of Article 3 Common to the Geneva Conventions and of Additional Protocol II.
:::

Milestones: in May 1998 Jean Kambanda pleaded guilty to genocide; in September 1998 Jean-Paul Akayesu was convicted of
genocide — in that case, sexual violence was found to constitute the actus reus. The Residual Mechanism (MICT) was
established in 2010, and in December 2015 appellate work was completed and the ICTR closed.

The ICTR succeeded in arresting and convicting (high-profile) accused and in establishing that genocide had occurred,
and contributed to the development of ICL, particularly in terms of sexual offences and incitement. Criticisms relate
to delays, treatment of victims, selective prosecutions, and distance from affected communities.

:::examtip
Tutorial questions 1 and 2: the ICTY and ICTR were created by Security Council resolutions under Chapter VII, not by
treaty. That is why all states were mandated to cooperate: under art 25 of the UN Charter, "the Members of the United
Nations agree to accept and carry out the decisions of the Security Council". The ICC, by contrast, rests on a treaty,
the Rome Statute, which binds its States Parties.
:::

### Classification

The ad hoc ICTY and ICTR were located far from the countries they served. A need was recognised for other tribunals
to address serious crimes committed in other parts of the world. Treaty-based, 'hybrid' courts with both national and
international elements were proposed to help create efficient, more locally based courts to address serious
international crimes.

:::definition
Hybrid or internationalised criminal tribunals are part national and part international in nature. This can be
discerned from (1) the way they were established (e.g. agreement between the host state and the UN); (2) their
subject-matter jurisdiction (both international crimes and national crimes); and (3) their staff (generally,
international and national judges and practitioners are employed in hybrid, internationalised or mixed criminal
tribunals).
:::

### The Special Court for Sierra Leone

In an attempt to promote justice and end impunity for the atrocities committed by warring factions in Sierra Leone
during its civil war, the UN and the Government of Sierra Leone jointly established the SCSL in 2002. Its mandate was
to try "persons who bear the greatest responsibility for serious violations of international humanitarian law and
Sierra Leonean law committed in the territory of Sierra Leone since 30 November 1996".

The SCSL was not a part of the Sierra Leonean legal system, but did include some aspects of Sierra Leonean law
(although none of these laws were used by the SCSL). It employed Sierra Leoneans as staff members and had its
permanent seat in Freetown, Sierra Leone. Not a UN body (unlike the ICTY and ICTR), it had jurisdiction over
international crimes, and employed international staff, including a majority of international judges.

**Jurisdiction.** Crimes against humanity; violations of Article 3 common to the Geneva Conventions and of Additional
Protocol II; other serious violations of international humanitarian law; and certain crimes under Sierra Leonean law.

**Milestones.** The SCSL convicted nine persons, imposing sentences of terms of imprisonment ranging from 15 to 52
years. It was the first international(ised) criminal tribunal to try and convict persons for the use of child
soldiers (AFRC trial), for forced marriage (an other inhumane act) as a crime against humanity (RUF trial), and for
attacks directed against UN peacekeepers (RUF trial). In the slides' words, it was "the first modern ICT to indict,
try, and convict a sitting head of state" (the *Taylor* trial).

### The Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia

**Establishment.** The ECCC were established to try individuals responsible for serious crimes under the Khmer Rouge
regime from 1975 to 1979 — addressing Khmer Rouge atrocities. After a request made by Cambodia to the UN, the ECCC were
established through an international agreement in 2004, following lengthy negotiations (the UN–Cambodia Agreement).
They sat within the Cambodian court structure but received international assistance through the United Nations
Assistance to the Khmer Rouge Trials (UNAKRT).

**Jurisdiction.** Crimes committed between 17 April 1975 and 6 January 1979: (1) genocide under the 1948 Genocide
Convention; (2) crimes against humanity as per the Rome Statute; (3) grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions; and (4)
crimes under Cambodian law. The ECCC investigated and prosecuted (1) senior leaders of Democratic Kampuchea and (2)
those most responsible for crimes and serious violations of Cambodian and international law committed from 17 April
1975 to 6 January 1979.

**Distinctive features.** Part of the Cambodian judiciary, applying national and international law; two
Co-Prosecutors, one national and one international; a majority of national judges in all Chambers; a structure more
akin to civil law, reflecting Cambodia's legal system; Co-Investigating Judges responsible for investigations, not
Prosecutors; civil parties had a right to participate in proceedings; and located within Cambodia's domestic legal
system.

### The Special Tribunal for Lebanon

**Establishment.** In 2005, the Government of Lebanon requested the UN to establish a tribunal to try those
responsible for the 14 February 2005 attack that killed Prime Minister Rafic Hariri and 22 others. The STL Statute,
annexed to UN Security Council Resolution 1757, entered into force on 10 June 2007.

**Jurisdiction.** The STL Statute stipulated that the STL shall apply provisions of the Lebanese Criminal Code
relating to terrorism, among other crimes.

**International character.** The international character of the STL was explicitly stipulated in the request
submitted by the Government of Lebanon to the UN Secretary-General. The UN and Lebanon agreed that the STL would have
a mixed composition, with both Lebanese and international judges, as well as an international prosecutor.

:::examtip
To show that a tribunal is hybrid, run the three markers from the classification slide — establishment,
subject-matter jurisdiction, staff. SCSL: established jointly by the UN and Sierra Leone; international crimes plus
certain Sierra Leonean crimes; Sierra Leonean staff with a majority of international judges. ECCC: a UN–Cambodia
agreement; international crimes plus crimes under Cambodian law; a majority of national judges. STL: Lebanon's
request and a Statute annexed to Resolution 1757; the Lebanese Criminal Code; Lebanese and international judges and
an international prosecutor.

### Establishment

In 1948, when the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide was adopted, the UN General
Assembly recognised the need for a permanent international court to deal with the kinds of atrocities which had just
been perpetrated. The idea of a system of international criminal justice re-emerged after the end of the Cold War. On
17 July 1998, in Rome, a conference of 160 states established the first treaty-based permanent international criminal
court.

Among other things, the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court sets out the crimes falling within the
Court's jurisdiction, certain rules of procedure, and the mechanisms for states to cooperate with the ICC. 125
countries are States Parties to the Rome Statute, representing all regions: Africa (33 states), Asia Pacific (19),
Eastern Europe (20), Latin America and the Caribbean (28), and Western Europe and North America (25). The slide then
asks: any notable exceptions?

### A case from start to finish

1. **Preliminary examinations.** The Office of the Prosecutor must determine whether there is sufficient evidence of
   crimes of sufficient gravity falling within the ICC's jurisdiction; whether there are genuine national
   proceedings; and whether opening an investigation would serve the interests of justice and victims.
2. **Investigations.** After gathering evidence and identifying a suspect, the Prosecution requests ICC judges to
   issue an arrest warrant (the ICC relies on countries to make arrests and transfer suspects to the ICC) or a
   summons to appear (suspects appear voluntarily; if not, an arrest warrant may be issued).
3. **Pre-trial stage.** At the initial appearance, three Pre-Trial judges confirm the suspect's identity and ensure
   the suspect understands the charges. At the confirmation of charges hearings, after hearing the Prosecution, the
   Defence and the legal representative of victims, the judges decide (usually within 60 days) if there is enough
   evidence for the case to go to trial.
4. **Trial stage.** Before three Trial judges, the Prosecution must prove beyond reasonable doubt the guilt of the
   accused. Judges consider all evidence, then issue a verdict and, when guilty, a sentence — up to 30 years, and
   under exceptional circumstances, life. Judges can also order reparations for the victims.
5. **Appeals stage.** Both the Prosecutor and the Defence have the right to appeal a Trial Chamber's decision on the
   verdict (the decision on guilt or innocence of the accused) and the sentence.
6. **Enforcement of sentence.** Sentences are served in countries that have agreed to enforce ICC sentences.

### Jurisdiction and admissibility: the questions in order

The slides set the ICC analysis out as a flowchart: subject-matter jurisdiction met? (art 5) → temporal jurisdiction
met? (art 11) → situation referred? (art 13) → preconditions met? (art 12) → issues of admissibility? (art 17) →
proceed to the ICC.

:::statute
Article 5, Rome Statute — Crimes within the jurisdiction of the Court: "The jurisdiction of the Court shall be limited
to the most serious crimes of concern to the international community as a whole. The Court has jurisdiction in
accordance with this Statute with respect to the following crimes: (a) The crime of genocide; (b) Crimes against
humanity; (c) War crimes; (d) The crime of aggression." Article 6 Genocide; Article 7 Crimes against humanity;
Article 8 War crimes; Article 8 bis Crime of aggression.
:::

**Example: genocide (art 6).** For the purpose of the Statute, "genocide" means any of the following acts committed
with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such: (a) killing
members of the group; (b) causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group; (c) deliberately inflicting
on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part; (d) imposing
measures intended to prevent births within the group; (e) forcibly transferring children of the group to another
group. Key element: acts committed with intent to destroy a protected group.

**Example: aggression (art 8 bis).** "Crime of aggression" means "the planning, preparation, initiation or execution,
by a person in a position effectively to exercise control over or to direct the political or military action of a
State, of an act of aggression which, by its character, gravity and scale, constitutes a manifest violation of the
Charter of the United Nations" (art 8 bis(1)). "Act of aggression" means "the use of armed force by a State against
the sovereignty, territorial integrity or political independence of another State, or in any other manner
inconsistent with the Charter of the United Nations"; the acts then listed qualify as acts of aggression regardless of
a declaration of war, in accordance with General Assembly resolution 3314 (XXIX) of 14 December 1974 (art 8 bis(2);
the list itself is not on the slide). Key element: control over political or military action of a State.

## ICC jurisdiction: time, triggers and preconditions

**Article 11 — jurisdiction ratione temporis.** The Court has jurisdiction only with respect to crimes committed after
the entry into force of the Statute. If a state becomes a Party after its entry into force, the Court may exercise its
jurisdiction only with respect to crimes committed after the entry into force of the Statute for that state, unless
that state has made a declaration under art 12(3).

**Article 13 — exercise of jurisdiction.** The Court may exercise its jurisdiction with respect to an art 5 crime if
(a) a situation in which one or more of such crimes appears to have been committed is referred to the Prosecutor by a
State Party in accordance with art 14; (b) such a situation is referred to the Prosecutor by the Security Council
acting under Chapter VII of the Charter of the United Nations (the slides' example is the Security Council referral of
Libya in 2011); or (c) the Prosecutor has initiated an investigation in respect of such a crime in accordance with art
15 (the Prosecutor's proprio motu power).

### Triggers in practice: Libya and the Philippines

**Security Council referral: Libya (2011).** The slides headline Resolution 1970 (2011), adopted by the Security
Council at its 6491st meeting on 26 February 2011, as the "UNSC Chapter VII referral of Libya to the ICC". The
Council decided to refer the situation in the Libyan Arab Jamahiriya since 15 February 2011 to the Prosecutor of the
ICC (para 4); decided that the Libyan authorities shall cooperate fully with and provide any necessary assistance to
the Court and the Prosecutor and, while recognising that states not party to the Rome Statute have no obligation under
the Statute, urged all states and concerned regional and other international … (the rest of para 5 is cut off on the
slide); decided that nationals, current or former officials or personnel from a state outside the Libyan Arab
Jamahiriya which is not a party to the Rome Statute shall be subject to the exclusive jurisdiction of that state for
all alleged acts or omissions arising out of or related to operations in the Libyan … (cut off; para 6); invited the
Prosecutor to address the Security Council within two months of the adoption of the resolution and every six … (cut
off; para 7); and recognised that none of the expenses incurred in connection with the referral shall be borne by the
United Nations (para 8).

**The Prosecutor's proprio motu power: the Philippines.** On 24 May 2021 the Prosecutor requested an investigation of
crimes committed during the 'war on drugs' campaign; on 15 September 2021 Pre-Trial Chamber I authorised the
Prosecutor to commence the investigation. On 10 November 2021 the Philippines requested deferral of the investigation,
claiming national proceedings were underway; in January 2023 the Pre-Trial Chamber granted the Prosecutor's request
to resume, after finding the national proceedings insufficient in scope. On 10 February 2025 the Prosecutor applied
for an arrest warrant against Mr Duterte; the warrant, for crimes against humanity, was issued as "secret" on 7 March
2025 and reclassified as "public" on 11 March. On 12 March 2025 Mr Duterte was arrested by Philippines authorities and
surrendered to the ICC.

### Preconditions: article 12

**Article 12 — preconditions to the exercise of jurisdiction.** A state which becomes a Party thereby accepts the
jurisdiction of the Court with respect to the art 5 crimes. In the case of art 13(a) or (c), the Court may exercise
its jurisdiction if one or more of the following states are Parties or have accepted the Court's jurisdiction under
art 12(3): (a) the state on the territory of which the conduct in question occurred or, if the crime was committed on
board a vessel or aircraft, the state of registration of that vessel or aircraft; (b) the state of which the person
accused of the crime is a national. A non-party whose acceptance is required may, by declaration lodged with the
Registrar, accept the exercise of jurisdiction with respect to the crime in question; the accepting state shall
cooperate with the Court without any delay or exception in accordance with Part 9.

**Accepting jurisdiction, and referring a situation: Ukraine.** The slides show two letters. In the first (9 April
2014), the Embassy of Ukraine informed the Registrar that, "in conformity with Article 12, paragraph 3" of the
Statute, Ukraine recognised the jurisdiction of the Court for the purpose of identifying, prosecuting and judging the
authors and accomplices of acts committed on the territory of Ukraine within the period 21 November 2013 – 22 February
2014, on the basis of a Declaration of the Verkhovna Rada (the Parliament of Ukraine) in force from 25 February 2014
for an indeterminate duration; as a consequence, Ukraine "will cooperate with the Court without delay or exception, in
conformity with Chapter IX of the Rome Statute". In the second (2 March 2022), the UK Ambassador, "acting under
article 13(a) and 14(1)" and in coordination with a group of States Parties (among them the Commonwealth of
Australia), notified the Prosecutor of a referral of the situation in Ukraine.

**Preconditions and exercise.** The slides' diagram links the precondition — territoriality or nationality — to (1) a
State Party referral and (3) a proprio motu investigation, but not to (2) a Security Council referral. A State Party
referral and a Security Council referral lead straight to an investigation; a proprio motu investigation needs
authorisation by the Pre-Trial Chamber first.

for a State Party referral or a proprio motu investigation, is the territorial (or flag) state or the accused's
nationality state a party, or has it accepted jurisdiction under art 12(3)? A Security Council referral needs no such

## Admissibility: complementarity

:::rule
Article 17: having regard to paragraph 10 of the Preamble and article 1, the Court shall determine that a case is
inadmissible where (a) the case is being investigated or prosecuted by a state which has jurisdiction over it, unless
the state is unwilling or unable genuinely to carry out the investigation or prosecution; (b) the case has been
investigated by a state which has jurisdiction over it and the state has decided not to prosecute the person
concerned, unless the decision resulted from the unwillingness or inability of the state genuinely to prosecute; (c)
the person concerned has already been tried for conduct which is the subject of the complaint, and a trial by the
Court is not permitted under art 20(3); (d) the case is not of sufficient gravity to justify further action by the
Court.
:::

The ICC is accordingly "complementary to national criminal jurisdictions" (art 1, ICC Statute).

**Unwillingness.** To determine unwillingness in a particular case, the Court shall consider, having regard to the
principles of due process recognised by international law, whether one or more of the following exist: the
proceedings were or are being undertaken, or the national decision was made, for the purpose of shielding the person
concerned from criminal responsibility for art 5 crimes; there has been an unjustified delay in the proceedings which
in the circumstances is inconsistent with an intent to bring the person concerned to justice; the proceedings were not
or are not being conducted independently or impartially, and in a manner which, in the circumstances, is
inconsistent with an intent to bring the person concerned to justice.

**Inability.** To determine inability in a particular case, the Court shall consider whether, due to a total or
substantial collapse or unavailability of its national judicial system, the state is unable to obtain the accused or
the necessary evidence and testimony or otherwise unable to carry out its proceedings.

## Applicable law and cooperation

:::statute
Article 21 — Applicable law. The Court shall apply: (a) in the first place, this Statute, Elements of Crimes and its
Rules of Procedure and Evidence; (b) in the second place, where appropriate, applicable treaties and the principles
and rules of international law, including the established principles of the international law of armed conflict; (c)
failing that, general principles of law derived by the Court from national laws of legal systems of the world
including, as appropriate, the national laws of states that would normally exercise jurisdiction over the crime,
provided that those principles are not inconsistent with this Statute and with international law and internationally
recognized norms and standards.
:::

The Court may apply principles and rules of law as interpreted in its previous decisions. The application and
interpretation of law under art 21 must be consistent with internationally recognised human rights, and be without
any adverse distinction founded on grounds such as gender, age, race, colour, language, religion or belief, political
or other opinion, national, ethnic or social origin, wealth, birth or other status.

**Cooperation.** Article 86 — general obligation to cooperate: "States Parties shall, in accordance with the
provisions of this Statute, cooperate fully with the Court in its investigation and prosecution of crimes within the
jurisdiction of the Court." Article 88 — availability of procedures under national law: "States Parties shall ensure
that there are procedures available under their national law for all of the forms of cooperation which are specified
under this Part." Article 25 of the Charter of the United Nations: "The Members of the United Nations agree to accept
and carry out the decisions of the Security Council in accordance with the present Charter."

:::examtip
Tutorial question 3: a non-party is not bound by the Rome Statute as such. On this week's materials it comes under a
duty to cooperate if it lodges an art 12(3) declaration (it must then cooperate "without any delay or exception"), or
as a UN member bound under art 25 of the Charter by a Security Council decision taken under Chapter VII.
