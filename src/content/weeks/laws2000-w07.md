## Overview

Part 1 — constituent elements for state responsibility: (a) wrongful conduct; (b) state attribution. Part 2 —
grounds to preclude wrongfulness; consequences of the breach (cessation and reparation); and who may invoke a
state's international responsibility (injured states and non-injured states).

## What state responsibility is

Rules of "state responsibility" indicate the circumstances in which a state's responsibility for the violation of
an international obligation is triggered, and the consequences this entails, including how other states may
respond to this. State responsibility is a term that has a legal meaning: it's not like accountability, it's not
like a duty — it means that there has been a violation of international law, and more specifically that two
elements have been met.

:::definition
Rules of state responsibility are of a secondary nature. Primary rules (found in treaty law or customary
international law) determine the substantive legal obligations (e.g. non-use of force). Rules of state
responsibility determine the legal consequences arising from a breach of those obligations.
:::

The general regime of state responsibility concerns states only, and it is not a specialised regime (lex
specialis) — for instance, the rules of responsibility under the World Trade Organization and trade law. There is
almost the same set of draft articles that apply to international organisations: they basically almost copied
and pasted what was established for states.

## The law on state responsibility: ARSIWA

The law on state responsibility was studied and developed by the International Law Commission ("ILC") over more
than 40 years. It is now codified and developed in the ILC's Articles on Responsibility of States for
Internationally Wrongful Acts, adopted on 10 August 2001 ("ARSIWA"). The 2001 ILC Articles provide the practical
and conceptual structure within which issues of state responsibility can be examined.

:::examtip
ARSIWA is not binding — it was adopted by commissioners, not by states, so it is not a treaty — but many articles
appear to codify customary international law, and the ICJ has on many occasions found that some of its rules are
rules of customary international law. The draft articles with commentaries (article in bold, then the
commissioners' commentary) are a very helpful guide, especially for art 8.
:::

## Internationally wrongful conduct

:::rule
"Every internationally wrongful act of a State entails its international responsibility" (art 1
ARSIWA). An internationally wrongful act exists when conduct consisting of an act or omission is attributable to
a State and constitutes a breach of an international obligation owed by that State (art 2 ARSIWA).
:::

Internationally wrongful act is an overarching term: it covers criminal acts but also torts — there is no
distinction under international law as there is in the domestic system. If you have a breach, prima facie you may
have a wrongful act, but you still have to demonstrate that it is a state that has breached international law
with its conduct in that specific case.

## Element 1: conduct in breach of an international obligation

- Breach is synonymous with violation.
- It concerns a primary rule of international law irrespective of the source (art 12 ARSIWA): custom or treaty,
  it doesn't matter — but it must be an international obligation, not a domestic one. Non-compliance with a
  decision of the International Court of Justice could also engage a state's responsibility.
- The state needs to be under the obligation at the time of the breach (arts 13–14 ARSIWA; *Legal Consequences
  of the Separation of the Chagos Archipelago* [2019] ICJ Advisory Opinion). There is no retroactivity.
- Composite acts (art 15 ARSIWA): one or two specific actions may not amount to a breach, but taken together they
  eventually will — for example, a level of pollution that adds up over time.
- Act or omission (art 2 ARSIWA; *Tehran* case, ICJ; *Corfu Channel*, ICJ).

:::case
*Tehran* (US Embassy hostages) — protesters stormed the US embassy in Tehran and took hostages. Under the Vienna
Convention on Diplomatic Relations the host state has an obligation to protect the embassy; Iran's failure to
protect the embassy and its staff was an omission amounting to an internationally wrongful act.
:::

## Attribution: the general principle and Corfu Channel

Articles 4–11, chapter III of ARSIWA, deal with attribution. In principle, states are only responsible for their
own actions, not for those of private individuals. However, under certain circumstances, a state can be found to
be in breach of its international obligations in relation to actions of private individuals — e.g. *Corfu
Channel Case (UK v Albania)* [1949] ICJ, p 22: a state may not "allow knowingly its territory to be used for acts
contrary to the rights of other States".

:::case
*Corfu Channel (United Kingdom v Albania)*, ICJ, 1949 — in October 1946 two British cruisers and two destroyers
entered the North Corfu Strait in Albanian waters. The passage was regarded as safe (swept in 1944 and
check-swept in 1945), but two ships struck mines: 45 British officers and sailors died and 42 were wounded. Who
laid the mines remained a matter for conjecture. Evidence of the Albanian Government's attitude, and the fact that
the minelaying would have been visible from the Albanian coast, led the Court to conclude that Albania knew of the
minefield. Albania's obligations consisted in notifying shipping of the minefield and warning the approaching
British warships of the imminent danger — another example of a breach by omission.
:::

## Attribution: organs and governmental authority (arts 4–7)

:::rule
Art 4 ARSIWA: the conduct of any State organ shall be considered an act of that State under international law,
whether the organ exercises legislative, executive, judicial or any other functions, whatever position it holds
in the organisation of the State, and whatever its character as an organ of the central Government or of a
territorial unit of the State. An organ includes any person or entity which has that status in accordance with
the internal law of the State.
:::

The state works through its organs — legislative, executive and judiciary — and this includes municipalities,
courts, and the states within Australia. Usually the internal law (often the constitution) shows whether a body is
an organ. But in the *Application of the Genocide Convention (Bosnia)* case, ICJ 2007 (paras 392–393), persons,
groups or entities may be equated with state organs even if that status does not follow from internal law,
provided that in fact they act in "complete dependence" on the state, of which they are ultimately merely the
instrument — otherwise states could escape responsibility by acting through entities whose supposed independence
is purely fictitious. So to equate them with state organs "must be exceptional" (de facto organs).

**Art 5 — entities exercising governmental authority.** Conduct of a person or entity which is not an organ under
art 4 but which is (i) empowered by the law of that state to exercise elements of the governmental authority
shall be considered an act of the state, (ii) provided it is acting in that capacity in the particular instance.
This applies where state functions have been delegated to private entities — e.g. privatisation of military and
police functions, prisons, border management, airlines doing border control functions.

**Art 6 — organs placed at the disposal of a state by another state.** Their conduct is an act of the former
state if the organ is acting in the exercise of elements of the governmental authority of the state at whose
disposal it is placed — e.g. sending a health service section to another state to fight a disease outbreak, or
New Zealand police placed under the control of the Australian government.

**Art 7 — ultra vires acts.** The conduct of an organ, or of a person or entity empowered to exercise elements of
governmental authority, is an act of the state if it acts in that capacity, "even if it exceeds its authority or
contravenes instructions". The rationale is that the state should be able to monitor and control its organs.
*Velásquez Rodríguez v Honduras*, IACtHR, 1988, para 170: a state is responsible for the acts of its agents
undertaken in their official capacity and for their omissions, even when those agents act outside the sphere of
their authority or violate internal law.

## Attribution: private individuals and the effective control test (art 8)

:::rule
Art 8 ARSIWA: the conduct of a person or group of persons shall be considered an act of a State if the person or
group of persons is in fact acting on the instructions of, or under the direction or control of, that State in
carrying out the conduct.
:::

Two situations. Under the instructions — with respect to each operation (*Nicaragua* and *Application of the
Genocide Convention* cases, ICJ): the instructions must be very specific, not general and vague, and relate to
every action in violation of international law. Under the direction or control — effective control over each
operation; originally two separate items, now assessed together by the courts. The question came to the fore
after 9/11: could Afghanistan be held responsible for the actions of Al-Qaeda?

:::case
*Military and Paramilitary Activities in and against Nicaragua (Nicaragua v United States)*, Merits, ICJ Reports
1986 — United States participation, "even if preponderant or decisive", in the financing, organising, training,
supplying and equipping of the contras, the selection of targets, and the planning of the whole operation, was
still insufficient in itself to attribute the contras' acts to the United States. It "would in principle have to
be proved that that State had effective control of the military or paramilitary operations in the course of which
the alleged violations were committed" (para 115). A very high threshold.
:::

:::case
*The Prosecutor v Duško Tadić*, IT-94-1-A (15 July 1999) (ICTY Appeals Chamber) — the degree of control "may
vary according to the factual circumstances of each case"; the Appeals Chamber "fails to see why in each and every
circumstance international law should require a high threshold for the test of control" (para 117). It developed
the less demanding overall control test, appropriate in areas such as international humanitarian law and
international criminal law.
:::

:::case
*Application of the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (Bosnia and Herzegovina
v Serbia and Montenegro)*, ICJ 2007 — the "overall control" test "has the major drawback of broadening the scope
of State responsibility well beyond the fundamental principle … a State is responsible only for its own conduct";
it "stretches too far, almost to breaking point, the connection which must exist between the conduct of a State's
organs and its international responsibility". Effective control, under the customary rule reflected in art 8, is
the law as it stands now.
:::

## Attribution: insurrections, adoption and other states (arts 10, 11, 16–18)

**Art 10 — mob violence, insurrections and civil war.** The conduct of an insurrectional movement which becomes
the new Government of a State shall be considered an act of that State; the conduct of a movement which succeeds
in establishing a new State in part of the territory of a pre-existing State is an act of the new State. This is
without prejudice to attribution under arts 4–9. If the state has acted in good faith, it shouldn't be held
accountable for the actions of insurgents seeking to undermine the government.

**Art 11 — endorsement of conduct.** Conduct not attributable under the preceding articles shall nevertheless be
considered an act of the State "if and to the extent that the State acknowledges and adopts the conduct in
question as its own". In *Tehran*, the government had not stormed the embassy, but afterwards issued a decree
supporting what the students had done, endorsed it in public statements and supported the maintenance of the
hostage situation — so the students' conduct was attributed to Iran under art 11.

**Acts by another state.** A state aids or assists another state in breaching an obligation owed by both of them
(art 16) — responsible only for the portion of the help it gave; a state exercises direction or control over
another state (art 17) — mostly historical, e.g. protectorates; a state coerces another state into breaching an
obligation (art 18) — the coercing state is held responsible.

## Circumstances precluding wrongfulness (arts 20–25)

International law accepts that there are circumstances where a state will breach its international obligation,
and the breach is attributable to it, but because of the circumstances surrounding that breach it won't be held
responsible. In effect, they act like a defence or an excuse — a little like defences in national criminal law.
The six: consent, self-defence, lawful countermeasures, force majeure, distress, necessity.

**Consent (art 20).** Valid consent by a State to the commission of a given act by another State precludes the
wrongfulness of that act in relation to the former State to the extent that the act remains within the limits of
that consent. Consent must be valid, given freely (without coercion), clearly expressed before or at the time of
the act, with no ambiguity — e.g. aircraft entering foreign airspace, rescue at sea. Reliance on consent to breach
the prohibition on the use of force is unsettled and quite controversial.

**Self-defence (art 21).** Wrongfulness is precluded if the act constitutes a lawful measure of self-defence taken
in conformity with the Charter of the United Nations — i.e. art 51: "Nothing in the present Charter shall impair
the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs against a Member of the
United Nations…". Art 21 must be read with art 51: you need to prove an armed attack before acting in
self-defence.

**Lawful countermeasures (arts 22 and 49–59).** Wrongfulness is precluded if and to the extent that the act
constitutes a countermeasure taken against the responsible state in accordance with chapter II of part three. An
injured state may only take countermeasures against a state responsible for an internationally wrongful act in
order to induce that state to comply with its obligations (art 49(1)). Countermeasures must not breach jus cogens
obligations (e.g. use of force, human rights) (arts 50, 53); must be proportional, determined case by case (art
51); and must meet procedural requirements — first ask to negotiate, notify, give a chance to cease, terminate
once the wrongful act has ceased (arts 52, 53). Otherwise they could be deemed reprisals, which do not preclude
wrongfulness.

:::case
*Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros Project (Hungary v Slovakia)* [1997] ICJ — Hungary and Czechoslovakia agreed by treaty to
build a joint system of locks on the Danube. In 1989 Hungary stopped work over ecological concerns; Czechoslovakia
developed an alternative that diverted the Danube into its territory and, from October 1992, dammed the river.
Hungary's refusal to complete the project was a breach of the treaty, but by unilaterally assuming control of a
shared resource Czechoslovakia deprived Hungary of its equitable share, so the diversion was not a lawful
countermeasure because it was disproportionate.
:::

## Force majeure, distress, necessity and peremptory norms (arts 23–26)

**Force majeure (art 23(1)).** "The occurrence of an irresistible force or of an unforeseen event, beyond the
control of the State, making it materially impossible in the circumstances to perform the obligation."
Involuntary, unintentional conduct beyond the state's control: no one should be legally required to perform the
impossible — e.g. a catastrophic tsunami damaging state-owned offshore nuclear facilities. It will not stand if
the invoking state contributed to the situation or assumed the risk. The standard is very high: in the *Serbian
loans* case the PCIJ refused to accept that the First World War made it impossible to repay a debt, and the
*Rainbow Warrior* arbitration (France–New Zealand, 82 ILR 500 (1990)) required "absolute and material
impossibility".

**Distress (art 24).** Wrongfulness is precluded if the author of the act "has no other reasonable way, in a
situation of distress, of saving the author's life or the lives of other persons entrusted to the author's care"
— historically ships or aircraft in distress crossing a frontier because of bad weather. It does not apply if the
distress is due, alone or in combination with other factors, to the conduct of the state invoking it, or if the
act is likely to create a comparable or greater peril.

**Necessity (art 25).** Necessity "may not be invoked … unless the act: (a) is the only way for the State to
safeguard an essential interest against a grave and imminent peril; and (b) does not seriously impair an
essential interest of the State or States towards which the obligation exists, or of the international community
as a whole." It is written in the negative to emphasise its exceptional character; it relates to rare and truly
exceptional situations (*Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros* paras 49–55: essential interest, grave and imminent peril, only
way, no serious impairment of another's essential interest, no contribution by the invoking state). In the *Wall*
advisory opinion the construction was not necessary: there were other means to protect Israel's interests.

:::rule
Art 26 ARSIWA: "Nothing in this chapter precludes the wrongfulness of any act of a State which is not in
conformity with an obligation arising under a peremptory norm of general international law" — no state can
commit genocide as "counter-genocide", or claim it was necessary.
:::

## Consequences of an internationally wrongful act

**Cessation and non-repetition (art 30).** The responsible state is under an obligation (a) to cease that act, if
it is continuing; (b) to offer appropriate assurances and guarantees of non-repetition, if circumstances so
require — see *Construction of a Wall* (ICJ Advisory Opinion): Israel "must offer appropriate assurances and
guarantees of non-repetition".

**Reparation (art 31).** "The responsible State is under an obligation to make full reparation for the injury
caused by the internationally wrongful act. Injury includes any damage, whether material or moral." *Factory at
Chorzów*, 1928 PCIJ: reparation must "as far as possible, wipe all the consequences of the illegal act and
re-establish the situation which would, in all probability, have existed if that act had not been committed".

Three forms of reparation: restitution (art 35) — in kind, e.g. the return of property; compensation (art 36) —
usually a monetary sum where restitution is not possible, covering only material damage that is financially
assessable; and satisfaction (art 37) — for moral damage: an apology, an expression of regret, or a court's
judgment declaring the conduct wrongful.

:::case
*Construction of the Wall* Advisory Opinion (2004) — the ICJ found Israel's construction of a wall in the Occupied
Palestinian Territory an internationally unlawful act; Israel was obliged to stop construction, dismantle portions
already built, and provide reparations for damage caused. *Chagos* Advisory Opinion (2019) — the UK's detachment
of the Chagos islands from Mauritius and continued administration was an internationally unlawful act; the UK was
obliged to end its administration and return them to Mauritius as rapidly as possible.
:::

## Who may invoke a state's international responsibility?

**The injured state(s) (art 42).** The rule is that only the state(s) whose individual rights have been violated
are entitled to invoke international responsibility (demand cessation, reparation, etc.) — a state is injured if
the obligation breached was owed to it, for instance under a bilateral or multilateral treaty.

**Non-injured states (art 48).** If an erga omnes norm has been violated (e.g. the non-use of force principle,
prohibition of aggression and genocide, and breaches of international environmental law), any state other than an
injured state may invoke responsibility if (a) the obligation breached is owed to a group of states including
that state and is established for the protection of a collective interest of the group (e.g. a regional
nuclear-free zone treaty or regional human rights system); or (b) the obligation breached is owed to the
international community as a whole — *Barcelona Traction (Belgium v Spain)* ICJ (1970): obligations erga omnes.

## Putting it together: five steps

1. Primary legal obligation? (treaty law, customary law…)
2. Based on the given facts, prima facie breach of a primary legal obligation?
3. Act attributable to a state?
4. Any possible grounds precluding wrongfulness?
5. Then: internationally wrongful conduct — legal consequences: cease, reparation…

:::examtip
The tutorial's pandemic Convention hypothetical (Company X in Armadonia distributing vaccines in priority to Y and
Z) asks exactly these questions, plus whether Australia can invoke responsibility — so step through primary
obligation, breach, attribution (is Company X an organ, art 5 entity or art 8 actor?), preclusion, invocation and
consequences.
:::
