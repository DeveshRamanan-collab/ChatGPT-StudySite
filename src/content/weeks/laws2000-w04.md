## Subjects of international law

"Public international law consists of rules and principles which facilitate the conduct of states and
international organisations in their relations with one another and, in some cases, with individuals, groups
and transnational companies" (Office of International Law, Attorney-General's Department). So international
law is primarily about states, but there are also other actors.

:::definition
"A subject of international law is an entity possessing international rights and obligations and having the
capacity (1) to maintain its rights by bringing international claims; and (2) to be responsible for its
breaches of obligation by being subjected to such claims" (Crawford, *Brownlie's Principles of Public
International Law*, 2019).
:::

The question of who is a subject of international law relates to who has international legal personality:
rights it can claim, and obligations and responsibilities under international law. Different capabilities are
ascribed to subjecthood — the ability to make claims to rights granted under international law; the power to
enter into international diplomatic relations; bearing obligations directly imposed by international law; the
capacity to create international law (e.g. make binding treaties); and the ability to enjoy immunities from
other states' jurisdiction. States do not have the power to prosecute another state, because each state is
sovereign and there is no authority above a state — Australia cannot try New Zealand in front of its courts.

:::rule
States are the only subjects that have full legal capacity. Not all subjects of international law have the
same powers: some subjects have full legal capacity, others have only partial capacity.
:::

:::case
*Reparation for Injuries Suffered in the Service of the United Nations* (Advisory Opinion) [1949] ICJ Rep 174,
178–179: the General Assembly asked whether the UN could bring a claim for reparation against Israel for
injuries (the death of a UN mediator) suffered by its own officials. The UN's international legal personality
does not appear anywhere in the Charter, but the Court concluded "that the Organization is an international
person. That is not the same thing as saying that it is a State, which it certainly is not, or that its legal
personality and rights and duties are the same as those of a State." The opinion admits that international law
is evolving: "The subjects of law in any legal system are not necessarily identical in their nature or in the
extent of their rights, and their nature depends upon the needs of the community."
:::

## Beyond the state: emerging actors

**Individuals.** Individuals were not originally considered to be subjects of international law: they were the
responsibility of the state of their nationality. After WWII there was an emerging belief that individuals may
have certain rights under international law, exercisable against states — the Universal Declaration of Human
Rights 1948 (non-binding), then binding treaty law such as the European Convention on Human Rights 1950 and the
International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights 1966. Certain treaties also give individuals the right to
complain before international bodies: under the Optional Protocol to the ICCPR, a group of eight Torres Strait
Islanders brought a claim before the Human Rights Committee that Australia's failure to implement and mitigate
climate change commitments violated their rights, won, and Australia accepted the recommendation. Individuals
may also have obligations: in May 1999 Slobodan Milošević was indicted by the International Criminal Tribunal
for the Former Yugoslavia for crimes against humanity during the Kosovo War — the first time a head of state
was indicted before an international tribunal. The origin is Nuremberg; the ICTY, the Rwanda tribunal and then
the International Criminal Court followed.

**Groups of individuals.** Peoples have attained some rights under international law, such as self-determination
and the rights of indigenous peoples (e.g. the 2007 UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, which is
not binding, and ILO conventions, which bind states that ratify them). There is no clear definition of a
people; we look at shared language, territory or culture. Rights of minorities protect their culture, language,
religion and other characteristics, and are usually claimed by individuals in their individual capacity. Non-state
armed actors in non-international armed conflicts are bound by certain obligations under international
humanitarian law — common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions applies to them.

**Multinational corporations** are a new frontier, subject to much debate. They are provided with certain rights
under international economic law (e.g. foreign investment protections), but to date there are no legally binding
obligations of multinational companies under international law — hence the push on human rights, e.g. the 2011
Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, which are not binding.

## States as the primary subjects

The 'nation' state remains the primary subject of international law, with international law traditionally
understood as governing the relationships between states. States have full legal personality at the
international level, and thus the most comprehensive rights, duties and competences (e.g. they can make
international treaties and enforce claims through international tribunals). Other actors have emerged as
subjects of international law, but they do not have full legal personality. As a result of decolonisation in the
1950s and 1960s, the number of UN member states has increased from 51 to 193 today — and so has the number of
primary subjects of international law.

## Criteria of statehood

:::statute
Montevideo Convention on Rights and Duties of States (1933) art 1: "The state as a person of international law
should possess the following qualifications: a. a permanent population; b. a defined territory; c. government;
and d. capacity to enter into relations with other states."
:::

It is an old and more regional convention with not many states party to it, but it is widely regarded as
codifying customary international law.

**Population.** No fixed or minimum number is prescribed (Nauru around 12,000 inhabitants, China around 1.4
billion) — the size of the population is irrelevant. Required is some core population that inhabits the
territory on a permanent basis; the criterion does not relate to nationality.

**Defined territory.** States are territorial entities and cannot exist without a certain territory, but no
minimum size is prescribed (the Vatican, around 0.5 sq km, against Russia's 17.1 million sq km; 'micro states'
such as Monaco, Nauru, Tuvalu, San Marino and Liechtenstein). Boundaries need to be defined, but that does not
mean completely fixed or entirely undisputed — border disputes are a common phenomenon among states.

:::case
*North Sea Continental Shelf* [1969] ICJ Rep 3, [46]: "There is for instance no rule that the land frontiers of
a State must be fully delimited and defined, and often in various places and for long periods they are not."
:::

**Effective government.** The most tricky one: some form of coherent and effective exercise of public power over
the territory and population — a "stable political community supporting a legal order to the exclusion of others
in a given area" (Crawford). A colony would not have qualified. Many governments have proven unable to control
parts of their territory (civil war, insurgency, foreign occupation) — so-called fragile or failed states such as
Somalia, Syria, Yemen and Afghanistan — but once statehood is established it carries a presumption of continuity:
ineffective government does not necessarily affect statehood (though effectiveness remains important for the
establishment and recognition of new states). No specific form of government is prescribed: dictatorships and
democracies alike are states.

**Capacity to enter into international relations.** The capacity of a state to contract with other states
internationally, which arises from independence and recognition by other states. Scotland might meet the three
other criteria but does not have the ability to enter into international relations on its own; Greenland, part of
Denmark, is another example.

## Recognition

Recognition is the act by which a state officially acknowledges the existence of another state or government. It
is a complex mixture of politics and law, and the international law on recognition is often an extension of
politics: states sometimes recognise an entity that does not meet all the Montevideo criteria, and sometimes refuse
to recognise one that does, for political reasons. A lack of diplomatic relations between two states does not
necessarily mean absence of recognition. Once recognition is given, it cannot be retracted.

:::definition
**Declaratory theory** (the most widely held today): recognition is nothing more than an acknowledgment of a
pre-existing legal capacity; statehood does not depend on recognition, which is only symbolic. **Constitutive
theory**: recognition is a necessary precondition to the existence of a state — it is recognition by other states
that creates the new state and confers legal personality on it. No recognition, no existence.
:::

An act of recognition may indicate that an entity meets the factual Montevideo criteria and enjoys legitimacy
under international law, and so has reached statehood. Collective recognition is recognition by a group of states
within an international organisation: UN membership needs the approval of two-thirds of the General Assembly and
the Security Council's recommendation, where a single veto can block admission — so non-membership does not mean
non-recognition by most states.

:::rule
Illegality and recognition: non-recognition may serve to prevent an entity from achieving statehood if the means
used to achieve the Montevideo criteria were illegal (1970 Friendly Relations Declaration; *Kosovo* Advisory
Opinion 2010, referring to "the unlawful use of force or other egregious violations of norms of general
international law, in particular those of a peremptory character (jus cogens)").
:::

:::case
Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus: Turkey invaded Cyprus in 1974 and occupied the north; the "TRNC" declared
independence in 1983 and is recognised only by Turkey. UN Security Council resolutions 541 (1983) and 550 (1984)
declared the declaration "legally invalid". It meets the Montevideo criteria, but achieved them illegally.
:::

Other contested or aspiring states: Abkhazia, South Ossetia and Transnistria (recognised only by Russia and a
handful of others); Western Sahara (former Spanish colony annexed by Morocco in 1975; independence declared by the
exiled Polisario, recognised by 46 UN members); Kosovo (independence 2008, recognised by more than 100 UN members,
member of the IMF and World Bank; the ICJ said the declaration was not in itself illegal but did not decide
statehood); and Palestine (1988 PLO declaration, recognised by over 150 UN members, but only a UN non-member
observer state — statehood contested).

## Modes of territorial acquisition

Acquisition of territory is the establishment of sovereignty — sovereign title — over a given piece of land
(continental territory or an island). There are five modes: cession, accretion, effective occupation of terra
nullius, prescription, and conquest (belligerent occupation).

1. **Cession**: one state transfers territory to another, often by a purchase agreement regulated by treaty (US
   purchase of Alaska from Russia in 1867; the 1917 US acquisition of the Danish West Indies). The acquiring state
   cannot obtain more rights than the ceding state had, and must respect the potential rights of third states and
   the indigenous population. It was also the primary way European colonial powers gained territory in Africa and
   Asia, through treaties heavily favouring them.
2. **Accretion**: new land created gradually by nature — river sediment, geological activity, or a new island
   from a volcanic eruption (1986, an undersea volcano created an island in Japan's territorial sea, so it became
   Japan's). Artificial islands "do not possess the status of islands" (UNCLOS art 60(8)); an island is "a
   naturally formed area of land, surrounded by water, which is above water at high tide" (art 121(1)), so
   artificial islands and low-tide elevations are not subject to sovereignty claims. The 2016 South China Sea
   arbitration held that artificial installations cannot establish sovereignty over a maritime feature, whose
   status depends on its original natural condition.
3. **Occupation of terra nullius**: title to territory never under another state's sovereignty, acquired when a
   state exercises effective control over it and intends to obtain title. Not really applicable today, but used
   by the British to justify colonial acquisitions.
4. **Prescription**: title to territory previously under another state's sovereignty. Controversial; it derives
   from implied consent of the dispossessed state, so the acquiring state's control must be peaceful and it must
   act as sovereign, and sustained protests and objections by the 'old' state will prevent prescription.
5. **Conquest (belligerent occupation)**: since the UN Charter 1945 a state cannot acquire territory by force (art
   2(4)). Military occupation instead activates the regime on belligerent occupation — temporary, with no transfer
   of sovereignty (Iraq's attempt to annex Kuwait in 1990; Turkey in northern Cyprus; Russia's 2014 annexation of
   Crimea and 2022 invasion of Ukraine; Israel and Palestine).

## Self-determination

The right of peoples to self-determination is a foundational UN principle carrying a variety of meanings. "At a
minimum, it entails the entitlement of peoples to have control over their destiny and to be treated respectfully"
(Australian Government). UN Charter art 1(2) refers to "respect for the principle of equal rights and
self-determination of peoples" (not framed as a right); ICCPR art 1 then enshrined it as a human right: "All
peoples have the right of self-determination."

In the decolonisation context (1950s–1970s) the right is generally accepted: the 1960 Declaration on the Granting
of Independence to Colonial Territories and Peoples says "the subjection of peoples to alien subjugation, domination
and exploitation constitutes a denial of fundamental human rights", and the ICJ has confirmed its customary status.

:::case
*Chagos* (Advisory Opinion) [2019] ICJ: on Mauritius' claim to the Chagos archipelago, administered by the UK
(which had "no doubt" of its sovereignty over the 'British Indian Ocean Territory'), the Court affirmed the right
of self-determination of the people concerned and the UK's obligation to complete the decolonisation of
Mauritius; separating the islands and removing their population before independence violated that right.
:::

Outside decolonisation, the right has internal and external dimensions, and whether it includes a right of a
people to unilaterally secede from an existing state is highly contentious (Kosovo/Serbia 2008). Referendums
include South Sudan (2010), failed attempts in Quebec and Scotland (2014), and contested unilateral attempts in
Iraqi Kurdistan and Catalonia (2017). One argument is that secession is a legitimate exercise of self-determination
in extreme cases of systematic discrimination and oppression; opponents say it threatens territorial integrity and
sovereignty.

:::case
*Re Secession of Quebec* (Supreme Court of Canada, 1998): unilateral secession is not consistent with international
law or the Canadian Constitution; Quebec's internal self-determination was fulfilled, so there was no ground for
external self-determination.
:::

## International organisations

International organisations (IOs) are a relatively recent phenomenon (19th century: the Danube Commission 1856, the
Permanent Court of Arbitration 1899), turning in the 20th century to global issues — peace and security, health,
trade (League of Nations 1919, WTO, WHO). They are the main emerging subject of international law, and their
powers differ dramatically depending on their constituent treaty.

:::definition
ARIO (ILC Draft Articles on the Responsibility of International Organizations 2011) art 2(a): an organisation
"established by a treaty or other instrument governed by international law and possessing its own international
legal personality. International organizations may include as members, in addition to States, other entities".
:::

Being governed by international law distinguishes IOs from NGOs and informal networks (G20, Greenpeace, Amnesty
International). Almost all IOs are created by treaty (the UN Charter), but some by other instruments such as
resolutions (OPEC; the ICTY and ICTR by Security Council resolution). The constitutive instrument sets
**membership** (UN Charter art 4: "peace-loving states"; OECD art 16; North Atlantic Treaty art 10; the EU is a
member of the FAO), **withdrawal** at the member's initiative (Rome Statute art 127: effective one year after
notification; the UN Charter has no withdrawal provision) and **expulsion** at the IO's initiative (UN Charter art
6).

**Legal personality** means a personality distinct from the members': concluding treaties, sending and receiving
delegations, bringing international claims, and responsibility for wrongdoing. It may be conferred explicitly by
the constitutive instrument (WTO art VIII; the EU, ASEAN — the norm for the last 30 years) or implicitly
(*Reparation for Injuries*). UN Charter art 104 gives only domestic legal capacity.

## Powers and responsibility of international organisations

:::rule
Principle of attribution (speciality, conferred powers): IOs have no general competence, only the powers their
members give them. *Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons* (Advisory Opinion) ICJ 1996 [25]: IOs "do not,
unlike States, possess a general competence" and are governed by "the principle of speciality".
:::

Powers may be **express** (UN Charter art 42: the Security Council can authorise the use of force) or **implied** —
"the Organization must be deemed to have those powers which, though not expressly provided in the Charter, are
conferred upon it by necessary implication as being essential to the performance of its duties" (*Reparation*,
182). Implied powers can be disputed, as with the Security Council's creation of the ICTY and ICTR in the 1990s.

**Responsibility**: the organisation's responsibility is distinct from its members' (the International Tin
Council: the IO, not member states, responsible). ARIO art 3: "Every internationally wrongful act of an
international organization entails the international responsibility of that organization"; art 4: conduct
attributable to the IO that breaches its international obligation. The draft articles are not binding and have
been criticised for modelling one framework, on state responsibility, for IOs that differ widely. Art 6: conduct
of an organ or agent in performing its functions is an act of the IO. Art 7: conduct of a state organ placed at
the IO's disposal is the IO's act "if the organization exercises effective control over that conduct" — key for
UN peacekeepers, who are subsidiary UN organs but troops over which contributing states retain jurisdiction.
