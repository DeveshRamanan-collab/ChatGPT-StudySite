## 1. Overview

This week: what pure economic loss is, and when the law allows recovery for it. Lecture 1 deals with loss caused
by negligent words; Lecture 2 deals with negligent acts and defective structures. We are still inside the first
element of negligence — is a duty of care owed? — and pure economic loss is a category of cases in which the only
damage the plaintiff has suffered is economic loss.

:::examtip
Navigating the text readings for PEL: the text's middle headings ("third party situations", "tort and contract",
etc) can look like separate categories. They are not. Start with the lectures' doctrinal framework — the way the
courts do it — and use the readings to fill it in.
:::

## 2. Concept of pure economic loss

Pure economic loss (PEL) refers to an economic loss which does not result from any physical injury to the person
or property. PEL can be caused intentionally, negligently, or innocently, and may be caused by negligent words,
acts or omissions. Intentional conduct may also result in PEL which may be actionable under "economic torts" such
as deceit, fraudulent misrepresentation or passing off — this is outside the scope of this unit.

Consequential economic loss — wage loss due to physical injury, medical expenses due to physical injury, the cost
of required home modifications linked to an injury — has never been a problem: once there is physical injury,
the consequential economic loss follows. Pure economic loss is different: investment losses due to negligent
financial advice, loss of profit due to electrical cable damage, or diminished property value due to an
architect's defective advice. If a council worker negligently cuts the cable a cake business needs, the lost
profits are PEL; if the same cut short-circuits the fridge and the stock spoils, that is property damage and the
losses are consequential.

The distinction between consequential economic loss and PEL is critical because the latter was not recoverable
until recently. Even now, PEL is recoverable only in certain situations if additional conditions are satisfied.
Reasonable foreseeability is always needed, but where we move away from the paradigm of physical injury or
property damage, more is needed — and we find what more by going to the cases.

### Policy considerations

:::rule
There are several policy considerations behind courts' general reluctance to allow the recovery of PEL:
indeterminate liability; the relationship with the law of contract; and loss linked to a lawful commercial
activity.
:::

Indeterminate liability means liability without boundaries — for example, advice to "go out and buy" a
cryptocurrency given on radio or a podcast, where there is no way of defining who heard it and relied on it. A
large class is not necessarily a problem; a class that cannot be defined is. On contract, where parties have
negotiated the terms of their relationship, courts are reluctant to impose additional obligations through a duty
of care. And a loss caused by lawful competition — a second supermarket opening down the road — is a pure
economic loss, but courts will not impose a duty of care for it.

:::statute
The Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) s 5 defines "harm" for the negligence Part as harm of any kind, including
personal injury or death, damage to property, and economic loss.
:::

## 3. PEL caused by negligent words

### Words and acts

PEL may be caused by negligent words — for example, negligent advice from a lawyer, accountant, auditor or
valuer, or a public body that provides information negligently. Courts have drawn a distinction between negligent
words and negligent actions. Lord Reid in *Hedley Byrne v Heller* observed: "[T]he law must treat negligent words
differently from negligent acts. … Quite careful people often express definite opinions on social or informal
occasions, even when they see that others are likely to be influenced by them; and often they do that without
taking that care which they would if asked for their opinion professionally, or in business transactions …
there can be no duty of care on such occasions."

The same advice given at a barbecue and given across a desk in an office may be treated very differently: is it
reasonable that the person relied on it, did the speaker know they might, did the speaker assume responsibility,
and does the speaker have the requisite skill and knowledge? In a business setting, a duty is more likely — not
definite.

### From deceit to negligent misstatement

At common law, the remedy for fraudulent misstatement was available under deceit. However, the House of Lords in
*Derry v Peek* (1889) ruled that negligent misstatement will not suffice for deceit — rather, it must be an
intentional or reckless misstatement. That is where the decision in *Hedley Byrne* became critical: it created a
liability for negligent misstatement at common law. (Deceit is an intentional tort and is not covered in this
unit; it does not use the duty of care framework.)

:::case
*Hedley Byrne v Heller* [1964] AC 465 — an advertising agency, about to extend credit to a client, asked its bank
to obtain a credit reference; the reference came back favourable but "without responsibility"; the client
collapsed and the agency lost money. Duty of care for negligent words where a special relationship exists. Lord
Reid: the inquirer trusts the speaker to take care, reasonably relies, and the speaker knows or ought to know of
the reliance. Lord Morris: a person with special skill who undertakes to apply it for another who relies on it.
The disclaimer meant no duty arose on the facts.
:::

:::case
*Mutual Life & Citizens' Assurance Co v Evatt* (1968) 122 CLR 556 — advice about investing in an associated
company proved wrong and caused loss. Barwick CJ: information or advice on a serious matter; the speaker realises
(or should realise) they are being trusted; it is reasonable for the recipient to act on it; a duty of reasonable
care follows. (Quoted in the Esanda casebook extract.)
:::

### Shaddock, San Sebastian and Tepko

:::case
*Shaddock & Associates Pty Ltd v Parramatta City Council* (1981) 150 CLR 225 — the council told an intending
purchaser (first by phone, then by certificate) that no road-widening proposal affected the land; there was one,
and the land's value was destroyed. Duty found: it covers information as well as advice, and there is no separate
requirement that the speaker possess special skill.
:::

In *Shaddock* the council was the only place the information could have come from, and the reliance on it was
clear. The Court suggested the phone call alone might not have been enough; the phone call together with the
certificate was. If the plaintiff knew the speaker lacked a skill the task required, a duty is less likely,
because reliance becomes harder to prove.

:::case
*San Sebastian Pty Ltd v Minister* (1986) 162 CLR 340 — developers bought and planned in Woolloomooloo on the
strength of a publicly exhibited council redevelopment scheme, which the council later abandoned. A duty can
arise without any request — for example, known special competence, an assumption of responsibility, inviting or
intending reliance, or an interest in the recipient acting. The claim failed: the statement was one of intention,
and relying on a mere intention was not reasonable.
:::

Brennan J's conditions: the representor realises the representee will trust their special competence; it is
reasonable for the representee to accept and rely on the statement; and loss is reasonably foreseeable if the
statement is wrong.

:::case
*Tepko Pty Ltd v Water Board* (2001) 206 CLR 1 — landowners wanting to subdivide needed a water-supply costing for
their bank; the Board reluctantly gave a "ball-park" figure, which was shown to the bank; the figure was
overstated and the arrangements collapsed. No duty: the Board neither knew nor should have known of the reliance,
and reliance on a reluctant, provisional estimate was not reasonable. Needed: known reliance, an assumption of
responsibility, or both. The dissenters would have found a duty (statutory authority, special knowledge, serious
business consequences).
:::

### Esanda

:::case
*Esanda Finance Corp v PMH* (1997) 188 CLR 241 — Esanda lent money to companies associated with Excel; PMH had
audited and certified Excel's accounts; Esanda alleged PMH were negligent and that it consequently suffered
economic loss after Excel was placed into receivership. The question was whether PMH owed Esanda a duty of care.
The High Court held that no such duty existed — there were no circumstances which might serve to establish any
relationship of proximity between Esanda and PMH.
:::

Esanda's pleading rested on being one of a class who might reasonably rely on audited accounts. That was not
enough: there was no request, no intention by the auditors to induce the financier to act, and no assumption of
responsibility. The purpose of an audit is to inform the company and its shareholders, not people investing in or
lending to it.

Dawson J observed: "the mere foreseeability of harm does not, where the only harm is pure economic loss, give rise
to a duty of care. The reason for this is that a duty of care imposed by reference to the mere foreseeability of
harm in the form of financial loss would extend liability in negligence beyond acceptable bounds. … for a duty of
care to arise in cases of pure economic loss, the law requires, in addition to the foreseeability of harm, a
special relationship between the parties which is described as a relationship of proximity."

:::rule
The Court in Esanda identified several factors that could establish the necessary "proximity" or "special
relationship" for pure economic loss claims, in addition to reasonable foreseeability: reasonable reliance; the
nature of the relationship; special skill or knowledge; knowledge of reliance; and assumption of responsibility.
"Proximity" is the historical label; the modern approach treats these as salient factors (*Sullivan v Moody*).
:::

McHugh J gave several policy reasons why auditors should not have a duty of care to third parties: an increase in
the cost of auditing services; more complex litigation; the risk of indeterminate liability; and difficulties of
proving reliance.

### Disclaimers and assumption of responsibility

A disclaimer is one factor against a duty arising (*Hedley Byrne* itself: no duty arose). Distinguish a disclaimer
of responsibility, which excludes the duty, from a warning that care has not been taken, which goes to whether
reliance was reasonable.

:::case
*Mallonland Pty Ltd v Advanta Seeds Pty Ltd* [2024] HCA 25 — "assumption of responsibility" is imprecise; it is
understood as an undertaking to take on a task from which acceptance of reasonable care can be inferred. No duty
of care was found; the High Court's language is cautious, working through the circumstances step by step.
:::

### Vulnerability

:::case
*Anchorage Capital Master Offshore Ltd v Sparkes* (2023) 111 NSWLR 304 — lenders relied on false financial
notices from the borrower's officers; the company collapsed; no duty. Sophisticated commercial parties able to
protect themselves by contract are not vulnerable.
:::

Vulnerability is a separate salient factor. The text's flowchart places reliance and assumption of responsibility
underneath vulnerability: if the defendant knew or ought to have known you would rely, and your reliance was
reasonable, there is a vulnerability. That is not an error — just a different way of thinking about it. Esanda
does not mention vulnerability as such, but it is part of the reasoning.

### Statutory liability for misstatements

:::statute
There are now also statutory provisions which provide for liability for misrepresentation. Section 18 of the
Australian Consumer Law provides: "A person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading
or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive."
:::

Section 18 covers both conduct and words. Under s 18 there is no need to establish a duty of care or prove
negligence — what needs to be proved is the existence of misleading or deceptive conduct (a causal link is still
needed, though nowhere near to the extent required at common law). Yet the common law remains relevant, for
example where the defendant is not engaging "in trade or commerce", and a plaintiff can argue both.

:::case
*ACCC v Kimberly-Clark Australia* [2019] FCA 992 (first instance); [2020] FCAFC 107 (appeal) — the ACCC brought
action on two counts: whether the wipes were "flushable" and whether they were "made in Australia". The Federal
Court found the "made in Australia" claim was false and misleading — the wipes were made elsewhere — but ruled
that Kimberly-Clark had not made false and misleading claims about the "flushable" wipes, which was upheld on
appeal by the Full Federal Court. Kimberly-Clark had faced similar actions in the US and decided to settle out of
court.
:::

### Approaching duty of care for PEL by negligent misstatement

:::rule
Factors relevant to the inquiry (the relevant salient features) include: reasonable foreseeability (necessary but
not sufficient); indeterminacy of liability; category of relationship; the defendant's knowledge (of matters
affecting the plaintiff, including reliance); reliance by the plaintiff (reasonable in the circumstances?);
assumption of responsibility by the defendant; special skill of the defendant; and vulnerability of the
plaintiff.
:::

This is not a rigid checklist: there is a cross-over between factors depending on the circumstances, and courts
look to the circumstances as a whole. You always need reasonable foreseeability, but you do not need to tick every
other factor to find a duty. Reliance and assumption of responsibility have come through the cases as especially
important.

:::examtip
There is not necessarily a right answer about whether a duty is owed. Marks come from the process: knowing the
cases and principles, applying them to the facts, and weighing them up.
:::

## 4. PEL caused by an act

Here the defendant has done something — a sale, damage to something — rather than given words or advice. In
England, no duty is generally imposed by courts for PEL caused by an act. On the other hand, courts in Australia
may find such a duty after applying the "salient factors" approach — case by case, incrementally and
conservatively, wary of incoherent law and indeterminate liability. The subcategories (words, acts, defective
structures) exist because of precedent: they tell you which earlier cases are most helpful.

### Perre v Apand

:::case
*Perre v Apand Pty Ltd* (1999) 198 CLR 180 — Apand supplied potato seeds to Sparnon's farm; Sparnon's farm and
Perre's farm were 3 km apart; Apand's seeds were infected with a "bacterial wilt" disease; WA had prohibited
importing potatoes grown within 20 km of wilt disease; the Perres suffered PEL by being unable to export potatoes
to WA. Answer in the High Court: yes, a duty was owed.
:::

Both Sparnon and Perre brought claims. In the lower courts Sparnon won: Apand's duty to Sparnon was clearer,
Sparnon suffered the physical damage of the potatoes being diseased, and Sparnon could also sue under contract and
consumer law. Perre lost: Perre did not suffer physical damage — there was no disease on their property — so the
courts treated it as PEL, and there was no contract between the parties. The High Court had to consider whether
Apand had a duty to take reasonable care to protect the Perres from PEL.

:::rule
McHugh J's questions: (1) Was the loss suffered by the Perres or members of the group reasonably foreseeable?
(2) If yes, would imposing a duty of care impose indeterminate liability on Apand? (3) If no, would it impose an
unreasonable burden on Apand's autonomy? (4) If no, were the Perres or some of them vulnerable to loss from
Apand's conduct? (5) Did Apand know that its conduct could cause harm to individuals such as the Perres?
:::

The 20 km limit set by the regulation defined the class, so liability was not indeterminate; the Perres had no
control over who in the radius grew potatoes from which seeds, so they were vulnerable; and Apand, in the potato
industry, knew of the disease and the risk. Each judge applied the salient features approach but weighed the
features differently — students are not expected to reach McHugh J's exact analysis, but to show they understand
the features and how they apply to new facts.

From various judgments, one can find several salient factors: Is the financial loss reasonably foreseeable? Any
risk of indeterminate liability? Is coherence of law threatened (*Sullivan v Moody*)? Is the plaintiff a member of
an ascertained class? Did the defendant have knowledge of the risk and its magnitude? Was the defendant in control
of the situation? Was the plaintiff vulnerable? (Compare the list in *Caltex Refineries (Qld) Pty Ltd v Stavar*
from Topic 5.)

### Caltex Oil

:::case
*Caltex Oil v Dredge 'Willemstad'* (1976) 136 CLR 529 — even before the salient factors approach was firmly
established, the High Court had found a duty of care in PEL cases. The defendant dredge negligently damaged an oil
pipeline; Caltex did not claim for the small amount of property damage but instead claimed pure economic loss,
being the significant expenses of transporting the oil by road around the bay. The Court held that the defendant
owed a duty of care to Caltex.
:::

:::examtip
Precedent at work: acting for a plaintiff in a similar position to Caltex, draw out the similarities; acting for
the defendant, point to significant differences in the facts to distinguish it.
:::

## 5. PEL caused by defective structures

Parties can deal with obvious defects in buildings through contractual arrangements. However, the law of torts
comes into the picture to deal with latent defects — defects not identifiable until years later, perhaps after
the building has been sold three or four times. Where the defect causes a physical injury, the builder or architect
will generally be liable to anyone foreseeably injured. However, a defect in the building has been viewed by the
courts as PEL — for example, diminishing the value of the property. Such PEL may arise because a building has been
negligently planned, designed or built.

:::case
*Bryan v Maloney* (1995) 182 CLR 608 — a builder constructed a house with defective foundations; Mrs Maloney, who
was the third owner since the construction, noticed cracks in the walls due to the defective foundations. The High
Court ruled that a builder owed a duty of care to a subsequent purchaser to take reasonable care in the building
of a house.
:::

In *Bryan*, the High Court used the proximity principle to impose a duty of care to avoid PEL. It also drew a
distinction between the construction of dwellings and other buildings: a homeowner is thought to be more
vulnerable and less sophisticated than a commercial party. The High Court had a chance to consider these aspects
in a subsequent case, *Woolcock*.

:::case
*Woolcock Street Investments Pty Ltd v CDG Pty Ltd* (2004) 216 CLR 515 — CDG designed a commercial complex; as one
of the owners refused to pay for a geotechnical inspection, it was not done; Woolcock purchased the building from
the original owners; the building settled, causing structural distress; Woolcock sued for failure to properly
design the structure and foundations. The Court did not find any duty: there was no reliance or assumption of
responsibility, and Woolcock was not vulnerable.
:::

As a commercial purchaser, Woolcock could have done more due diligence and could have protected itself through
inspections or insurance; there is more of an onus on commercial enterprises to protect themselves than on an
individual buying a house.

### Statutory duty of care regarding building construction

:::statute
In NSW, the Design and Building Practitioners Act 2020 (NSW) provides owners a cause of action against builders
for negligent construction. The duty extends to all subsequent purchasers (s 37); the duty is non-delegable
(s 39); and the duty cannot be contracted out (s 40). This makes it easier for owners and subsequent purchasers to
sue builders, as there is no need to establish a duty of care at common law.
:::

:::statute
Section 37: "(1) A person who carries out construction work has a duty to exercise reasonable care to avoid
economic loss caused by defects— (a) in or related to a building for which the work is done, and (b) arising from
the construction work. (2) The duty of care is owed to each owner of the land in relation to which the
construction work is carried out and to each subsequent owner of the land. (3) A person to whom the duty of care
is owed is entitled to damages for the breach of the duty as if the duty were a duty established by the common
law."
:::

The term "construction work" has been defined broadly by s 36: in addition to building work, it includes the
preparation of designs, the manufacture or supply of building products, and supervision, coordination or project
management over building work.
