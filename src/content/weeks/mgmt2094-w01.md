## What is the unit about?

- Creating a different mind set… **zig when everyone else is zagging!** What does that mean to you? The lecturer
  says this is the question the unit keeps returning to; by the end you should have worked out the different meanings
  creativity has, and the specific meaning it has for you.
- The terminology now used is **"flip"**, which is repeatedly moving between different perspectives and paradigms.
  This is now done in "real" time and on demand.
- It is a **flexible mindset**: it stimulates a way of thinking and approach to life.
- It is a more **counter-intuitive approach** that reflects the way business is heading today, to be more creative
  and innovative.

Why now? The lecture's explanation: the world of business is fast moving and changing very quickly, so you need to be
able to move between different perspectives, and do it with ease. With the advance of AI, technical knowledge is not
enough, because AI can provide any organisation or entrepreneur with technical knowledge and skill. What matters is
creativity and innovation, and learning to work with AI to boost creativity and create innovation. The lecturer adds
that the approach is useful beyond business, in any sphere of life.

## Why study creativity and innovation?

- Aren't we just that naturally or not? Some of us may well be, others aren't.
- Creativity isn't just about the arts, and innovation isn't just about new things.
- It will actually allow us to distinguish ourselves from others in the "new world".
- Name some who have done that?

The lecturer's view: people who are not naturally creative can probably improve, and even naturally creative people
(even artists) gain from studying creativity: it lets them build structures around their creativity, leverage it,
and use it to be innovative in creating something practical and useful, such as a business. Creativity is pretty much
everywhere, especially in business and organisations. Inspiration can come from business people, but also from
historical figures, politicians, artists, even generals; one way to learn from creative people is to read their
biographies.

## Creativity — definition

:::definition
"Creativity is seeing what everyone else has seen, and thinking what no-one else has thought!" — Einstein
:::

We have an abundance of, and easy access to, all kinds of information, so we all can see a lot. The key is to think,
or perceive, the things no-one else has thought.

## Being creative

- "When people say to me that they aren't creative, I assume they haven't learnt what is involved" — Sir Ken
  Robinson (2011).
- What is creative thinking? It is seen simply as the ability to think, imagine or invent something new. Who has done
  that here?
- Whom do we admire as being creative in a business sense? It may have been the likes of Jeff Bezos, Elon Musk etc.
- Now we look at creative ways/methods of doing business, e.g. **dropshipping** (where you don't physically hold the
  stock, but the manufacturer/wholesaler supplies your customer directly).

The lecture keeps the definition broad: imagining things while reading a book or watching a film, or one day taking a
different route to campus, is already creative thinking, however small. We all have this ability. The question then
becomes whether we can take this creative thinking, leverage it, and make innovation with it. Dropshipping is a
simple idea that someone came up with and was able to implement, and it brings value.

## How can we become creative?

- Without creativity we would have no progress and would be forever repeating the same experiences/patterns.
- What are some methods that can assist us in being creative?
  1. Mind mapping
  2. Brainstorming
  3. Sketching
  4. Free writing
  5. Thinking opposites
  6. Seeking inspiration

Since we are all somewhat creative already, the lecturer rephrases the question: how can we become **more** creative?
Progress requires imagination. The methods are an arsenal of tactics or tools, and even more important is how to
unleash creativity in a group or an organisation: relying on a group of people is much more effective than relying
only on yourself. Mind maps are covered in detail in the tutorials.

## Developing skills — working out at the creativity gym

1. **Starting off** — learning and developing the skills.
2. **Being proficient** — training your mind and getting inspiration.
3. **Becoming an expert** — practising your creative thoughts and training your mind to automatically go into default
   creative mode, and then receiving feedback on those thoughts/actions etc.

The first stage is the base level: if we want to create a business, we need to know and understand the industry we
are entering and develop the skills required to run a business. The second needs a systematic approach to training
our minds and our teams for creativity and inspiration, since everybody, and every organisation, is slightly
different. At the third, creativity becomes part of who you are as a professional, so it comes much more easily when
it is needed.

## What competencies do we need to become creative?

**Individual competencies:** exploring problems; building up willpower; self-awareness; being open to failure and
feedback from others.

**Team/group competencies:** supporting ideas from team members; practising psychological safety; creating and sharing
visions; pushing the frontiers.

Exploring problems: some people are naturally curious; others can develop it by exploring problems in a systematic
way. Willpower: without it we cannot be creative or keep looking for solutions to the problems we find. Self-awareness:
understanding our strengths and weaknesses, to leverage the strengths and minimise the weaknesses (the lecturer links
this to emotional intelligence; the term is not on the slides). Openness to failure: learn from failures, see failure
in a positive light, and use feedback, especially when you fail. In the recording the lecturer says entrepreneurs fail
on average three to four times before becoming successful; the figure is not on the slides.

:::definition
**Psychological safety** (as the lecture explains it): an environment where everybody feels safe, not to relax but to
take risks. Without it, people hold back their ideas because they are afraid of being seen as stupid or incompetent.
:::

In the team: become a champion of other people's ideas, consistently; rely on each other's different expertise to
create shared visions; and, feeling supported by the team, push the boundaries. That is where creative ideas come in,
and it is the fuel for innovation.

## The path this semester

The slide shows the path as **vision → imagination → creativity → innovation**. Across the semester we look at how
they are different, how they support each other, and how we can build them.

## Reading — Empire of Tech (Lev-Ram, 2015)

Michal Lev-Ram's profile of Disney CEO Bob Iger (Fortune, 1 January 2015; listed on iLearn as "Iger, 2015") argues that
Iger has spent his near decade at Disney wearing an additional corporate hat, CTO, and has brought the innovations of
Lucasfilm, Pixar, Marvel and ESPN into a single galaxy. Its lesson: not only do today's media companies need to start
thinking like technology companies, their CEOs also need to start thinking like CTOs.

Iger made big and early bets on new technologies, even some seemingly at odds with the company's business model: in
2005 ABC's *Lost* and *Desperate Housewives* went on iTunes, the first deal of its kind, despite backlash from
affiliates, retailers and the guilds; in 2006 Disney offered free full-length TV shows online. He bought Pixar ($7.4
billion, 2006), Marvel ($4.3 billion, 2009) and Lucasfilm ($4 billion, 2012). "If you're going to win big, you have to
get in early." Not every bet paid: the interactive division was in the red for five years, losing more than $1 billion
— "we learned from that".

His strategy rested on three pillars: investing in creative content, international expansion and technological
innovation. He shifted decision-making from a central strategic planning division to the business units and, instead
of one corporate CTO, appointed a CTO for each business, giving them "the ability to experiment and try even if they
fail … without a corporate watchdog looking over their shoulder all the time". A CTO council shares challenges and
runs hackathons and a "Best of Disney" symposium.

Imagineering's Dish, a virtual reality chamber for testing future rides, is "blue sky" experimentation still too
expensive and cumbersome for the parks. MagicBands, RFID wristbands for entry, hotel rooms and payment, were rolled out
at Disney World through a gradual, iterative process (more than 28,000 door locks changed; more than 9 million bands
given out). Tim Cook: "He understands the tradition of Disney but isn't wedded to it." John Lasseter: "Art challenges
technology, and technology inspires art."

:::examtip
Use Disney as a worked example across the week: the iTunes deal shows the flip; the Dish shows creativity (thinking up
new things) not yet innovation; MagicBands show innovation (doing new things); a CTO for each business shows the team
competencies, such as psychological safety and openness to failure.
:::
