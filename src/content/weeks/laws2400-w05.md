## Overview of negligence

Negligence is a relatively new tort, but it has become a very important cause of action over the years.
Unlike the intentional torts, which are actionable per se, negligence is an action on the case, so damage is
vital to found an action.

:::rule
Negligence has three key elements the plaintiff must prove: (1) the defendant owed the plaintiff a duty of
care; (2) the defendant breached that duty of care; and (3) that breach caused the plaintiff compensable harm
— assessed through factual causation and the scope of liability (remoteness of harm).
:::

Reasonable foreseeability (the neighbour principle from Donoghue v Stevenson) is always required for a duty
of care, but not always sufficient; in practice, establishing a duty of care is often not controversial.
Breach asks about the degree of foreseeable risk created by the defendant's conduct and whether the
defendant's response to that risk was reasonable; causation asks about factual causation and the scope of
liability.

:::statute
Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) s 5A: this Part applies to any claim for damages for harm resulting from
negligence, regardless of whether the claim is brought in tort, contract, under statute or otherwise, but does
not apply to civil liability excluded from the Part by s 3B.
:::

Civil liability excluded from the CLA by s 3B includes an intentional act done with intent to cause injury or
death, sexual assault or sexual misconduct, claims where workers' compensation legislation applies, and dust
diseases — these areas remain governed by the common law or other legislative regimes. The CLA applies, in
part, to motor vehicle accidents (for this unit, assume the CLA applies for motor vehicle accidents). Duty of
care is mostly common law, modified by the CLA only in specific circumstances (public authorities, mental
harm, recreational activities, professional negligence, intoxication, criminal enterprise); breach and
causation, by contrast, have been codified or modified by the CLA (breach: ss 5B and 5C; causation: ss 5D and
5E), though the common law principles remain relevant to interpreting those sections.

## The neighbour principle: Donoghue v Stevenson

The House of Lords' ruling in *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562 remains a landmark decision to date. It is
widely regarded as one of the most important decisions of the 20th century — the neighbour principle has
become the lingua franca for the tort of negligence — and shows how English cases have influenced tort law in
other jurisdictions.

:::case
*Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562 — in August 1928, Mrs Donoghue's friend bought her an ice-cream soda and
a ginger beer at a Scottish café; as the ginger beer was poured from its opaque bottle, a decomposed snail
floated out, and Donoghue suffered shock and severe gastro-enteritis. She sued the manufacturer, Stevenson,
despite having no contract with him. The House of Lords, by a 3:2 majority, allowed her appeal.
:::

Lord Atkin reasoned: "The rule that you are to love your neighbour becomes in law, you must not injure your
neighbour; and the lawyer's question — Who is my neighbour? — receives a restricted reply. You must take
reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your
neighbour." He continued: "Who, then, in law is my neighbour? The answer seems to be — persons who are so
closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation as being so
affected when I am directing my mind to the acts or omissions which are called in question."

:::rule
The neighbour principle: if you can reasonably foresee that your careless actions could put your neighbour at
risk of harm, then a duty of care is owed. The law has evolved significantly since 1932, so the neighbour
principle is a starting position only, not the end of the inquiry.
:::

The principle was applied in *Grant v Australian Knitting Mills* [1936] AC 85, where free sulphites left in
woollen underpants by negligent manufacturing caused Dr Grant severe dermatitis; the Privy Council held the
case fell within Lord Atkin's principle, clarifying that it applies where the defect is "hidden and unknown to
the consumer, otherwise the directness of cause and effect is absent". The paradigm case in negligence is an
act causing physical injury or property damage: if nothing is "special" about the plaintiff or defendant,
reasonable foreseeability alone is generally enough. For omissions, initial harm that is not physical injury
or property damage, or where there are special parties, more than reasonable foreseeability is usually
required — we look to the common law cases that have decided what "more" means.

## From neighbour principle to proximity to salient features

From the 1960s onwards, courts began expanding negligence to new situations, such as pure economic loss and
the liability of public authorities. *Hedley Byrne v Heller* [1964] AC 465 concluded that even pure economic
loss (economic loss not derived from personal injury or property damage) caused by negligent words may be
recoverable. Then came *Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co Ltd* [1970] AC 1004: seven boys in juvenile detention,
working on an island under the supervision of three officers, escaped and boarded a yacht that collided with
the respondent's yacht; the House of Lords held the Home Office owed a duty of care, because it was
foreseeable that yachts nearby were at risk if the boys escaped, and the Home Office had control of the boys
and knowledge of the escape risk.

In *Anns v Merton London Borough Council* [1978] AC 728, the House of Lords laid down a two-stage test: was
there a sufficient relationship of proximity or neighbourhood between the parties, and are there any
considerations which ought to negative or limit the scope of the duty? Courts in Australia did not follow the
Anns test (*Sutherland Shire Council v Heyman* (1985) 157 CLR 424). Later, in *Caparo Industries plc v
Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605, the House of Lords proposed a three-stage test: was the harm reasonably foreseeable;
was there a relationship of proximity; and is it fair and reasonable to impose a duty in the circumstances?
The Caparo test has never been incorporated into Australian negligence law.

In the 1980s, Australian courts used proximity as a principle: the first step was still reasonable
foreseeability of harm, and proximity was then used as a limiting factor. Deane J in *Sutherland Shire Council
v Heyman* said: "The requirement of a relationship of proximity serves as a touchstone and control of the
categories of case in which the common law will adjudge that a duty of care is owed", directed to the
relationship between the parties so far as relevant to the defendant's negligent act or omission and the
plaintiff's loss or injury; proximity indicators included physical or circumstantial proximity, reliance,
assumption of responsibility and control. However, since the late 1990s, the High Court of Australia has
followed the "salient factors" approach instead: courts still begin with reasonable foreseeability, then apply
the salient factors approach to decide whether a duty of care should arise, because a duty of care is not a
general duty but a contextual one.

## What reasonable foreseeability means

:::case
*Chapman v Hearse* (1961) 106 CLR 112 — Chapman's negligent driving caused a collision; thrown from his car,
he lay unconscious on the road. Dr Cherry, aiding him on a dark, wet night, was struck and killed by another
driver, Hearse. Cherry's widow successfully sued Hearse, who joined Chapman as a third party for contribution.
Chapman argued he owed Dr Cherry no duty of care because the precise chain of events was not reasonably
foreseeable. The High Court rejected the argument and found a duty was owed.
:::

The Court held: "It is, we think, sufficient ... to ask whether a consequence of the same general character as
that which followed was reasonably foreseeable ... it is not necessary for the plaintiff to show that the
precise manner in which his injuries were sustained was reasonably foreseeable; it is sufficient if it appears
that injury to a class of person of which he was one might reasonably have been foreseen as a consequence."
What is reasonably foreseeable can also change with the state of scientific knowledge about risk — for
example, the risk of exposure to asbestos, or the impact of climate change.

:::definition
Reasonable foreseeability is central to negligence, but it is asked three times, and means something different
each time. Glass JA explained (*San Sebastian Pty Ltd v Minister* [1983] 2 NSWLR 268): at the duty stage,
whether an injury was reasonably foreseeable to a "class of persons" — emphasis on the relationship between
the parties (*Chapman v Hearse*); at the breach stage, whether "the kind of carelessness" by the defendant
might cause damage to the plaintiff — emphasis on the defendant's action; and at the remoteness stage, whether
the "kind of damage" suffered by the plaintiff was reasonably foreseeable as a possible outcome — emphasis on
the damage suffered. The inquiry "progressively decline[s] from the general to the particular".
:::

## Established categories of duty

In most cases, whether the defendant owed the plaintiff a duty of care is uncontroversial, because the
relationship between them falls into an "established category" — a relationship that has come before the
courts previously, and that the courts have recognised as giving rise to a duty of care. In such cases you can
point to case law history to show the relationship falls into an established category — for example, "this is
a manufacturer/consumer case, an established category per Donoghue v Stevenson".

The courts have recognised certain relationships as giving rise to a duty of care, including: a driver of a
vehicle owes a duty to other road users; employers owe a duty to their employees; a doctor owes a duty to
their patients; a manufacturer owes a duty to consumers or users of their products; and a solicitor owes a duty
to exercise due care in their work for their clients.

:::examtip
Sometimes the facts of a particular case can draw it away from the accepted paradigm — *Chapman v Hearse* was
a car accident case (drivers/road users is an established category), but because of its unusual facts, the
parties still raised whether a duty of care was owed.
:::

## Novel duty situations and the salient features approach

When the facts move away from the original paradigm, more than reasonable foreseeability of harm is required
to establish a duty of care. In such novel cases, Australian courts adopt an incremental approach and consider
the salient factors.

:::case
*Sullivan v Moody* (2001) 207 CLR 562 — a father was suspected of sexually abusing his daughter; a medical
practitioner and social workers were alleged to have acted negligently in examining the child and
investigating the allegations. No criminal charges were laid, but the investigation caused the family's
divorce and other impacts; the father alleged he suffered shock, distress, psychiatric injury and consequential
financial loss, and sued the investigators for negligence. The High Court did not find a duty of care in these
circumstances.
:::

The Court noted that if mere reasonable foreseeability of harm entailed legal liability, this would impose an
intolerable burden of potential liability on people, and would result in the tort of negligence subverting
other principles of law and statutory provisions. It considered coherence of the law an important factor — the
defendants' duty to protect the interests of children being paramount — and the difficulty of limiting the
extent and potential indeterminacy of liability (a duty may not be limited to the father, but also to friends,
teachers or anyone else suspected of abusing the child). The Court highlighted these as salient (relevant)
factors.

:::case
*Caltex Refineries (Qld) Pty Ltd v Stavar* (2009) 75 NSWLR 649 — Stavar's husband was exposed to asbestos
while working at an oil refinery; when washing his contaminated work clothes, Stavar's wife was exposed to
asbestos dust and developed cancer. An employer owing an employee a duty of care is not novel, but the
question here was whether the employer owed a duty to the employee's wife, making it a novel case requiring
the salient factors approach. The NSW Court of Appeal held a duty of care could be found on these facts.
:::

:::definition
Salient factors (Caltex Refineries v Stavar) are a non-exhaustive list — the plaintiff is not required to meet
all of them. Key factors include: foreseeability of harm to the plaintiff; the degree and nature of control by
the defendant; vulnerability of the plaintiff and their ability to self-protect; the degree of reliance by the
plaintiff; any assumption of responsibility by the defendant; proximity of the plaintiff to the defendant; the
category of relationship between them; the nature of the defendant's activity; the degree of danger from the
defendant's conduct; the defendant's knowledge that the conduct will harm the plaintiff; the indeterminacy of
liability; the nature and consequences of potential action to avoid harm; imposition on individual autonomy;
conflicting duties; consistency with relevant statutes; and coherence in the common law.
:::

The salient factors test is not the general test for duty of care — it is a tool used only in specific
circumstances, such as novel cases (for example, harm from climate change), and specific factors are used for
establishing a duty of care where the case is not the paradigm act causing physical injury or property damage
(omissions, pure mental harm, pure economic loss, public authorities). In practice, most negligence cases
involve established categories where the law on duty of care is settled.

## Policy factors that can negate a duty

Even where harm is reasonably foreseeable, several reasons of policy can lead a court to refuse to recognise a
duty of care: if finding a duty would result in indeterminate liability; if it would impose on the autonomy or
freedom of individuals, including the right to pursue their own interests; if it would conflict with a duty
arising from other principles of law or statute (as in *Sullivan v Moody*); if it would be inconsistent with
the terms, scope and purpose of a statute relevant to the existence of a duty; or if it would threaten the
structure and fabric of the common law.

## Duty of care and pure economic loss (negligent misstatement)

:::definition
Pure economic loss (PEL) is economic loss not consequent upon any physical injury to person or property. The
Civil Liability Act's definition of "harm" (s 5) expressly includes economic loss alongside personal injury,
death and property damage.
:::

A plaintiff is likely to suffer pure economic loss if reasonable care is not taken; the test of reasonable
foreseeability (*Donoghue v Stevenson*) still applies, along with the Wagon Mound (No 2) question of whether
the loss was far-fetched or fanciful in the circumstances. But reasonable foreseeability of economic loss is
necessary, and not sufficient on its own, to justify imposing a duty of care regarding that loss (*Perre v
Apand Pty Ltd* (1999) 198 CLR 180). A limit on liability for pure economic loss is needed because of the fear
of liability in an indeterminate amount (*Ultramares Corp v Touche, Niven and Co* (1931) 174 NE 441) and a
disproportion between the defendant's blameworthiness and the potential extent of liability (*San Sebastian
Pty Ltd v Minister* (1986) 162 CLR 340).

:::case
*Hedley Byrne v Heller* [1964] AC 465 — Hedley Byrne asked its bank, National Provincial, to check the credit
status of Easipower Ltd with Easipower's own bank, Heller & Partners. Heller gave a favourable reference "without
responsibility". Hedley Byrne relied on the reference and suffered loss when Easipower went into liquidation.
The House of Lords held a duty of care can arise where the loss is pure economic loss, but Hedley Byrne's own
claim failed because Heller had disclaimed responsibility for the reference.
:::

Lord Reid: "where it is plain that the party seeking information or advice was trusting the other to exercise
such a degree of care as the circumstances required, where it was reasonable for him to do that, and where the
other gave the information or advice when he knew or ought to have known that the inquirer was relying on
him." Lord Morris: "if someone possessed of a special skill undertakes, quite irrespective of contract, to
apply that skill for the assistance of another person who relies upon such skills, a duty of care will arise."
The Hedley Byrne principle requires, in addition to reasonable foreseeability: (i) a special relationship
between the parties; (ii) a voluntary assumption of responsibility by the party providing the advice; (iii)
the other party's reliance on the advice; and (iv) reasonableness of such reliance.

In addition to reasonable foreseeability, pure economic loss cases generally also require vulnerability of the
plaintiff — an inability to protect themselves from the defendant's lack of reasonable care (*Woolcock Street
Investments Pty Ltd v CDG Pty Ltd* (2004) 216 CLR 515, confirmed in *ABN AMRO Bank NV v Bathurst Regional
Council* (2014) 224 FCR 1). Vulnerability is established by considering the factors identified in Hedley Byrne:
known reliance (the speaker knows or should know the other will rely on the information), assumption of
responsibility, and reasonable reliance — the circumstances must be such that it is reasonable for the
recipient to seek, accept and rely on the speaker's utterance, having regard to the nature of the subject
matter, the occasion of the interchange, the identity and relative position of the parties as regards
knowledge, and their capacity to form or exercise judgment (*Shaddock & Associates Pty Ltd v Parramatta City
Council (No 1)* (1981) 150 CLR 225).

:::examtip
For a negligent-misstatement problem, run all four Hedley Byrne factors first, then check vulnerability
separately — a plaintiff who could have protected themselves another way (independent advice, a contractual
warranty) is not vulnerable, however foreseeable the loss.
:::
