## The three steps of creative problem solving

Creative problem solving runs in three steps:

1. **Identify an interesting problem** — find problems, identify needs.
2. **Divergent thinking** — find solutions.
3. **Convergent thinking** — find « the » solution, or a good solution.

The first two steps make up the **prospective phase**; convergent thinking is the **analytical phase**. The slide
draws each step as a funnel: problem identification narrows to a point, divergent thinking opens out, convergent
thinking closes again.

:::definition
**Prospective phase**: identify an interesting problem, then divergent thinking. **Analytical phase**: convergent
thinking — find « the » solution, or a good solution.
:::

## Identify an interesting problem

Identifying meaningful problems, asking the right questions is a key characteristic of creative people…

- "If I had one hour to save the world, I would spend 59 minutes defining the problem and one minute solving it…"
  (attributed on the slide to Albert Einstein)
- "Computers are useless. They can only give answers…" (attributed on the slide to Pablo Picasso)

Some related qualities:

- Listen to the unusual.
- Identify consequences that make the subject essential.

Examples of important questions:

- Why is the speed of light the same everywhere?… led to the discovery of relativity by Einstein.
- How to use colours differently?… one of the essential questions that led to impressionism.
- How to make bus stop shelters viable?… led to the worldwide success of Decaux.

## Divergent thinking — example: the quota method

Always ask at least three ideas from your team (& yourself!):

- different options, alternate solutions;
- or refuse to decide.

When at least three good ideas have been found, many more are generally found!

Break the "linear thinking" propensity to:

- settle for the first idea;
- logical reasoning, that locks you in the first solution.

## Convergent thinking

- Ability to identify benefits and risks.
- Keep the best and eliminate wrong answers.
- Evaluation / selection.
- Positive and critical thinking.

IQ tests measure deductive capacity: deduce the consequences.

## Creativity is a long process

The three steps are also drawn as a cycle: problem identification → divergent thinking → convergent thinking → back to
problem identification.

:::rule
Great advances are usually the result of a long process that is a sum of intuitions and successive progresses.
:::

## Why can't we create a standardised test measuring individual creativity?

- Creativity is a process.
- Creativity is often a group phenomenon.
- Creativity implies domain knowledge.

Each step tests differently:

| Step | What the lecture says about testing it |
| --- | --- |
| Identify an interesting problem | No standard test for ability to formulate interesting problems |
| Divergent thinking | Divergent thinking tests produce mixed results |
| Convergent thinking | IQ tests; emotional intelligence |

**Tests of creativity.** The lecture shows an ambiguous drawing (a duck or a rabbit). How easy was it to see both? Did
it require real mental strain? The ease to flip between the two is related to creativity (Wiseman et al. 2011). Now
let's think about a door: how many uses can you write down in one minute?

## Creativity techniques

The lecture sorts creativity techniques into four families:

| Family | Techniques |
| --- | --- |
| Rational — « serious creativity » | TRIZ (Genrich Altshuller); lateral thinking, 6 hats (Edward De Bono); mindmaps (Tony Buzan) |
| Gaming | Frame games (Thagi); improvisation; innovation games (Luke Hohmann); constructions: LEGOs, blocs… |
| Idea gathering | Brainstorming, brainwriting, method 635, CPS; idea box, idea intranets, participatory innovation; crowdsourcing |
| Irrational | Detour approaches; Synectics (analogical reasoning); random approaches |

## Idea gathering techniques

**Brainstorming, brainwriting methods** (Alex Osborne, president & co-founder of BBDO): in order to answer a question,
generate in a meeting as many ideas as possible in a short time without exercising censorship on the participants.
Analyse and prioritise ideas afterwards.

**Participatory innovation — idea boxes, idea intranets:**

- 1721: the best ideas deposited in an "idea box" (*meyasu bako*) are rewarded (Shôgun Tokugawa Yoshimune).
- Seventies: Japanese companies develop quality circles and « Kaizen ».
- 2000s: idea management software; participatory innovation: open procedures for the collection, analysis and
  application of ideas.
- 2010s: companies seeking new ideas outside with idea crowdsourcing: Crossing, YourEncore, CrowdSpirit, InnoCentive.

## Rational approaches

- **TRIZ** (Genrich Altshuller). He discovers universal laws of technological invention. Each solves a contradiction
  between physical quantities. To innovate, one identifies contradictions, then applies these laws, which provide
  avenues to explore.
- **Lateral thinking, 6 hats** (Edward de Bono). "Lateral thinking" escapes from the linear thinking, for example by
  introducing a random component in reasoning. The "six hats" are a means to materialise the most common reasoning
  attitudes.
- **Mindmaps** (Tony Buzan). Means to visually represent ideas, facts… Mindmaps are used to summarise existing
  knowledge, gather ideas (brainstorming), or organise tasks or information (meeting management…).

## « Non rational » techniques

**Analog techniques** shift the problem with analogies, and explore them — Synectics (William Gordon): "Making the
familiar strange, making the strange familiar." Three types of analogies:

- Direct: natural (animal, plant), or graphic.
- Symbolic: parable, metaphor.
- Fantasy: dream, daydream.

Personal approach: personal identification with the situation.

**Detour techniques**: one distances themself from the problem and then they return to it to converge. The distance
stimulates imagination. Idea production follows.

- *Distanciation phase.* Principle: distort and transform the problem into an evocation that stimulates the
  imagination. Approaches: deformation, forced encounters, dreamlike techniques, projective graphics…
- *Ideation phase.* 1/ Blanks fuzzy, ambiguous ideas; 2/ Scanning / crossover: one follows the track to push the idea
  as far as possible until it becomes useful. Principle: turn ideas into positive — "To make it work, it would have
  to…"

## 4 methods useful for marketing & sales

| Method | What it is | Usage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mindmaps (Tony Buzan) | Represent information (ideas or facts) in the form of a diagram called "mental map" | Synthesis of information / collaborative brainstorming… |
| Innovation games (Luke Hohmann) | Fun activities rather than games; they aim to collect, organise and prioritise ideas | Product roadmap, identifying growth drivers… |
| Lateral thinking, 6 hats (Edward de Bono) | Approach to overcome the limitations of the "linear, logic thinking" while remaining rational | Problem solving, product ideas… |
| Brainstorming (Alex Osborne, BBDO) | Collection of ideas to produce a maximum of ideas which are then analysed and sorted | Product ideas, solutions to a problem, names, slogans… |

## What are mind maps?

- A mind map is a way to visually organise notes, thoughts, ideas, and conversations.
- Tony Buzan (a British psychologist) takes the credit for inventing modern mind mapping. In reality different forms
  of mind mapping have been used over many centuries — Buzan really just popularised it and offered some theories
  around why they work so well to capture thoughts and are compatible with the thought process.
- A mind map is created around a single word or text, placed in the centre, to which associated ideas, words and
  concepts are added.
- Mind maps have many uses, including business situations: document conversations, general note taking,
  brainstorming, or to tweeze out a strategy or a particularly complicated situation you may face.
- In business mind maps are most frequently used collaboratively — but it is possible to use mind mapping on your
  own, as an individual.

## Mind map guidelines

In his books on Mind Maps Buzan suggests the following guidelines for creating them:

1. Start in the centre of a blank page turned as landscape — it gives your brain freedom to spread out in all
   directions and express itself freely and naturally.
2. Use an image or picture for your central idea — an image is worth a 1000 words and helps you use your imagination;
   a central image is more interesting, keeps you focused, helps you concentrate.
3. Use colours throughout, three colours at minimum — colours are as exciting to your brain as are images; colour adds
   extra vibrancy and life to your mind map, and energy to your creative thinking.
4. Connect your main branches to the central image, and your second and third level branches to the first and second
   levels — your brain works by associations; it likes to link two, three or four things together. If you connect the
   branches, you will understand and remember a lot more easily.
5. Make your branches curved, rather than straight lined — straight lines are boring to your brain.

The remaining five:

6. Use one key word per line — a single word gives your mind map more power and flexibility. Use upper and lower case.
7. Use images throughout — each image gives another 1000 words. Each word/image is best alone and sitting on its own
   line.
8. The lines should be connected, starting from the central image. The central lines are thicker, organic and thinner
   as they radiate out from the centre.
9. Make the lines the same length as the word/image they support.
10. Develop your own personal style of Mind Mapping.

The lecture's own mind map of the guidelines groups them as Centre (start; image of topic; at least 3 colours),
Keywords (print; case: UPPER and lower), lines (organised: central thicker and more important, outer thinner and less
important; style: organic, free flowing; length the same as the word or image; connect: centre, radiate out; for each:
word, image, alone), Use (links, colours, emphasis, images, codes, dimension), Clarity (hierarchy, order, outlines)
and Style (personal, develop).

## Tutorial: mind mapping

"Business people don't just need to understand designers better, they need to become designers." — Roger Martin,
Dean, Rotman School of Management.

**Windows exercise (individual).** How many windows in your home? Now draw a mind map of how you went calculating the
number of windows. This exercise demonstrates how we think in radial associations. The debrief: you most likely
formed a mental picture of your home, and used it to create a map that you navigated from the exterior or interior —
the size of the windows and rooms, the window coverings, the hallways and doors, even the colour of the walls and
where the furniture is (and from outside, the driveway, the landscaping and the weather — most likely a sunny day).
You didn't need a calculator: you mentally navigated from a starting point, adding windows along the way until you
returned to where you started. You were creating associations to solve a problem. We think like a radial map ("How
Many Windows?" at the centre), not like a list (Bedrooms 6 + Kitchen 1 + Family Room 2 + Garage 1 + Living Room 2 +
Sun Room 4 = 16).

**Group exercise — time management.** In groups of 4–5 students, 15 minutes to draw a Time Management mind map, then
display and explain to the class what you have linked. Make it visually appealing, and note what happened in the group
whilst undertaking the mind map.

**Critique the mind map.** Using the Time Management map: is it effective? Are there too many branches, is it
distracting? Is the focus on the images and not the words? What should it be — does it matter? Does the centre image
take too much attention? How could you improve this mind map?
