## Recall: why the relationship is a question at all

Sovereign equality of states: each state can make its own decisions about how to order its affairs, with the
result that one state may not force its will upon others (Rose 2022). International law is a voluntary
consent-based system: states have the authority or capacity to limit the exercise of their sovereign power by
consenting to be bound by international legal rules. It is a horizontal rather than vertical system of authority:
there is no superior authority, so states are both the makers and the enforcers of international law. And
"international law is everywhere": international law has expanded, with increasing penetration of domestic legal
orders. What does this all mean for the relationship between national laws and the international laws?

## The international law perspective

From an international law perspective, a state cannot justify a breach of international law based on its internal
law. The absence of a law or a contradictory provision in the domestic law of a state is no justification for
violation of international law.

:::rule
"A party may not invoke the provisions of its internal law as justification for its failure to perform a treaty…"
(art 27 VCLT).
:::

:::rule
A state may not invoke the fact that its consent to be bound by a treaty has been expressed in violation of a
provision of its internal law regarding competence to conclude treaties as invalidating its consent "unless that
violation was manifest and concerned a rule of its internal law of fundamental importance". "A violation is
manifest if it would be objectively evident to any State conducting itself in the matter in accordance with normal
practice and in good faith" (art 46 VCLT).
:::

Expanding beyond treaties to rules of international law in general, the important provision is art 3 of the
International Law Commission's Draft Articles on State Responsibility (2001). The draft articles are a product of
the International Law Commission, but we bother with them because they mostly represent, or have been recognised
as representing, customary international law.

:::rule
"The characterization of an act of a State as internationally wrongful is governed by international law. Such
characterization is not affected by the characterization of the same act as lawful by internal law" (ILC Draft
Articles on State Responsibility (2001) art 3).
:::

The relationship from the perspective of international law is simple: national law is (mostly) irrelevant in the
consideration of international law obligations, and state responsibility for internationally wrongful acts. Yet
the domestic law perspective is more complicated.

## National law remains relevant to international law

- International law obligations regularly require the enactment of national legislation and action — therefore
  national law is the fulfilment of international law obligations; national law is usually seen as a vehicle
  through which international obligations and rights are brought into the domestic legal system.
- National law can act as a source of factual evidence about a state's compliance or non-compliance with
  international law, and can serve as evidence for the existence of both state practice and opinio juris when
  determining customary international law.
- National court decisions can be subsidiary sources of international law (art 38 of the Statute of the
  International Court of Justice).
- National law can offer interpretation of international law, and can help us identify certain general principles
  of law.

## Two theories: monism and dualism

**Monism** sees national and international law as part of one single normative legal order. International law has
direct effect (incorporation) and holds the same legal authority as national law in the legal system, so courts
are able to use international law directly. Inconsistency between the two legal systems must be resolved
(frequently in favour of international law). Monism is often associated with the civil law tradition — mostly
continental Europe.

**Dualism** sees international and national law as distinct legal orders. The only law that has force at the
domestic level is national law — therefore international law must be transformed into domestic law to have effect
(transformation). Inconsistencies do not arise: the systems have different subjects and fulfil different purposes,
and usually national law will have priority. Dualism is often associated with the common law tradition — the UK,
Australia, Canada, and so on.

:::examtip
These are ideal-type theories: in reality, blends of the two theories characterise the relationship of
international law to most domestic legal orders, with some states leaning more toward dualism and some more toward
monism. Tutorial question 1 asks which one best reflects Australia's approach — primarily dualist.
:::

:::statute
German Basic Law art 25 (primacy of international law): "The general rules of international law shall be an
integral part of federal law. They shall take precedence over the laws and directly create rights and duties for
the inhabitants of the federal territory." A very clear example of a monist system: no legislative step is
required to integrate international law.
:::

:::statute
Dutch Constitution (1983): art 90 — the Government shall promote the development of the international rule of law;
art 91(1) — the Kingdom shall not be bound by treaties, nor shall such treaties be denounced, without the prior
approval of the Parliament; art 91(3) — treaty provisions that conflict with the Constitution may be approved only
by at least two-thirds of the votes cast; art 93 — provisions of treaties and of resolutions by international
institutions which may be binding on all persons by virtue of their contents become binding after they have been
published; art 94 — statutory regulations in force within the Kingdom are not applicable if that application is
in conflict with such treaty provisions or resolutions. A more nuanced approach that mixes dualism and monism:
Parliament's approval is still required, but no law replicating the treaty is needed, and a conflicting internal
law gives way.
:::

## Basic approach to international law in Australia

The Law Society of NSW, *The Practitioner's Guide to International Law* (2nd ed, 2014) sets out the basic approach:

1. International law as such does not form part of Australian law.
2. Effect is first and foremost given to Australian law.
3. Australian courts may refer to international law.
4. In the absence of any contrary indications, Australian law is to be interpreted consistently with Australia's
   international obligations.
5. The international obligations applicable to Australia are generally to be identified as they exist at the time
   the interpretive question arises — so a treaty that will enter into force tomorrow cannot serve as a basis for
   interpretation for a case that is arising today.
6. In the event of conflict, international law cannot be invoked to override clear and valid Australian legal
   provisions.
7. Parliament may legislate on matters in breach of international law, thereby "taking the risk of international
   complications".
8. Australian courts should give special attention to protecting human rights and fundamental freedoms as
   recognised under international law.

:::examtip
The international law perspective (international law prevails) is in clear contradiction with the national law
perspective (Australian law prevails). A decision or statute that contradicts international law can still be valid
under Australian law — but that does not mean there will be no repercussion for the state under international law.
Something being legal in Australia does not mean it will be considered legal at the international level.
:::

## International law and the Australian Constitution

Three key sections:

- Section 51(xxix), known as the external affairs power, grants federal Parliament the power to enact legislation
  with respect to "external affairs".
- Section 61: the power to conclude treaties is vested in the executive branch of government.
- Section 75(i) vests the High Court with original jurisdiction in relation to "matters arising under a treaty".

The Constitution does not address how Australia enters into binding relationships on the international stage, the
legal effect of international law within the domestic legal system, or responsibility for enforcement at the
domestic level.

Section 51(xxix) "is intended to enable Australia to carry out its functions as an international person, fulfilling
its international obligations and acting effectively as a member of the community of nations" (Murphy J in *New
South Wales v Commonwealth* [1975]). It is relevant particularly when determining when the federal government has
power to enact legislation that might otherwise fall to the powers of the states — which is why the power was
controversial in a federation. The scope of valid use of the external affairs power includes, among others,
legislation that:

- carries out or gives effect to Australia's international treaty obligations;
- gives effect to Australia's obligations under customary international law;
- has a subject matter that may affect Australia's relations with international persons, including states and
  international organisations;
- is related to a matter of "international concern" (*Koowarta v Bjelke-Petersen* [1982] HCA 27).

The Commonwealth has broad discretion when exercising the external affairs power.

:::case
*Commonwealth v Tasmania* [1983] 158 CLR 1 (Tasmanian Dam Case) — in 1974 Australia became party to the World
Heritage Convention; the Commonwealth passed legislation to protect part of Tasmania's wilderness from the building
of a dam on the Gordon River, and Tasmania challenged its validity. The High Court majority held that even though
the dam would normally be a matter for Tasmania, in light of Australia's international obligations the
Commonwealth was entitled to intervene: being a party to such an agreement directly relates to external affairs.
However, if the Commonwealth's use of the external affairs power was no more than a device to exercise legislative
power, it would probably not be legitimate.
:::

## Treaties and Australian law

Treaties do not have a direct effect in Australia: treaty provisions need to be validly incorporated into
Australian domestic law. There is a very limited class of treaties that are directly applicable without
legislative implementation, e.g. certain peace treaties, boundary treaties, etc.

:::case
*Dietrich v The Queen* (1992) 177 CLR 292 — the appellant claimed he should have had access to publicly funded
representation and so was denied a fair trial; the ICCPR, to which Australia is party, guarantees the right to a
fair trial and free representation in certain cases (art 14(3)(d)). "Ratification of the ICCPR as an executive act
has no direct legal effect upon domestic law; the rights and obligations contained in the ICCPR are not
incorporated into Australian law unless and until specific legislation is passed implementing the provisions. No
such legislation has been passed" (Mason CJ and McHugh J).
:::

Transformation of treaties into domestic law can happen by:

1. Adoption of new legislation that gives effect to the treaty provisions (reproducing all or part of the text).
2. Amendment of existing legislation — as happened with the Criminal Code, amended to give effect to certain
   provisions of the Genocide Convention.
3. A schedule of a treaty to an existing Act (this does not constitute full incorporation).
4. The treaty provision already being given effect in existing legislation — Australia does not need to do
   anything.
5. Giving the treaty the force of law, e.g. *Diplomatic Privileges and Immunities Act 1967* s 7, which gives effect
   to certain articles of the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations.

## International law and statutory interpretation

- Where a statute directly incorporates the text of a treaty, the statute should be interpreted in line with the
  meaning given to it in the treaty unless the legislator expressed a contrary intention (*Applicant A v Minister
  for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs* (1997) 190 CLR 225).
- A treaty can assist in the interpretation of Australian legislation (*Acts Interpretation Act 1901* (Cth)
  s 15AB(2)(d)), but only if (a) there was actual or contemplated participation by Australia in that treaty prior
  to enactment of the legislation in question; and (b) it is used to confirm the ordinary meaning of the text of the
  Act (s 15AB(1)(a)), or the legislation is ambiguous or obscure, or would otherwise lead to a result that is
  manifestly absurd or is unreasonable (s 15AB(1)(b)).
- However, a treaty cannot qualify or modify the meaning of legislative words or expressions which are otherwise
  clear (*Yager v The Queen* [1977] 139 CLR 28 (Mason J)).

Two common law presumptions:

:::rule
In a case of ambiguity in a statute, the courts should favour a construction that accords with international law
(*Jumbunna Coal Mine NL v Victoria Coal Miners' Association* (1908) 6 CLR 309). It is a presumption only, and does
not apply to clear legislation contrary to international law (*Polites v Commonwealth* (1945) 70 CLR 60).
:::

:::rule
Principle of legality: Australian courts will not impute to Parliament an intention to abrogate or curtail certain
human rights or fundamental freedoms, including rights and privileges recognised under the common law, unless such
an intention is clearly manifested by unambiguous and unmistakable language (*Coco v The Queen* (1994) 179 CLR 427,
437).
:::

## Customary international law: transformation or incorporation?

Recall: customary international law is formed when there is uniform/widespread and consistent state practice across
a wide range of states and where there is evidence that this practice is maintained out of a sense of legal
obligation (opinio juris). What is its effect on Australian law? The position here is less settled than it is for
treaties. Debate surrounds two approaches:

- **The doctrine of transformation** — customary international law only becomes part of Australian law when
  formally adopted by Parliament or the courts as part of common law.
- **The doctrine of incorporation** — customary international law is automatically a source of Australian law,
  unless there is a rule to the contrary.

Australia has not clearly adopted either approach — both doctrines have been recognised in High Court decisions.
But a rule of customary international law cannot generally be adopted where inconsistent with domestic legislation
or essential common law doctrine.

:::case
*Nulyarimma v Thompson* [1999] 96 FCR 153 — members of an Aboriginal community claimed that sponsorship of Native
Title Act amendments and failure to seek World Heritage listing of the Lake Eyre region were acts of genocide, and
argued that, as genocide is part of customary international law, Australian courts could try individuals even
without legislation criminalising genocide. The Federal Court ruled that even if genocide is recognised as part of
customary international law, it does not form part of Australian law in the absence of domestic legislation
declaring genocide a crime; it accepted that the prohibition of genocide is a jus cogens norm, but without
implementing legislation it had no effect on Australian law.
:::

Wilcox J (at [25]): "[I]t is one thing … for courts of a particular country to be prepared to treat a civil law rule
like the doctrine of foreign sovereign immunity as part of its domestic law … It is another thing to say that a norm
of international law criminalising conduct that is not made punishable by the domestic law entitles a domestic court
to try and punish an offender against that law." Because the rule was a rule of criminal law, it definitely needed
to be transformed.

Merkel J, in dissent, identified criteria for customary international law to become a source of common law — points
since cited by other judges:

- the customary rule must have "attained … general acceptance by … the community of nations";
- the courts must consider whether the rule has been "received into and so become a source of" Australian law, so it
  must be "not inconsistent with rules enacted by statutes or finally declared by [the courts]";
- inconsistent rules may only be enacted by legislation;
- once received, a rule of custom is to be treated as a true declaration of the common law;
- the reception into domestic law will only be taken to have occurred "as from the date the particular rule … has
  been established".

## International law and the common law

There is a broadly accepted view that international law serves as a legitimate influence for the development of the
common law — the common law may have "to march in step with international law in order to provide the body of law
to apply" (Brennan J in *Mabo v Queensland (No 2)* [1992] 175 CLR 1). More narrowly, international law is viewed as
filling lacunae in the existing common law: "Judicial development of the common law must not be seen as a back door
means of importing an unincorporated convention into Australian law" (Mason CJ and Deane J in *Minister for
Immigration and Ethnic Affairs v Teoh* [1995] 183 CLR 273, 288).

## Interpretation of the Constitution?

Can international law assist in the interpretation of the Constitution? There is no majority support in the High
Court. The Constitution sits above all statutes in the Australian legal order and can only be modified by
referendum.

- Kirby J has espoused it in several cases: "[I]nternational law is a legitimate and important influence on the
  development of the common law and constitutional law, especially when international law declares the existence of
  universal and fundamental rights. To the full extent that its text permits, Australia's Constitution … accommodates
  itself to international law … [It] also speaks to the international community as the basic law of the Australian
  nation which is a member of that community" (*Newcrest Mining (WA) Ltd v Commonwealth* [1997] 190 CLR 513).
- McHugh J in *Al-Kateb v Godwin* [2004] 219 CLR 562: use of international law for constitutional interpretation is
  "heretical"; it amounts to amending the Constitution without a public referendum; and international law rules are
  qualitatively different from taking into account political, social and economic developments.

The High Court has been "very cautious in its embrace of international law; it has kept its gloves and hat on at all
times" … international law "unsettles and challenges many of the rigidities and limitations of Australian law"
(Hilary Charlesworth).

## Conclusion on Part A

Australia is primarily a dualist system. The interaction of Australia's domestic legal system with international
law reflects the separation of powers. The executive (and many members of the legislature) have shown a shifting
attitude to international law — supportive of international trade law, but sceptical about the applicability of
international human rights law to Australia (reflected in not fully implementing many treaty obligations): preserving
Australian sovereignty is at the core of Australian unease about accepting international obligations. Members of the
judiciary hold contrasting views on international law, and these in turn create considerable uncertainty on the
position of international law within the Australian legal system.

## Part B: jurisdiction — meaning and sovereignty

:::definition
Jurisdiction: "…the lawful power of a State to define and enforce the rights and duties, and control the conduct, of
natural and juridical persons" (Oxman, MPEPIL).
:::

In international law, the concept of jurisdiction is linked to the notion of sovereignty — supreme authority within a
territory, subject to international requirements. Sovereignty is not a precondition for statehood; it is a
description of statehood: it describes the attributes of statehood (Besson, "Sovereignty", MPEPIL, paras 1–2).
Sovereignty implies:

- **Sovereign equality** — states are regarded as equal in international law regardless of their size, population or
  wealth.
- **Jurisdiction** — states have authority to create laws and enforce them.
- **Territorial control** — the freedom to use and dispose of the territory under the state's jurisdiction (for
  instance, leasing it, as Cuba did with Guantanamo) and to perform activities deemed necessary to the population.
  Territory = land, the territorial sea up to 12 nautical miles from the coast, and the airspace above land and sea.
- **Duty of non-intervention** — no other state should intrude into the state's territory: ius excludendi alios (the
  right to exclude others).
- **Sovereign immunity** — immunity from the jurisdiction of foreign courts for acts performed by the state in its
  sovereign capacity.
- **Diplomatic immunity** — state representatives acting in their official capacity are immune from the legal system
  of the state in which they are operating.

## Forms of jurisdiction

- **Jurisdiction to prescribe** (prescriptive or legislative jurisdiction): the power of a state to apply its national
  law to any person or property — "to make its law applicable to the activities, relations, or status of persons, or
  the interests of persons in things, whether by legislation, by executive act or order, by administrative rule or
  regulation, or by determination by a court" (American Law Institute, *Restatement (Third) Foreign Relations Law of
  the United States* (1987) § 401(a)).
- **Jurisdiction to enforce** (enforcement jurisdiction): the right of a state to put its laws into operation, e.g.
  arrest, seizure, freezing of assets and imposing fines. "Enforcement means the exercise of jurisdiction by the
  government of a state, namely, the executive branch of government … responsible for the enforcement of regulations
  and the maintenance of public order" (Rose et al, 102).
- **Jurisdiction to adjudicate** (adjudicative jurisdiction): the power of domestic courts to hear and make a binding
  decision on a legal dispute — including power to apply the law to the facts of a case, interpret legal provisions,
  and determine the rights and obligations of the parties.

:::case
*The Case of the SS Lotus (France v Turkey)* (PCIJ 1927) — on prescription: "Far from laying down a general
prohibition to the effect that States may not extend the application of their laws and the jurisdiction of their
courts to persons, property and acts outside their territory, [international law] leaves them in this respect a wide
measure of discretion which is only limited in certain cases by prohibitive rules; as regards other cases, every
State remains free to adopt the principles which it regards as best and most suitable" (p 19). On enforcement: "The
first and foremost restriction imposed by international law upon a state is that — failing the existence of a
permissive rule to the contrary — it may not exercise its power in any form in the territory of another State" (p 18).
:::

States are in principle free to exercise prescriptive jurisdiction as they please, unless a prohibitive rule to the
contrary can be identified (e.g. universal human rights). Enforcement jurisdiction can be exercised within the
territory almost without restriction, but it is one of the most intrusive forms of jurisdiction: unauthorised
enforcement jurisdiction within another state is illegal — a serious violation of that state's sovereignty and
territorial integrity. Permission can be given beforehand by treaty, or after a situation has happened by agreement.

:::case
Eichmann (1960) — Israeli agents (a Mossad team) apprehended Nazi officer Adolf Eichmann in Argentina and smuggled him
out without the consent of the Argentine authorities. The UN Security Council responded to the unlawful Israeli
incursion by requesting Israel to offer compensation (eventually done through an apology). Eichmann was tried in
Israel for crimes against humanity and war crimes and executed in 1962.
:::

:::case
Lockerbie (1988) — Pan Am Flight 103, departing Heathrow for New York, exploded over Lockerbie, Scotland: 259
passengers and crew were killed, and falling wreckage destroyed 21 houses and killed 11 people on the ground. A large
proportion of passengers were American. The device was dispatched from Malta by Libyan nationals, passed through
Frankfurt, and was loaded at Heathrow. The UK asserted jurisdiction on an objective territorial basis (the bomb
exploded in UK airspace); the US and UK both asked for extradition; Libya asserted jurisdiction over its nationals and
refused to extradite, citing fair-trial concerns, but was under substantial international pressure, particularly UN
sanctions. Under the UK/Netherlands Agreement Concerning a Scottish Trial in the Netherlands 1998, a special Scottish
court sat at Camp Zeist, with Scottish judges applying Scottish law in a foreign country.
:::

:::examtip
The three forms illustrated with theft: prescriptive jurisdiction — the domestic criminalisation of theft (in
Australia, s 131 of the Criminal Code); enforcement jurisdiction — the physical apprehension of a suspected thief,
custody on remand while awaiting sentence, and imposing a sentence of imprisonment; adjudicative jurisdiction — the
criminal prosecution of the individual.
:::

## Grounds for asserting jurisdiction

To lawfully assert prescriptive jurisdiction in cases involving a "foreign element", a state must justify the
grounds for jurisdiction: the principle of territoriality; the nationality principle (active nationality), with the
principle of passive personality; the principle of protection; and the principle of universality.

### Territorial jurisdiction

The fundamental basis of jurisdiction, derived from state sovereignty. A state has jurisdiction over all acts, whether
criminal or not, committed on its territory and over everyone located on the territory of that state — citizenship is
not required. Subject to limitations imposed by international law, e.g. human rights law, a state can legislate as it
pleases on whatever matter it desires on its own territory. (If a Greek woman living in Greece assaults her Australian
partner there, Greece can straightforwardly assert jurisdiction on the basis of territoriality — and it would equally
have jurisdiction if the Australian partner were the assailant, because he is in Greek territory.)

Sometimes a sequence of events does not occur entirely within one state: a criminal offence may be initiated or
planned in one state but committed in another ("cross-border/transnational crimes"). Two approaches:

1. **Objective territoriality** focuses on the effects of an offence: a state has jurisdiction over a criminal offence
   that is completed on its territory even if some constituent elements of the offence took place abroad.
2. **Subjective territoriality**: a state has jurisdiction over all criminal offences as long as they are initiated or
   planned on its territory.

Both are important in fighting transnational crime, such as international cybercrime.

### Nationality (active personality)

There is no accepted definition of nationality in international law: states are generally free to decide who they
consider to be their nationals and under what circumstances an individual qualifies for citizenship ("domaine
réservé") — the two most important forms of acquisition being ius soli (birth in the territory) and ius sanguinis
(the nationality of your parents). Ships and aircraft have the nationality of the state where they are registered.

:::case
*Nottebohm* (ICJ, 1955) — according to state practice, nationality is "a legal bond having as its basis a social fact
of attachment, a genuine connection of existence, interests and sentiments, together with the existence of reciprocal
rights and duties".
:::

The nationality principle is relevant where a state of nationality may wish to criminally prosecute a national for
acts committed abroad. If an individual has dual nationality, both states of nationality may assert jurisdiction on
the basis of nationality. Many states in continental Europe tend to extend their criminal law to their nationals
abroad; common law countries tend to be more restrictive. In Australia, for instance, a crime that is extended to
nationals abroad is the crime of having sexual relations with people under the age of 16.

### Passive personality

A state can assert its jurisdiction over an offence committed abroad on the sole ground that the victim of the offence
was a national of the state — here we look at the nationality of the victim, not of the offender.

:::case
*US v Yunis* (1988, US District Court for the District of Columbia) — Yunis, a Lebanese national, was involved in the
1985 hijacking of Royal Jordanian flight 402 with American citizens aboard. The US claimed jurisdiction over the
non-US citizen for crimes (hostage taking) committed outside the United States, arguing his actions were against
American citizens; Yunis was captured in international waters by the FBI and brought to the US, and the court found
the US had jurisdiction.
:::

The passive personality principle remains controversial — it is not universally accepted as a solid ground in the
same way as territoriality and active nationality, and is better as a mere subsidiary means of jurisdiction, combined
with another ground. There is a fairness argument: if you injure an American national in a fight while on holiday in
Spain, you would think you were governed by Spanish law, not liable under US law.

### Protective jurisdiction

Allows a state to claim jurisdiction over actions committed abroad by non-citizens that threaten the nation's
security. It requires the existence of a genuine threat to a vital state security interest — e.g. counterfeiting
currency (or national documents like passports), espionage, conspiracy to commit treason (coup d'état), and attack on
diplomatic personnel abroad. There may be debate about what constitutes a vital interest: the US has claimed
protective jurisdiction over the illegal drug trade, but many states do not follow it and have instead extended their
jurisdiction through treaties.

### Universal jurisdiction

Certain offences are so serious and disruptive to the international community as a whole that any state can claim
jurisdiction over them no matter where they have been committed, who they have been committed against or the
nationality of the offender — there is a lack of a direct link between the state and the offence. One justification
is preventing impunity. Originally, it was the crime of piracy (committed on the high seas, outside the jurisdiction
of all states) that gave rise to the notion of universal jurisdiction; today it also covers genocide, war crimes,
crimes against humanity and torture. Universal jurisdiction is based on customary international law, and it is really
only after World War II that the right and even the duty of states to exercise it was fully recognised.

:::statute
Geneva Convention (IV) art 146: "The High Contracting Parties undertake to enact any legislation necessary to provide
effective penal sanctions for persons committing, or ordering to be committed, any of the grave breaches of the
present Convention … Each High Contracting Party shall be under the obligation to search for persons alleged to have
committed, or to have ordered to be committed, such grave breaches, and shall bring such persons, regardless of their
nationality, before its own courts. It may also, if it prefers, and in accordance with the provisions of its own
legislation, hand such persons over for trial to another High Contracting Party concerned, provided such High
Contracting Party has made out a prima facie case." Article 147 defines the grave breaches — including wilful
killing, torture or inhuman treatment, unlawful deportation or confinement of a protected person, taking of hostages,
and extensive destruction and appropriation of property not justified by military necessity.
:::

To exercise universal jurisdiction when no treaty exists establishing it is controversial, so states usually require a
territorial link in their legislation: the alleged perpetrator must be present on their territory. In Australia,
Division 268 of the *Criminal Code Act 1995* concerns genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity, and usually the
person will need to be present to be prosecuted. The number of cases initiated and defendants tried under universal
jurisdiction has risen considerably over the past two decades (Langer et al) — because of domestic legislation
expressly providing for it, special units within domestic law enforcement, and NGOs documenting international crimes
and helping with the collection of evidence.

## Concurring jurisdiction

Since states can assert jurisdiction in respect of a given person or matter on multiple grounds, it is not uncommon
for more than one state to claim jurisdiction in any given situation (as in Lockerbie). There are no universally
binding rules on how to resolve issues of concurring jurisdiction. International treaties provide clearer pathways:
the 2001 Cybercrime Convention provides that when more than one state claims jurisdiction over an offence under the
Convention, the states involved shall "consult with a view to determining the most appropriate jurisdiction for
prosecution" (art 22(5)) — it does not stipulate that one ground has priority over another. In most cases, the state
with the closest link to a particular offence is in the best position to investigate and try the offenders; practical
considerations (where is the evidence? where is the offender?) influence the decision, so you have to evaluate case by
case.

:::examtip
Tutorial hypotheticals: poisoned olive oil sent from Greece kills a man in London (objective territoriality for the
UK, subjective territoriality and nationality for Greece); an Australian injures two Americans in Bali (territoriality
for Indonesia, active nationality for Australia, passive personality — controversial — for the US); an XWZ member who
tortured civilians in State A flees to Germany (universal jurisdiction over crimes against humanity and torture, with
his presence in Germany as the territorial link).
:::
