## Introducing reflection

The lecture opens with an example from *Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire* (JK Rowling, 2000), in which Dumbledore
(headteacher and Chief Wizard) talks to Harry about having excess thoughts. Dumbledore sometimes has too many thoughts
and memories crammed into his mind. At those times he uses the Pensieve, a stone basin: he siphons the excess thoughts
from his mind, pours them into the basin and examines them at his leisure, because in that form "it becomes easier to
spot patterns and links".

In the recording the lecturer draws the parallel: we have too many thoughts, ideas and memories in our heads, and
reflective writing is the container we put them into. Putting them there helps us spot patterns in our thinking, link
different ideas together and think them through.

## Why reflect?

- It is not sufficient to merely have experience in order to learn. Without reflecting it may be quickly forgotten or
  its learning potential lost.
- Moon (1999) suggests reflection is "a form of mental processing with a purpose".
- The slide adds a quotation attributed to John Dewey: "We do not learn from experience… we learn from reflecting on
  experience."

:::definition
**Reflection** is "a form of mental processing with a purpose" (Moon, 1999).
:::

The lecturer adds why reflection gets less attention today: we are always distracted by phones, tablets, computers and
social media, and constantly bombarded with information. We have lots of experiences, but we don't take the time to
sit down and reflect on them, so we don't really learn from them.

## Purposes of reflection

1. Consider the process of our own learning — a process of recognition
2. Critically review something — our own behaviour, that of others etc
3. Build on theory from observation
4. Engage in personal or self development
5. Make decisions or resolve uncertainty

In respect to your self-reflective writing, it relates to your group experiences, theory on the innovative project you
presented and how it may have affected the type and outcome of the project.

In the recording the lecturer glosses the list: recognition is step one (we sit down and recognise that we want to learn
something from this experience); critical review means looking critically at our own behaviour or other people's;
building theory means we observe and come up with an explanation for what we observe; and, based on our reflection, we
can make decisions. Ask what you learnt, how you could have done things differently, how others could have done things
differently, and how you would do it next time.

## Reveal information wisely

- A reflective paper is somewhat personal in that it includes your subjective feelings and opinions. Instead of
  revealing everything about yourself, carefully ask yourself if something is appropriate before including it in your
  writing.
- If you feel uncomfortable about a reflection, don't include it unless it did change the final outcome of your
  experience. In your writing, indicate the concerns you have about such reveals.
- Always maintain a professional and academic tone. Yes, it is personal and subjective, but keep your thoughts in an
  organised fashion so they appear sensible.
- Avoid dragging someone else down in your writing. Whilst that person may well have made life difficult for you, you
  must keep a level of detachment as you describe their influence.

:::rule
Uncomfortable about a reflection? Leave it out, unless it changed the final outcome of your experience; if you include
it, say in your writing what concerns you have about revealing it.
:::

The lecturer adds that it is fine not to share a personal feeling you are uncomfortable with; that you might jot down
some notes first, perhaps as free writing, and then organise them into a more structured piece; and that you should not
badmouth or accuse other people.

## How can I write a reflective paper?

- It is one of the few academic papers where you can use "I" in your writing.
- You should still relate your subjective feelings and opinions using specific evidence to explain them.
- Avoid using slang, jargon etc and always proofread.
- It is NOT a personal journal (diary) entry.
- Keep your sentences focused; don't ramble. Avoid squeezing multiple ideas into one sentence.
- Avoid sentence fragments.
- Vary your sentence length, but not too long; it makes it more conversational.
- Use paragraphs.

In the recording the lecturer says "I" is acceptable in other academic writing too (journal authors write "I found");
that feelings should not come "out of the blue" — think critically about the experience and give specific evidence; and
that the paper is still academic writing in paragraphs, not a stream of consciousness.

### Transitional phrases

Transitional phrases shift the argument and introduce specific details. They allow you to illustrate how one experience
or detail directly links to a conclusion or understanding. Common transitional phrases include: *for example*, *for
instance*, *as a result*, *an opposite view is*, *a different perspective is*.

You must relate this to your group project and experiences, with theoretical underpinnings.

## Questions to ask myself

- What have I learnt from the process?
- What do I now know, that I didn't before?
- What insights have I gained?
- What were the challenges that I encountered/observed, and how well did I and the team handle them?
- What would I do better or differently next time?
- What have I learnt from this?

:::examtip
Remember: ultimately do not just describe; you must analyse and reflect. Review the criteria within the group project
and keep the marking rubric in mind.
:::

## Tutorial: the Floor Company Exercise

The tutorial is there to think about and practise convergent and divergent thinking for creativity and innovation.

You are the new product development team for the Boring, Drab and Awful Floor Company. Your job is to develop 100 new
floor covering product ideas in 10 minutes. Avoid just adding an adjective like "soft" or "blue". Use the creativity
tool. Have fun and create energy! (The slide is illustrated with photographs of unusual printed floors: an underwater
scene with dolphins, and a walkway printed with an aerial view of fields and a river.)

### A creativity tool

- **Try to** — slide it, dissolve it, compress it, roll it, stir it, shake it, spread it, squeeze it, separate it, blend
  it, slice it, melt it, decrease it, increase it, stack it, inflate it, divide it, pry it, clamp it.
- **Make it** — transparent, movable, immovable, sticky, flexible, inflexible, spin, bounce, rotate, remove, hot, cold,
  slippery, stretch, pop up, fly, flicker, foamy, explode.
- **Add or subtract** — springs, elastic, cohesion, traction, nets, filters, waves, vacuum, fibreglass, rubber, tension,
  wedges, dials, gears, spirals, turbulence, energy, power, time.
- **Imagine that you** — can read minds, are the problem, can fly, have a magic wand, rule the world, have unlimited
  money, have a magic genie, can travel in time, have a computer brain, move at the speed of light, can change any
  object, can see into the future, can make yourself smaller.

Debrief: How many ideas do you have? What helped you be more creative? Pick your top 3 innovative ideas. Report one
idea to the rest of the class.

### Patterns of creative thinking

**Diverging**

- defer judgement
- look for lots of ideas
- accept all ideas
- make yourself "stretch"
- take time to "simmer"
- seek combination: bring together odd combinations

**Converging**

- look for what is really new
- what gets people excited, even if not very realistic
- avoid premature closure
- take the risk to look at difficult issues or "sneaky spots"
- develop a sense of "affirmative judgement"
- keep your eyes on your objective
