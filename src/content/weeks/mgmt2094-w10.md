## Agile innovation

Agile innovation is a term used to describe a series of methods which originated in the field of software development.
It involves:

- high-intensity team work (often called scrums), which are self-managed;
- rapid prototyping, testing and learning;
- methods that are more open-ended than traditional project management techniques;
- creativity tools such as design thinking.

There are **two key roles within agile innovation**: the **team leader** and the **process facilitator**.

## What is agile? (tutor slides)

The tutorial opens with a question: how do organisations turn creative ideas into innovations when customer needs and circumstances keep changing? Its opening dilemma: your team has spent two months developing a mobile app for university students. Just before launch, customer research reveals that students don't want several of its key features. Do you (A) continue with the original plan, (B) reconsider priorities based on feedback, or (C) add the requested features while keeping everything originally planned?

:::definition
**Agile** is an approach to managing complex work through collaboration, iterative development, feedback and adaptation.
:::

**The agile cycle**: **prioritise** (choose valuable work) → **deliver** (create usable outcomes) → **review** (gather feedback) → **adapt** (adjust the plan) → **repeat** (keep learning).

Agile does not mean working without a plan. It means planning in shorter cycles and adapting as new information emerges. Read against the cycle, the dilemma's agile response is B: review the feedback, then adapt the plan.

## Embracing agile (Rigby, Sutherland and Takeuchi, 2016)

Agile innovation methods have revolutionised information technology. Over the past 25 to 30 years they have greatly
increased success rates in software development, improved quality and speed to market, and boosted the motivation and
productivity of IT teams. Agile methodologies — new values, principles, practices and benefits, and a radical
alternative to command-and-control-style management — are now spreading across industries and functions and even into
the C-suite: National Public Radio uses them to create new programming, John Deere to develop new machines, Saab to
produce new fighter jets, Intronis in marketing, C.H. Robinson in human resources, and Mission Bell Winery for
everything from wine production to running its senior leadership group.

By taking people out of their functional silos and putting them in self-managed and customer-focused
multidisciplinary teams, agile is accelerating profitable growth and helping to create a new generation of skilled
general managers. But a serious impediment exists: executives who don't really understand agile unwittingly continue
to manage in ways that run counter to it. They launch countless initiatives with urgent deadlines, spread their best
people across too many projects, become overly involved in the work of teams, talk more than listen, overturn team
decisions and add review layers and controls.

Innovation is what agile is all about. It is less useful in routine operations and processes, but most companies now
operate in highly dynamic environments and need innovation in products, services and functional processes.

## The Agile Manifesto: four values

In 2001, 17 software developers (including Jeff Sutherland) met in Snowbird, Utah, to improve on traditional
**'waterfall' development**, in which detailed requirements and execution plans are created up front and then passed
sequentially from function to function. That approach worked in stable environments, but not when software markets
began to change rapidly and unpredictably: specifications were outdated by the time the software was delivered, and
developers felt oppressed by bureaucratic procedures. Their 'Agile Manifesto' proposed four values (as adapted in the
reading):

:::definition
**People over processes and tools.** Build projects around motivated individuals, give them the support they need
and trust them to get the job done; talk face-to-face; keep a sustainable pace.

**Respond to change rather than follow a plan.** Create a vision and plan, but plan only the tasks that won't have
changed by the time you get to them; be happy to learn things that alter your direction, even late.

**Working prototypes over excessive documentation.** Experiment on small parts of the product with a few customers
for short periods; keep what customers like; resolve arguments with experiments rather than endless debates or
appeals to authority.

**Customer collaboration over rigid contracts.** Time to market and cost are paramount, and specifications should
evolve, because customers can seldom predict what they will actually want.
:::

### The four values: manifesto wording and organisational practice

The tutor slides set the manifesto's own wording (Beck et al., 2001, *Manifesto for Agile Software Development*) beside how Rigby, Sutherland and Takeuchi (2016) apply each value in organisational innovation.

| Agile Manifesto (Beck et al., 2001) | Organisational practice (Rigby et al., 2016) |
| --- | --- |
| Individuals and interactions over processes and tools | Empower cross-functional, self-managing teams to collaborate and make decisions. |
| Working software over comprehensive documentation | Deliver usable outcomes through short, iterative development cycles. |
| Customer collaboration over contract negotiation | Involve customers regularly to understand their needs and create value. |
| Responding to change over following a plan | Use feedback and continuous learning to adapt priorities and solutions. |

Discussion question: which value would be hardest to implement in a traditional organisation, and why?

## How agile really works: scrum

Agile is neither anarchy (everybody does what he or she wants) nor 'doing what I say, only faster'. It comes in
several varieties: **scrum** emphasises creative and adaptive teamwork in solving complex problems; **lean
development** focuses on the continual elimination of waste; **kanban** concentrates on reducing lead times and the
amount of work in process. Scrum and its derivatives are employed at least five times as often as the other
techniques. Jeff Sutherland helped develop scrum, inspired in part by 'The New New Product Development Game', a 1986
HBR article coauthored by Hirotaka Takeuchi.

- **The team** is small (usually three to nine people), mostly full-time and cross-functional, with all the skills
  necessary to complete its tasks. It manages itself and is strictly accountable for every aspect of the work.
- **The initiative owner** (also known as a product owner) is ultimately responsible for delivering value to
  customers and to the business. They build a 'portfolio backlog' of promising opportunities (perhaps using design
  thinking or crowdsourcing) and continually and ruthlessly rank-order it. They don't tell the team who should do
  what or how long tasks will take.
- **Sprints**: the team creates a simple road map, plans in detail only those activities that won't change before
  execution, breaks the highest-ranked tasks into small modules, decides how much work to take on, develops a clear
  definition of 'done', and builds working versions of the product in short cycles (less than a month).
- **The process facilitator** (often a trained scrum master) guides the process, protects the team from
  distractions and helps it put its collective intelligence to work.
- **Transparency and testing**: brief daily 'stand-up' meetings review progress and identify roadblocks;
  disagreements are resolved through experimentation and feedback; small working prototypes are tested with a few
  customers for short periods, and if customers get excited a prototype may be released immediately. The team then
  brainstorms ways to improve future cycles and attacks the next top priority.

The documented benefits: higher team productivity and employee satisfaction; less waste from redundant meetings,
repetitive planning, excessive documentation, quality defects and low-value features; better customer engagement and
satisfaction; the most valuable products and features to market faster and more predictably; reduced risk; mutual
trust and respect across disciplines; and senior managers freed from micromanaging for higher-value work.

## The Scrum framework (Scrum Guide)

The tutor slides move from agile principles to a practical framework for organising work, in three levels:

- **Agile** — principles of collaboration, iterative delivery and adaptation.
- **Scrum** — a framework that helps teams organise work into short, structured cycles called sprints.
- **Sprint** — a fixed-length cycle of one month or less in which a team works towards a **Sprint Goal** and produces a **usable increment**.

Scrum is one Agile framework; not all Agile teams use Scrum (Schwaber and Sutherland, 2020, *The Scrum Guide*).

### Roles, events and artifacts

- **Key roles**: the **Product Owner** represents the customer and maximises the value of the product; the **Development Team** is a cross-functional, self-managing team that delivers the work during the sprint; the **Scrum Master** facilitates the process, removes obstacles and helps the team improve.
- **Events (the sprint cycle)**: the **Product Backlog** is the prioritised list of possible work → **Sprint Planning** agrees a Sprint Goal and selects achievable work (typically 2–8 hours) → the **Sprint** develops a usable increment (typically 1–4 weeks), with a **Daily Scrum** of 15 minutes to inspect progress and adapt the plan → the **Sprint Review** inspects outcomes, gathers feedback from stakeholders and adapts the backlog (typically 1–4 hours) → the **Sprint Retrospective** reflects on how the team can improve its work (typically 1–3 hours).
- **Key artifacts**: the **Product Backlog** (prioritised list of features and work, maintained by the Product Owner); the **Sprint Backlog** (selected work for the current sprint, created during Sprint Planning); the **Increment** (a usable product outcome at the end of the sprint).

**Repeat**: after the Sprint Review and Retrospective, the team uses what it has learned to plan the next sprint.

:::examtip
The Scrum Guide's names line up with the reading's: the Product Owner is Rigby et al.'s initiative owner (also known as a product owner), the Scrum Master their process facilitator (often a trained scrum master), the Development Team their self-managing team. Keep the Sprint Review (the product, with stakeholders) apart from the Retrospective (how the team works). Rigby et al. say sprints run less than a month; the Scrum Guide slide says one month or less, typically one to four weeks.
:::

## Where agile does or does not work

Agile is not a panacea. It is most effective and easiest to implement under conditions commonly found in software
innovation: the problem to be solved is complex; solutions are initially unknown, and product requirements will most
likely change; the work can be modularised; close collaboration with end users (and rapid feedback from them) is
feasible; and creative teams will typically outperform command-and-control groups.

| Condition | Favourable | Unfavourable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Market environment | Customer preferences and solution options change frequently. | Market conditions are stable and predictable. |
| Customer involvement | Close collaboration and rapid feedback are feasible; customers know better what they want as the process progresses. | Requirements are clear at the outset and will remain stable; customers are unavailable for constant collaboration. |
| Innovation type | Problems are complex, solutions are unknown and the scope isn't clearly defined; specifications may change; creative breakthroughs and time to market are important; cross-functional collaboration is vital. | Similar work has been done before and solutions are clear; detailed specifications and work plans can be forecast with confidence; problems can be solved sequentially in functional silos. |
| Modularity of work | Incremental developments have value and customers can use them; work can be broken into parts in rapid, iterative cycles; late changes are manageable. | Customers cannot start testing parts of the product until everything is complete; late changes are expensive or impossible. |
| Impact of interim mistakes | They provide valuable learning. | They may be catastrophic. |

These conditions exist for many product development functions, marketing projects, strategic-planning activities,
supply-chain challenges and resource allocation decisions. They are less common in routine operations such as plant
maintenance, purchasing, sales calls and accounting. Because agile requires training, behavioural change and often new
information technologies, executives must decide whether the payoffs justify the effort and expense. Agile also
depends on having a cadre of eager participants: it is better to enlist passionate volunteers than to coerce
resisters.

**Example — OpenView Venture Partners and Intronis.** OpenView's founder, Scott Maxwell, found agile worked well for
strategic planning and marketing, where complex problems can be broken into modules, but not for selling: any sales
call can change a representative's to-do list on the spot. He offered agile training to the firm's portfolio companies
and let them decide. Intronis adopted it in marketing, which had relied on an annual plan built around trade shows; the
team learned to produce a topical webinar in a few days rather than several weeks (a session on CryptoLocker malware
drew 600 registrants, a company record).

## Six practices for leaders

The reading's six practices for leaders who want to capitalise on agile's potential:

1. **Learn how agile really works** — the values, principles and practices, not just the vocabulary of 'sprints' and
   'time boxes'.
2. **Understand where agile does or does not work** — see the conditions above.
3. **Start small and let the word spread** — the most successful introductions start small, often in IT, and each
   success creates passionate evangelists. At **John Deere**, George Tome began applying agile in corporate IT in 2004;
   it spread over several years, and in R&D's Enterprise Advanced Marketing unit a coach who could work with
   non-software people trained the teams. Innovation cycle times fell, in some cases by more than 75%.
4. **Allow 'master' teams to customise their practices** — as in the martial-arts process **shu-ha-ri**, first
   master the proven disciplines (shu), then modify them (ha), then improvise (ri). Stable teams are 60% more
   productive and 60% more responsive to customer input than teams that rotate members. A team that modifies a
   practice should experiment and track the results. **Spotify**'s 'squads' vary their practices — many omit burndown
   charts — because they have tested the changes and found they improve results.
5. **Practise agile at the top** — strategy development, resource allocation, breakthrough innovation and
   collaboration suit agile. **Systematic**'s nine-member executive group became an agile team with daily 20-minute
   stand-ups; at **GE Digital**, COO Brad Surak is initiative owner and an engineering executive scrum master of the
   leadership team; at **Mission Bell Winery**, Erik Martella's executive agile team ranks an enterprise backlog and
   protects the organisation from pet projects.
6. **Destroy the barriers to agile behaviours** — more than 70% of agile practitioners report tension between their
   teams and the rest of the organisation. Get everyone on the same page (one list of enterprise priorities); don't
   change structures right away, change roles instead; name only one boss for each decision; focus on teams, not
   individuals; and lead with questions, not orders ('What do you recommend?', 'How could we test that?').

The reading's conclusion: the greatest impediment to agile's spread is not methodology or evidence but the behaviour of
executives.

## Lean Start-Up (LSU)

Lean start-up is associated with **Steve Blank and Eric Ries** (the slide shows Ries's *The Lean Startup* and Blank's
HBR article 'Why the Lean Start-Up Changes Everything'). Key principles:

1. Minimum viable product (MVP).
2. Lean–agile development.

**Steve Blank's hypotheses**

- Entrepreneurs and investors are generally executing on guesses.
- Start-ups aren't small versions of large organisations.
- They are about learning/discovery, not execution.
- They need to surface hypotheses/assumptions and test them against facts.
- However, the facts live outside the building.

:::definition
**Minimum viable product** — a product with just those features that allow you to sell to early adopters and
validate demand.
**Pivoting** — course correction to the proposed set of hypotheses, based on market learnings.
**The business model canvas** — a view of all nine building blocks of a business on one page and their relative
hypotheses.
:::

The slide's 'Agile Innovation Framework in Organisation' places these tools in a pipeline: creativity (mindfulness,
environment, competence, mood, motivation, creative skills) feeds ideas (R&D, market needs); ideas become concepts
(including SIT, lean start-up and design thinking); concepts are developed (agile, scrum, kanban) into innovation.

## The business model canvas

The canvas puts value propositions at the centre, key partners, key activities and key resources to the left, customer
relationships, channels and customer segments to the right, and cost structure and revenue streams along the bottom.
Each block is a set of questions — and so a set of hypotheses to test.

- **Key partners** — Who are our key partners? Who are our key suppliers? Which key resources are we acquiring from
  our partners? Which key activities do partners perform?
- **Key activities** — What key activities do our value propositions require? Our distribution channels? Customer
  relationships? Revenue streams?
- **Key resources** — What key resources do our value propositions require? Our distribution channels? Customer
  relationships? Revenue streams?
- **Value propositions** — What value do we deliver to the customer? Which one of our customers' problems are we
  helping to solve? What bundles of products and services are we offering to each segment? Which customer needs are
  we satisfying? What is the minimum viable product?
- **Customer relationships** — How do we get, keep and grow customers? Which customer relationships have we
  established? How are they integrated with the rest of our business model? How costly are they?
- **Channels** — Through which channels do our customer segments want to be reached? How do other companies reach
  them now? Which ones work best? Which ones are most cost-efficient? How are we integrating them with customer
  routines?
- **Customer segments** — For whom are we creating value? Who are our most important customers? What are the customer
  archetypes?
- **Cost structure** — What are the most important costs inherent to our business model? Which key resources are most
  expensive? Which key activities are most expensive?
- **Revenue streams** — For what value are our customers really willing to pay? For what do they currently pay? What
  is the revenue model? What are the pricing tactics?

## Lean start-up core elements: build–measure–learn

The loop: **Build** (experiments) → *baseline* → **Measure** (metrics) → *analyse* → **Learn** (pivot or persevere) →
*hypothesis* → Build. A fuller version runs ideas → build → code → measure → data → learn → ideas, with one instruction:
**minimise the total time through the loop**.

- **Build faster**: continuous deployment, small batches, minimum viable product, refactoring.
- **Measure faster**: split testing, actionable metrics, net promoter score.
- **Learn faster**: customer development, five whys.

**Validated learning** (Lean Startup / Lean UX) asks the questions in reverse order: 1) What do we need to learn?
2) How can we measure it? 3) What is the simplest thing to build to measure it (the MVP)?

**Learn: the innovation accounting report.** Each experiment is written up as a report:

- **Background** — what are you trying to learn or achieve? Why is it relevant?
- **Falsifiable hypothesis** — declare the expected outcome; quantify your goals and set scope/timebox; test the value
  and growth hypothesis first.
- **Build** — plan how to execute the experiment.
- **Measure** — results = data.
- **Learn** — document the learning (validated, invalidated or inconclusive).
- **Next action** — move to the next experiment.

**Test and measure — to pivot or persevere.** At Learn the venture either perseveres or pivots (a course correction to
its hypotheses). The one-page lean start-up sheet (based on Ries's book) lays out pivot types across the business:
technology and platform pivots; zoom-in and zoom-out pivots (output); channel and engine-of-growth pivots;
customer segment and customer need pivots; and value capture and business architecture pivots (shared value).

## Lean strategy (Collis, 2016)

Strategy and entrepreneurship are often viewed as polar opposites: strategy as a clearly defined path identified in
advance, entrepreneurship as opportunism, pivoting continually as information comes in. Yet the two desperately need
each other: 'Strategy without entrepreneurship is central planning. Entrepreneurship without strategy leads to chaos.'
Effective strategy encourages entrepreneurial behaviour by identifying the bounds within which innovation and
experimentation should take place. Collis's **lean strategy process** guards against the extremes of both rigid
planning and unrestrained experimentation: strategy serves as both a **screen** that novel ideas must pass and a
**yardstick** for evaluating the success of experiments.

### The entrepreneur's challenge

Entrepreneurship is 'the pursuit of opportunity without regard to resources currently controlled' (Howard Stevenson),
so every venture is short of money, talent and access; the internal challenge is to shepherd the resources it has. The
single best piece of advice for any company builder: **know what not to do**.

- The opportunity cost of doing A is that you cannot also do B.
- Every choice creates a unique path with a different outcome and unforeseen implications — you cannot simply do A now
  and B later.
- Decisions are interdependent.
- Simple market tests aren't always useful: some ideas cannot be evaluated in a series of quick, cheap experiments
  (Tesla's lithium ion batteries would never have got off the ground had they been expected to demonstrate immediate
  success), and quick A/B tests can miss longer-run effects or mistake ephemeral traction — like Groupon's customers,
  who found repeated use uneconomical.

### How strategy can help

- **Choose a viable opportunity** — Groupon quickly generated sales but had low entry barriers and transitory demand;
  Eleet analysed its markets before testing any of eight use cases, chose B2B and set the B2C cases aside.
- **Stay focused on the prize** — Nasty Gal diversified from reselling vintage clothing into magazines, a book, stores
  and branded products; with no clear focus growth stalled. Picis gained share once it concentrated on operating rooms.
- **Align the entire organisation** — a clarified strategy stopped Muzzy Lane staff chasing work-for-hire games that
  would divert developers from its platform for educational publishers.
- **Make the necessary commitments** — some investments cannot be tested in phases and need careful analysis first.

### Combining deliberate and emergent strategy

- **Vision** — perhaps the only part that should be in any sense permanent: the organisation's reason for existence
  (Microsoft: 'a personal computer on every desk').
- **Deliberate strategy** — agreed by senior executives after analysing strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats,
  resources and capabilities. Its three elements are **objective** (the near-term goal that defines success),
  **scope** ('what business we are in': what the venture will and will not do — probably the most critical guide rail)
  and **competitive advantage** (why customers will buy from us rather than competitors). Southwest kept to short-haul
  flights between second-tier airports and chose not to serve meals, protecting rapid turnaround.
- **Emergent strategy** — managers at all levels make daily decisions and conduct experiments guided by the strategy;
  their sum gradually alters the company's position. Southwest employees suggested Business Select, introduced because
  it would not interfere with fast gate turns.
- **Learning** — strategy frames how results are read: Spitfire damage reports pointed to the wrong areas until someone
  saw that hits elsewhere meant planes never came back. Instagram's founders noticed that Burbn users posted a lot of
  pictures and refocused on photo sharing. Feedback and findings reshape the strategy, and the process restarts.

:::examtip
Collis does not reject lean start-up: he puts it inside strategic boundaries. Strong answers say what the strategy
screens out, and how experiment results feed back into it.
:::

## The UniConnect challenge: three levels of managing innovation

In the tutorial, groups become an agile innovation team for **UniConnect**, a university-backed start-up (a teaching case, not a real company) developing a platform to help students connect with peers, find study partners and participate in campus activities. The challenge runs in three connected rounds:

1. **Round 1 — Manage tasks**: prioritise the product backlog and plan your first agile sprint.
2. **Round 2 — Manage change**: use customer feedback to revise priorities and plan your next experiment.
3. **Round 3 — Manage innovation**: identify organisational barriers and recommend how managers can enable agile.

What the challenge shows about managing innovation, as three levels of decision-making:

- **Managing tasks** — prioritise work based on value and capacity.
- **Managing change** — test assumptions and adapt based on evidence.
- **Managing barriers** — remove organisational barriers and enable cross-functional collaboration.

## Agile + lean start-up + lean strategy

The tutor slides close the topic by putting the week's three approaches together: they complement one another to help organisations innovate more effectively.

| Approach | Its question | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agile | How do we organise teams to develop and improve innovations? | Organise work into short, iterative cycles (sprints); enable collaboration and quick adaptation; continuously improve the product. |
| Lean start-up | How do we test whether our innovation creates value for customers? | Test assumptions with real users; learn from feedback and adapt; develop solutions that create value for customers. |
| Lean strategy | How do we ensure experimentation remains aligned with strategic direction? | Set direction, scope and guardrails for experimentation; prioritise the right opportunities; allocate resources effectively. |
