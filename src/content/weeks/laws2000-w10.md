## Role of the United Nations

- Central institutional regime of international relations.
- Formally established to prevent instability and unchecked aggression in inter-state affairs (which had featured
  prominently in the first half of the 20th century).
- Primary aim of the organisation was to promote friendly international relations and the maintenance of
  international peace and security.
- Influential in the development of modern international law, and its enforcement.
- Structures influenced by the political climate in which it was created, but its role continues to evolve.

The lecture filled in the last point: the UN was created after the Second World War by the states that won it, and its
structure still reflects 1945 — the permanent members of the Security Council are the US, the UK, France, Russia and
China. Its role is also wider than collective security: it has been important in the development of international law,
including human rights law.

The UN system chart in the slides shows six principal organs: the General Assembly, the Security Council, the
Economic and Social Council, the Secretariat, the International Court of Justice and the Trusteeship Council (which
suspended operation on 1 November 1994, after Palau, the last UN Trust Territory, became independent).

## The UN Charter — purposes (art 1)

:::statute
Art 1: The Purposes of the United Nations are: (1) To maintain international peace and security, and to that end: to
take effective collective measures for the prevention and removal of threats to the peace, and for the suppression of
acts of aggression or other breaches of the peace, and to bring about by peaceful means, and in conformity with the
principles of justice and international law, adjustment or settlement of international disputes or situations which
might lead to a breach of the peace; (2) To develop friendly relations among nations based on respect for the
principle of equal rights and self-determination of peoples, and to take other appropriate measures to strengthen
universal peace; (3) To achieve international co-operation in solving international problems of an economic, social,
cultural, or humanitarian character, and in promoting and encouraging respect for human rights and for fundamental
freedoms for all without distinction as to race, sex, language, or religion; and (4) To be a centre for harmonizing
the actions of nations in the attainment of these common ends.
:::

## Collective security

:::definition
Collective security: the creation of a system where war and aggression are the concern of all states, not a private
dispute between states — war against one is war against all.
:::

- Both the League of Nations and the United Nations were founded on the principle of collective security (another
  example: the North Atlantic Treaty Organisation, NATO).
- Intended to provide machinery for joint action — a collective response to threats and breaches of peace.
- The UN system centralises the permissible use of force to one body: the Security Council.

The lecture stressed that collective security is not a legal term: it belongs more to the discipline of international
relations than to law. The idea is that threats to peace are no longer handled bilaterally, state by state, but at the
collective level, through an organisation. The League of Nations was the first such organisation; the UN framework is
the second iteration of an organisation that aims to limit war between its members.

Other organisations, such as NATO, also deal with collective security, but they are always governed by the overarching
framework of the UN Charter. NATO cannot decide for itself when it is acceptable to use force: without the Security
Council's authorisation, it may act only where a Charter exception applies — for example, if a member such as France
or the UK were attacked (self-defence, Week 9).

## The UN Security Council: composition

Membership consists of 15 UN member states (art 23):

- 5 permanent members with veto powers (the P-5): the US, China, Russia, the UK and France (veto = art 27(3)).
- 10 non-permanent members elected for a two-year term (5 elected each year by the UN General Assembly), divided
  along regional lines: 3 reserved for Africa, 2 for Asia, 2 for Latin America and the Caribbean, 2 for Western
  Europe and Other States, 1 for Eastern Europe.

The slides list the current elected members as Bahrain, Colombia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Denmark,
Greece, Latvia, Liberia, Pakistan, Panama and Somalia.

The lecture added that the elected seats rotate: each year five new members arrive for a two-year term. Australia,
which has sat on the Council before, is campaigning for a future seat (the lecturer said it is so far running
unopposed); and a substantial number of the 193 member states (the lecturer said at least 50) have never been elected.
These are lecture remarks, not on the slides.

## Primary responsibility and binding decisions (arts 24, 25, 103)

:::statute
Art 24: "(1) In order to ensure prompt and effective action by the United Nations, its Members confer on the Security
Council primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security, and agree that in carrying
out its duties under this responsibility the Security Council acts on their behalf. (2) In discharging these duties
the Security Council shall act in accordance with the Purposes and Principles of the United Nations. The specific
powers granted to the Security Council for the discharge of these duties are laid down in Chapters VI, VII, VIII, and
XII."
:::

Chapter VII of the UN Charter lays down the Security Council's powers with respect to threats to peace, breaches of
the peace, and acts of aggression.

:::rule
Decisions are legally binding upon all members (art 25): "The Members of the United Nations agree to accept and carry
out the decisions of the Security Council in accordance with the present Charter." To be read together with art 103:
"In the event of a conflict between the obligations of the Members of the United Nations under the present Charter and
their obligations under any other international agreement, their obligations under the present Charter shall
prevail".
:::

The lecture called art 25 really important: if the Council decides that a state must do something, the state has to
carry it out. The lecture presented the Council as discharging its art 24 responsibility mainly through the mechanisms of Chapters VI
and VII, examined below.

## Decisions and recommendations

Only decisions bind. A Security Council resolution can contain both recommendations and decisions — and often contains
a mix — and art 25 makes only the decisions binding. How to tell which is which was not examined in detail (it is
debated), but it usually turns on the words the Council uses in each paragraph, chiefly the verbs. Art 39 itself marks
the line: the Council "shall make recommendations, or decide" what measures shall be taken.

Art 103 adds force to a binding decision: the obligations of states under the Charter, including a binding decision of
the Security Council, prevail over their obligations under any other treaty. If a treaty obligation conflicts with a
binding Council decision, the decision prevails. The lecturer's example was Security Council sanctions that cut across
obligations within the European Union, where a court held that the Council resolution overrode the treaty obligation;
the case was not named and is not in the slides, so check it before relying on it.

## Tools for maintaining peace and security

| Tool | Charter basis | What it covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peaceful settlement | Chapter VI, art 33 | Good offices, inquiry, mediation, conciliation, adjudication (ICJ) / arbitration (PCA) |
| Collective security — no use of force | Chapter VII, art 41 | Sanctions; peacekeeping operations without the use of force |
| Collective security — use of force | Chapter VII, art 42 | The Security Council authorises member states, regional organisations or peacekeeping operations |

## The Security Council and peaceful settlement of disputes (Chapter VI)

:::statute
Art 33: "(1) The parties to any dispute, the continuance of which is likely to endanger the maintenance of
international peace and security, shall, first of all, seek a solution by negotiation, enquiry, mediation,
conciliation, arbitration, judicial settlement, resort to regional agencies or arrangements, or other peaceful means
of their own choice. (2) The Security Council shall, when it deems necessary, call upon the parties to settle their
dispute by such means."
:::

- The UNSC can investigate any dispute, or any situation which might lead to international friction or give rise to
  a dispute, to see if it is a danger to international peace and security (art 34).
- Any member of the United Nations may bring any dispute, or any situation of the nature referred to in art 34, to the
  attention of the Security Council (or of the General Assembly) (art 35).
- The UNSC can recommend appropriate procedures or methods of adjustment (art 36(1)).
- The UNSC should consider that legal disputes should as a general rule be referred by the parties to the ICJ
  (art 36(3)).
- The parties who fail to settle a dispute by the means indicated in art 33 shall refer it to the UNSC (art 37(1)).

Art 33 is the article studied in Week 8 on the peaceful settlement of disputes (adjudication and non-adjudication).
What the lecture added is that art 33 itself refers to the Security Council: if the Council thinks it necessary, it
can recommend that the parties settle their dispute by one of those peaceful means, and most of the other articles of
Chapter VI are about the Council's role. Under Chapter VI the Council recommends; it does not need to determine a
threat to the peace first.

## Chapter VII: the art 39 determination

:::statute
Art 39: "The Security Council shall determine the existence of any threat to the peace, breach of the peace, or act
of aggression and shall make recommendations, or decide what measures shall be taken in accordance with Articles 41
and 42, to maintain or restore international peace and security."
:::

The Security Council's power to determine the situation has an evolving interpretation in practice — e.g.
proliferation of nuclear, chemical or biological weapons; internal armed conflicts; terrorism; serious human rights or
IHL violations; the 2014 Ebola outbreak in Africa.

The art 39 determination is what divides Chapter VII from Chapter VI: before the Council can use its powers under arts
41 and 42, it must determine that there is a threat to the peace, a breach of the peace or an act of aggression. Note
the two verbs again — it may "make recommendations" (not binding) or "decide" (binding).

## The art 39 findings in practice

- **Act of aggression.** The Council has never, or only very rarely, found an act of aggression in its resolutions.
- **Breach of the peace.** Found on a very few occasions, each involving an invasion of territory: Iraq's invasion of
  Kuwait in 1990 and, the lecturer added, the Iran–Iraq war of the 1980s (named in the lecture only, not on the
  slides).
- **Threat to the peace.** By far the most common finding, and the one that has evolved most since 1945. It once
  meant traditional risks — an invasion, aggression or some other inter-state conflict — but the Council has
  increasingly found threats in non-traditional situations: proliferation of weapons of mass destruction, terrorism and,
  perhaps most strikingly, the 2014 Ebola outbreak in Africa, so that even epidemics and pandemics can be threats
  to international peace and security. The determination is therefore flexible and covers a lot.

The categories overlap: an invasion like Iraq's can be both a breach of the peace and an act of aggression, and the
lecturer accepted that the distinction is confusing. In answer to a question she mentioned a General Assembly
definition of aggression as guidance (not on the slides; not examined further).

During the Cold War, tension between the US and Russia — sometimes acting through proxies in other states — made it
hard for the Council to agree on determinations and action under Chapter VII. The Council has become much more active
since the Cold War.

## Provisional measures (art 40)

:::statute
Art 40: "In order to prevent an aggravation of the situation, the Security Council may, before making the
recommendations or deciding upon the measures provided for in Article 39, call upon the parties concerned to comply
with such provisional measures as it deems necessary or desirable. Such provisional measures shall be without
prejudice to the rights, claims, or position of the parties concerned. The Security Council shall duly take account
of failure to comply with such provisional measures."
:::

What provisional measures look like, from the lecture: calling on the parties to agree on and implement a ceasefire; a
cessation of hostilities (much the same thing); allowing the delivery of humanitarian aid.

- **Sequence.** The wording ("before making the recommendations or deciding upon the measures") suggests provisional
  measures come first, with arts 41 and 42 to follow if they fail. That is not what happens in practice: the Council
  often imposes art 41 sanctions, or authorises force, and at the same time demands a ceasefire or humanitarian
  access. This has been accepted.
- **Binding?** The lecturer said provisional measures are considered binding. The slides do not say so (art 40 speaks
  of the Council calling upon the parties and taking account of failure to comply), so check this point.
- **Is an art 39 determination needed first?** Debated, but the majority of scholars say yes. Some provisional
  measures resemble the art 33 peaceful means, so the debate can be theoretical: the Council needs no determination to
  act under Chapter VI anyway.

## Non-forcible measures (art 41)

:::statute
Art 41: the Security Council "may decide what measures not involving the use of armed force" are to be employed;
these "may include complete or partial interruption of economic relations and of rail, sea, air, postal, telegraphic,
radio, and other means of communication, and the severance of diplomatic relations".
:::

- A non-exhaustive list: it provides the SC with a wide scope of non-military coercive measures, including economic
  and diplomatic measures.
- Can include economic sanctions and arms embargoes, but also the establishment of ad hoc international criminal
  tribunals (such as the ICTY and ICTR).

Once the determination is made (and it covers a lot), the Council chooses between art 41 measures, which do not
involve the use of force, and art 42 measures, which do. Reading art 41's list — interrupting economic relations and
communications, severing diplomatic relations — you arrive at sanctions, which are a big part of art 41. But the list
"include[s]" these measures; it is not exhaustive. The best example is the Council's creation of the two ad hoc
international criminal tribunals: the ICTY (former Yugoslavia) and the ICTR (the 1994 Rwandan genocide), created at
almost the same time. At first it was debated whether the Council — a political organ — had authority under art 41 to
create a tribunal, since the article does not mention one; the members decided that it did, and today that is not in
question. (The tribunals return in Week 11. The live lecture stopped here; the detail on sanctions below was given in
the recorded online part, which has no transcript, so it rests on the slides.)

The slides map sanctions regimes by purpose: peace enforcement (e.g. Angola, CAR, DRC, Sudan), counter-terrorism (e.g.
Al-Qaida/ISIL, Libya, Lebanon), non-proliferation (e.g. DPRK, Iran, Iraq) and R2P (e.g. Libya).

**Comprehensive sanctions.** Financial, trade and other economic measures against states (e.g. Iraq during the 1990s,
and North Korea until today, because of nuclear proliferation). There is controversy surrounding their impact on
populations (e.g. Iraq in the 1990s).

**Targeted or 'smart' sanctions.** In response to the impact of comprehensive sanctions, there is a trend towards more
targeted or 'smart' sanctions, such as a focus on trade in specific commodities (e.g. conflict diamonds), or sanctions
against particular groups, corporations or individuals (e.g. travel bans and asset freezes). Details are often
determined by specific Security Council sanctions committees. Proper implementation ultimately depends on how states
interpret and apply the SC-mandated sanctions regime.

:::case
Democratic People's Republic of Korea — S/RES/2397 (2017) (a non-exhaustive list): reduced the caps on the supply,
sale or transfer to the DPRK of all refined petroleum products; introduced a ban on the DPRK's export of food and
agricultural products, machinery, electrical equipment, earth and stone including magnesite and magnesia, wood and
vessels; introduced a ban on the supply, sale or transfer to the DPRK of all industrial machinery, transportation
vehicles, iron, steel and other metals (except spare parts to maintain DPRK commercial civilian passenger aircraft
currently in use); and strengthened vessel-related provisions by prohibiting the provision of insurance or
re-insurance services to, and requiring member states to de-register, any vessels involved in illicit activities.
:::

## Ad hoc tribunals and referral to the ICC

Ad hoc tribunals try people responsible for serious crimes under international humanitarian law: the International
Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) and the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda (ICTR).

The International Criminal Court is not part of the UN, but the UNSC can refer situations to the ICC Prosecutor in
accordance with Chapter VII (e.g. Darfur, 2005).

:::statute
Rome Statute art 13(b): the Court may exercise its jurisdiction with respect to a crime referred to in art 5 if "(b) A
situation in which one or more of such crimes appears to have been committed is referred to the Prosecutor by the
Security Council acting under Chapter VII of the Charter of the United Nations".
:::

## Use of force under art 42

:::statute
Art 42: "Should the Security Council consider that measures provided for in Article 41 would be inadequate or have
proved to be inadequate, it may take such action by air, sea, or land forces as may be necessary to maintain or
restore international peace and security." Such action may include demonstrations, blockade, and other operations by
air, sea, or land forces of members of the United Nations.
:::

- An exception to the prohibition on the use of force in art 2(4) of the UN Charter.
- Last resort.
- Reliance on member states.

## Authorising coalitions and regional organisations

Art 43 of the UN Charter has never been implemented, so the UNSC may authorise coalitions of states.

:::case
Operation Desert Storm — in response to Iraq's illegal occupation of Kuwait, the Security Council in 1990 authorised
an ad hoc coalition of states led by the US to use all necessary measures to restore Kuwait's sovereignty (UNSC Res
678). Acting under Chapter VII, Res 678 (29 November 1990) demanded that Iraq comply fully with Res 660 (1990), allowed
it "one final opportunity, as a pause of goodwill", and authorised "Member States co-operating with the Government of
Kuwait", unless Iraq fully implemented the resolutions on or before 15 January 1991, "to use all necessary means to
uphold and implement resolution 660 (1990) and all subsequent relevant resolutions and to restore international peace
and security in the area". It was adopted by 12 votes to 2 (Cuba and Yemen), with 1 abstention (China).
:::

The UNSC may also authorise regional organisations to enforce its decisions (art 53; Chapter VIII) — e.g. ECOWAS
(Liberia, Sierra Leone, Côte d'Ivoire), the Commonwealth of Independent States (Tajikistan), the African Union
(Darfur), NATO (Libya), the Organization of American States (Haiti), the OSCE (Georgia) and the EU (DRC).

## Internal conflict, humanitarian aid and protection of civilians

Examples (not exhaustive):

- Internal conflict and facilitation of humanitarian aid: Somalia 1992 (Res 794) — "Acting under Chapter VII of the
  Charter of the United Nations, authorizes the Secretary General and Member States cooperating to implement the offer
  referred to in paragraph 8 above to use all necessary means to establish as soon as possible a secure environment for
  humanitarian relief operations in Somalia" (para 10); Haiti 1994 (Res 940); Albania 1997 (Res 1101).
- Protection of civilians: Libya 2011 (Res 1973); Côte d'Ivoire 2011 (Res 1975).
- Enforcement of no-fly zones and embargos: Libya 2011 (Res 1973).
- Repression of piracy: Somalia 2008 (Res 1816).

## Revived or implied authorisation? The 2003 invasion of Iraq

Member states have invoked claims to 'revive' an earlier authorisation, or to 'imply' such authorisation from UNSC
resolutions (without it being explicitly authorised). The most prominent example is Operation Iraqi Freedom in March
2003: a coalition of states led by the US and UK (including Australia) invaded Iraq with the stated aim of eliminating
weapons of mass destruction and stopping Saddam Hussein from supporting terrorism (e.g. Res 1441 threatened 'serious
consequences' against Iraq).

In its letter of 20 March 2003 to the President of the Security Council, the United States said the actions were
"authorized under existing Council resolutions, including its resolutions 678 (1990) and 687 (1991)". Res 687 imposed
a series of obligations on Iraq — most importantly, extensive disarmament obligations — that were conditions of the
ceasefire it established, and "a material breach of these obligations removes the basis of the ceasefire and revives
the authority to use force under resolution 678 (1990)". The letter pointed to Res 1441 (2002), in which the Council,
acting under Chapter VII, decided that Iraq had been and remained in material breach and gave it a "final
opportunity" to comply.

The participating states claimed that broad language from SC resolutions authorised the use of force — an
understanding rejected by most other SC and UN member states, as well as the majority of legal scholars. The UK's
Chilcot inquiry found that not all peaceful options had been exhausted before going to war. Twenty years after the
invasion there has been no inquiry in Australia (despite ongoing calls), and there are discussions about more
involvement of parliament in war decisions.

:::examtip
Look for express Chapter VII authorising language — Res 678's "all necessary means" — before treating any use of force
as authorised by the Council. Words like "serious consequences" or a "final opportunity" are not an authorisation.
:::

## UN peacekeeping and peace operations

- Not explicitly mentioned in the UN Charter.
- Primarily created to keep the peace, not to enforce it.
- Unlike authorisation to one state or group of states, these are UN operations.

:::rule
Traditional principles of peacekeeping: consent of the parties; impartiality; non-use of force except in
self-defence.
:::

- Gradually evolving — e.g. the UN as transitional administration (Kosovo and Timor-Leste).
- Changing attitude to the use of force — e.g. authorisation of some missions to use force to carry out their
  mandate, such as missions in the DRC (including targeted offensive operations) and Mali.

A chart in the slides (Pew Research Center) shows the number of UN peacekeeping troops, military experts and police
deployed rising from the late 1950s, peaking in the mid-1990s (when deployments to Europe were largest), falling, then
climbing past 100,000 by 2015, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa. Current missions: MINURSO (Western Sahara), MINUSCA
(Central African Republic), MONUSCO (DR Congo), UNDOF (Golan), UNFICYP (Cyprus), UNIFIL (Lebanon), UNISFA (Abyei),
UNMIK (Kosovo), UNMISS (South Sudan), UNMOGIP (India and Pakistan) and UNTSO (Middle East).

## UN General Assembly

- The General Assembly (GA) is the parliamentary body of the United Nations and consists of representatives of all UN
  member states (currently 193 states).
- Seen as the most 'democratic' international organ, but with a minimal role in enforcement.
- One state, one vote (art 18).

The GA has a subsidiary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security.

:::statute
Art 11(2): "The General Assembly may discuss any questions relating to the maintenance of international peace and
security brought before it … except as provided in Article 12, may make recommendations with regard to any such
questions to the state or states concerned or to the Security Council or to both. Any such question on which action
is necessary shall be referred to the Security Council by the General Assembly either before or after discussion."
:::

:::statute
Art 14: "Subject to the provisions of Article 12, the General Assembly may recommend measures for the peaceful
adjustment of any situation, regardless of origin, which it deems likely to impair the general welfare or friendly
relations among nations, including situations resulting from a violation of the provisions of the present Charter
setting forth the Purposes and Principles of the United Nations". See also *Certain Expenses of the United Nations*,
Advisory Opinion, ICJ (1962).
:::

:::rule
Art 12(1): "While the Security Council is exercising in respect of any dispute or situation the functions assigned to
it in the present Charter, the General Assembly shall not make any recommendation with regard to that dispute or
situation unless the Security Council so requests." See the *Wall* Advisory Opinion, ICJ (2004), paras 27–28.
:::

The lecture explained how this has developed. Traditionally the view was that the General Assembly should not look at
a situation endangering peace and security while the Security Council was already considering it, so that the two
did not overlap. That interpretation has evolved: it is now recognised that the Assembly has the power and authority
to consider the same issue in parallel with the Council. They usually take different approaches — the Assembly a much
broader view, the Council the detail (for example, authorising the delivery of humanitarian aid, or asking the
Secretary-General for reports).

## Uniting for Peace

Can the GA recommend non-forcible and forcible measures?

:::statute
UN GA Resolution 377 (V) (1950) — Uniting for Peace: "…if the Security Council, because of lack of unanimity of the
permanent members, fails to exercise its primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and
security in any case where there appears to be a threat to the peace, breach of the peace, or act of aggression, the
General Assembly shall consider the matter immediately with a view to making appropriate recommendations to Members
for collective measures."
:::

- The Cold War increased the scope of the GA in enforcement in the context of a deadlocked Security Council ('Uniting
  for Peace').
- The Uniting for Peace procedure was confirmed by the ICJ in the *Certain Expenses* case (Advisory Opinion). (One
  slide dates the opinion 1962, another 1949.)

## The Secretariat and the Secretary-General

- Constitutes an international civil service, led by the UN Secretary-General (UNSG); the current Secretary-General is
  António Guterres.
- The UNSG is appointed by the General Assembly for a five-year term.
- The UNSG can bring to the attention of the Security Council any matter which in their opinion may threaten
  international peace and security (art 99).
- 'Good office' functions in mediating in and resolving international crises or conflicts have expanded over the
  years (including through the appointment of Special Representatives of the UNSG).

## Main takeaway points

- The UN system constrains the use of force among its member states through a system of collective security.
- This UN system centralises the permissible use of force to one body: the Security Council.
- When there is a threat to peace, breach of peace, or act of aggression, it is for the UN Security Council to take
  decisions to maintain or restore international peace and security.
- The UNSC can employ a range of coercive measures under Chapter VII to enforce its decisions, both non-military and
  military (the range of non-military measures has expanded over time, including sanctions).
- In the absence of a standing UN army, the UN Security Council may decide on (1) a UN-run peacekeeping or peace
  operation; or (2) authorise the use of force.
- However, the UNSC has not been able to act in all instances of threats to and breaches of international peace and
  security (e.g. because of a lack of capacity, but also the veto power of the P-5).
