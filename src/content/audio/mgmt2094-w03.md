This week was design thinking. The reading's definition: in essence, design thinking is a process that harnesses
creativity to resolve problems and create solutions that make a product, a service or a business better. Tim Brown of
IDEO puts it as matching people's needs with what is technologically feasible and what a viable business can turn into
value. Our guest lecturer called it a human-centred, creative and experimentation-driven approach to innovation, and
the reason to use it is that more innovations fail from a lack of customers than from the product not working.

Those three things are the sweet spot. Desirable: people want it. Feasible: we can make it. Viable: we can make money
from it, or, for government, it has real impact for citizens. The trap is starting with what we can make, writing a
business case on made-up numbers, and letting the customer see it for the first time at launch. Start with
desirability.

The process in the reading runs empathy, define, ideate, prototype, test, under understand, create and deliver, with
eight steps: be empathetic, explorative, creative, iterative, rational, practical, honest, and repeat. Remember step
three, don't judge ideas while you generate them, and step five, avoid consensus thinking when you choose.

Meinel and Leifer give four rules. The human rule: all design is social. The ambiguity rule: preserve ambiguity, so
chance discovery can happen. The redesign rule: all design is redesign of solutions to needs that are millennia old.
And the tangibility rule: prototypes are communication media.

On empathy, affective empathy mirrors someone's emotion; cognitive empathy takes their perspective, and that is the one
design needs. Watch for the four roadblocks: idealism, egoism, realism and circumstantialism. Temple Grandin's curved
corrals show what unprejudiced observation can find. And remember the debate: IDEO goes into the customer's world,
while Verganti warns that customers can't see far into the future.

In the lecture, discovery meant being the customer, being with the customer, learning about them from people around
them, and talking to extreme users. Then we distil it into a customer narrative: a need written as a verb, and an
insight that reaches the root cause, like the Nepal team that found a transport problem rather than an incubator
problem.

For ideas, divergent thinking means many ideas, fast: think in threes, ask new questions, take nine perspectives. As
Deloitte put it, diverge before you converge.

Finally, prototype. Think with your hands, fail early while it's cheap, match how finished the prototype is to how sure
you are, and if a test fails, pivot or let the idea go.
