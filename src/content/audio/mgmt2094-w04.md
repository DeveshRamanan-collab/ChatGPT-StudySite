This week is Edward de Bono's six thinking hats. Start with parallel thinking. It is an alternative to argument: instead of attacking each other's thoughts, everyone puts their thoughts forward side by side, in one direction at a time. The hats make that possible, because a hat is a direction of thinking you can put on and take off.

Blue is control: the blue sky over us, the wider view. White is information and facts, neutral and colourless. Yellow is benefits, sunny and optimistic, asking why it will work. Green is growth and creativity, new ideas with no criticism. Red is emotion and intuition, a gut reaction with no need to justify it. Black is problems and caution, the risks and why it might fail; it makes plans tougher, but it can be over-used. Kivunja tells us judges wear black, which is why black is the critical hat.

Running a session, we begin and end with the blue hat. The facilitator asks what our focus is and what thinking we need, calls the switches, and closes with what we achieved, the conclusion and next steps. Use any hat as often as needed, keep each one short, stay in the idiom, and remember hats are directions, not categories of people.

Sequences serve purposes. Yellow then black assesses an idea. Black then green improves a design, like the All Weather bike. Red then white compares fact with opinion. Black, yellow, green compares and synthesises. White then blue goes from facts to a plan.

Why bother? De Bono names three difficulties: emotions, helplessness and confusion. The hats put feelings in the right place, give us next steps, and take one direction at a time.

The lecture opened with linear thinking. Upgrading cars from ten to twenty miles per gallon saves more fuel than twenty to fifty, because our brains draw straight lines where the world curves. Remember the four curves and the four steps, and sell benefits, not attributes, like Apple's thousand songs in your pocket.

Mattimore shows that brainstorming aloud lets one voice anchor the rest. Brainwriting passes the paper; brainwalking moves the people between ideation stations, adding energy and public ideas.

Finally, Carmeli and colleagues show that leaders who model and encourage knowledge sharing build their people's creative problem-solving capacity, and with it fluency and originality.
