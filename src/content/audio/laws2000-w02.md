This week is about where international law actually comes from. We start with Article 38(1) of the ICJ
Statute, which lists treaties, customary international law, and general principles of law as primary,
law-creating sources, and judicial decisions and scholarly writings as secondary, law-interpreting ones.
But the article wasn't really drafted to give us a clean list — it just tells the Court what it can apply —
and it isn't exhaustive, so there's more to come.

Treaties are an agreement between States, in writing, governed by international law, whatever you call them —
agreement, convention, protocol. What matters is consent: if a state ratifies a treaty, pacta sunt servanda
means it's bound to honour it, and must keep its domestic law consistent with it.

Custom is trickier. It needs a general, consistent state practice, plus opinio juris — a belief the practice
is legally required, not just courtesy. Neither has to be perfect: in Nicaragua, the Court said practice only
needs to be "in general" consistent, and a state excusing a breach by pointing to an exception actually
confirms the rule. In North Sea Continental Shelf, even a short period of practice can be enough, provided
it's extensive and uniform, especially among specially affected states. But the Asylum Case shows practice
that's too inconsistent just won't cut it.

Once a rule of custom exists, it usually binds everyone — but not always. A persistent objector, like Norway
in Fisheries, can escape a rule it rejected from the very start. And custom can be particular rather than
universal — Right of Passage over Indian Territory shows a custom can bind as few as two states, Portugal and
India. Treaty and custom aren't rivals either: a treaty can codify, crystallise, or even generate a rule of
custom, which is why the right of self-defence exists both in custom and in Article 51 of the UN Charter,
binding states whether or not they've joined the Charter.

General principles — equity, good faith, res judicata — are a last resort, used when treaty and custom can't
resolve a dispute. Judicial decisions don't bind anyone beyond the parties to that case, under Article 59, but
the ICJ rarely departs from its own reasoning, for legitimacy, predictability, and peaceful settlement.
Scholarly writings and the International Law Commission help interpret the law the same way.

And Article 38 isn't the end of the story. States can bind themselves through unilateral statements alone, as
Norway did over Eastern Greenland and France did over nuclear testing. Jus cogens norms, like the prohibition
on genocide, can never be overridden by treaty. Security Council decisions can bind states under Article 25 of
the Charter. And soft law, like the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, isn't binding itself, but can shape
practice and eventually harden into treaties or custom.
