This week is about special parties, where reasonable foreseeability is always needed but never enough.

Start with public authorities: bodies created by statute to perform public functions. There's no Crown immunity any
more, so they can be sued, but they're different from private defendants. They act under statute, and courts worry
about resources and the separation of powers: a court shouldn't tell the executive where to spend limited money.
Heyman gives us the policy versus operations line. High-level policy, like letting an industry regulate itself, is
pretty much off limits; operational negligence is more arguable.

Crimmins found a duty. The Stevedoring Authority wasn't the employer, but it directed casual workers where to work,
knew the risks, and the worker was vulnerable because he had to obey. McHugh J's six questions are foreseeability, a
specific class, vulnerability, knowledge, core policy functions and other policy reasons. Not all need a yes; the last
two point away from a duty.

Graham Barclay Oysters found no duty for the council or the State. The complaint was about omissions, self-regulation
was a policy choice, the council's powers served the public generally, and control was lacking. Amaca adds five
propositions: look at the whole relationship; control of a risky situation counts; but a power, its past use, or
knowledge of harm is not enough on its own.

The Civil Liability Act Part 5 then narrows things. Section 41 says who's an authority. Section 42 says resources limit
functions and their allocation can't be challenged. Sections 43 and 43A set the "so unreasonable" Wednesbury bar.
Section 44 protects failures to regulate, including licensing, and section 46 says exercising a function doesn't
create a duty to exercise it. In Refrigerated Roadways the RTA owed a duty about concrete dropped from an overpass,
section 43A didn't apply because anyone can put up a screen, but there was no breach. For police, Hill says no duty in
investigating crime, but Zalewski shows operational conduct can attract one.

Lecture two. Landlords now owe a duty: Jones v Bartlett says premises must be reasonably fit for habitation, and
reasonable steps to find and fix defects discharge it. Occupiers owe lawful entrants a duty; Cardy says trespassers
enter at their own risk. Barristers are immune for conduct in court, for reasons of conflicting duties, court time and
finality, but solicitors can be sued. Parents may owe others a duty to control their child, as in Smith v Leurs, and
can be liable to the child if they lead it into danger, which Hahn v Conley didn't find. Finally, third parties:
Dorset Yacht found a duty from control, limited to the immediate vicinity; Modbury found none for a stranger's crime.
