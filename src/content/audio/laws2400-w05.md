Negligence is different from the torts we've covered so far: it's an action on the case, so we always need damage
to get a claim off the ground. There are three elements: duty of care, breach, and causation of compensable
harm, whether that's physical, psychiatric or economic. This week is all about duty. The Civil Liability Act
applies to negligence claims under section five A, unless section three B carves them out — think intentional
acts meant to injure, sexual assault, workers' compensation and dust diseases, which stay with the common law.

Duty of care always starts with Donoghue versus Stevenson — the snail in the ginger beer bottle. Lord Atkin's
neighbour principle says we must take reasonable care to avoid acts we can reasonably foresee would injure our
neighbour, meaning anyone closely and directly affected by what we do. Grant versus Australian Knitting Mills
applied this to a manufacturer whose hidden chemical defect caused severe dermatitis. But the neighbour
principle is a starting point, not the finish line. English courts tried to generalise duty into fixed tests —
Anns's two stages, then Caparo's three — but Australia rejected both. We went our own way: proximity as a
touchstone in the eighties, then, since the late nineties, the salient factors approach.

Reasonable foreseeability itself isn't one test — it's asked three times, meaning something different each
time. Chapman versus Hearse is the key case here: a doctor killed helping at an accident scene didn't need his
death to be foreseeable in its exact sequence, just that injury to a class of person like him was foreseeable.
That's the duty question. Breach asks about the kind of carelessness; remoteness asks about the kind of damage.

Most of the time, duty isn't even controversial, because the facts fit an established category — driver and
road user, employer and employee, doctor and patient, manufacturer and consumer, solicitor and client. Cite the
category, cite the authority, move on. But when the facts are novel — outside any established category — we
need more than foreseeability. That's where salient factors come in, from Caltex Refineries versus Stavar:
control, vulnerability, reliance, proximity, coherence, and more, weighed together, not ticked off a checklist.
Sometimes that weighing points against a duty even where harm was foreseeable — Sullivan versus Moody found no
duty owed to a father under investigation for suspected abuse, because of indeterminate liability and
conflicting duties to protect children.

Finally, pure economic loss — loss with no physical injury or property damage attached. Foreseeability alone
is never enough here, Perre versus Apand tells us that directly. For negligent misstatement, we need Hedley
Byrne versus Heller's four factors: special relationship, assumption of responsibility, reliance, and
reasonable reliance — remember Hedley Byrne itself lost because Heller disclaimed responsibility. On top of
that, Woolcock Street Investments adds vulnerability: could the plaintiff have protected themselves another
way? If not, they're vulnerable, and a duty is more likely to be found.
