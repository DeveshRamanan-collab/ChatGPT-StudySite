This week we asked what a right is, using human rights as the example, through six issues: identity, authority, content, history, justification and implementation.

First, human rights are rights. On the will theory, you have a right if you have control or choice over whether others must act, so rights are tools of autonomy. On the interest theory, you have a right if it protects an important interest, so rights serve welfare. The infant is the will theory's problem: no choice, but surely rights. Human rights are also plural, universal and high priority, with priority over the general interest.

Hohfeld breaks "right" into four positions. A claim: Y owes X a duty. A privilege: X has no duty not to act. A power: X can alter Y's normative position. An immunity: Y cannot alter X's. Negative and positive rights: freedom from, freedom to.

Priority sets rights against utility. Do rights work as trumps or as shields? Either way, a true commitment to human rights is incompatible with a certain kind of utilitarian reasoning. In the ticking-bomb case an act-utilitarian may justify torture to save many lives; the rights view says no. On the lecture's account, Waldron finds a bridge in indirect utilitarianism: for utilitarian reasons, we tell officials not to reason as utilitarians.

Authority asks whether human rights are moral rights, legal rights, or both. Slavery shows the stakes: there was no legal right not to be enslaved, but arguably a moral one, which then needs a justification.

Content comes in three generations: civil and political; social, economic and cultural; and group or solidarity rights, which are the hardest to justify and implement. The history runs from Magna Carta in 1215 to the Universal Declaration in 1948, which put universality at the centre, and on to the Covenants.

Justification meets Bentham: natural rights are nonsense upon stilts, because only legal rights exist. Add the sceptical questions: myth, selfishness, oppression, community and ethnocentrism. Underneath sits the question whether moral statements can be true or false: relativism against objectivism.

Finally, implementation. Moral rights become legal rights through positivisation, though Australia has no bill of rights in its Federal Constitution, and rights that are recognised but never implemented are only paper rights. Most rights can be limited. Canada's section 1 and Victoria's section 7 allow reasonable limits demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society. Article 15 of the European Convention lets states derogate in an emergency, but never from the right to life (save lawful acts of war), torture, slavery or no punishment without law. Alexy explains the weighing: rights are principles, so a limit must be suitable, necessary and proportionate in the narrow sense.
