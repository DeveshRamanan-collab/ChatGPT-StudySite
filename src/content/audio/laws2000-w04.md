This week is about who international law is actually for. A subject of international law has rights it can
claim and obligations it can be held to — that's international legal personality. States are the only subjects
with full legal capacity. Others have partial capacity, and the key case is Reparation for Injuries in 1949: the
Charter never says the UN has international personality, but the Court found it from the functions the UN was
meant to exercise — an international person, but certainly not a state.

Beyond states, individuals gained rights after the Second World War, through the UDHR, which isn't binding, and
treaties like the ICCPR, which are. Individuals also carry obligations, as Milošević's indictment by the ICTY
shows. Peoples and minorities have rights, armed groups are bound by humanitarian law under common Article 3, and
corporations have rights but, so far, no binding obligations.

Statehood means the four Montevideo criteria: a permanent population of any size, a defined territory even with
disputed borders — North Sea Continental Shelf — an effective government, and capacity to enter into relations,
which Scotland lacks. Once a state exists, the presumption of continuity keeps it a state even when government
breaks down.

Recognition: the declaratory theory, which most people hold, says it is symbolic; the constitutive theory says no
recognition, no existence. And non-recognition can block entities created illegally, like the Turkish Republic of
Northern Cyprus.

Territory comes by cession, accretion, occupation of terra nullius and prescription. Artificial islands don't
count, and sustained protest stops prescription. Conquest has been out since 1945 under Article 2(4) — occupation
by force is temporary and passes no title.

Self-determination is settled in the decolonisation context, as Chagos confirms. Outside it, secession is highly
contested: Kosovo split opinion, and in Quebec the Supreme Court said a people whose internal self-determination is
fulfilled has no right to secede.

Finally, international organisations: created by treaty or other instrument governed by international law, which
is what separates them from NGOs. Their personality is express, like the WTO's, or implied, like the UN's. Their
powers are limited by speciality — no general competence, as the WHO found in the Nuclear Weapons opinion — though
they have implied powers essential to their duties. And they answer for their own wrongful acts, with effective
control deciding whether a peacekeeper's conduct belongs to the UN or to the state that sent them.
