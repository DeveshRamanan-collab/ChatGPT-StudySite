Collective security means war and aggression are everyone's concern, not a private dispute between two states:
war against one is war against all. The League of Nations and the United Nations were both built on it, and the UN
Charter puts it into practice by centralising the permissible use of force in one body, the Security Council.

The Council has fifteen members. Five are permanent, with a veto under article 27(3): the US, China, Russia, the UK
and France. Ten are elected by the General Assembly for two-year terms, along regional lines. Under article 24 members
give the Council primary responsibility for international peace and security, under article 25 they agree to carry out
its decisions, and under article 103 those Charter obligations beat any conflicting treaty. Only decisions bind, though: a resolution can mix recommendations and decisions, and the wording shows which.

The Council's tools come in order. First, Chapter VI, peaceful settlement: article 33 tells the parties to try
negotiation, enquiry, mediation, conciliation, arbitration, judicial settlement or regional bodies, and the Council can
investigate, call on them and recommend, sending legal disputes towards the ICJ. Then Chapter VII. Article 39 is the
gateway: the Council must determine a threat to the peace, a breach of the peace or an act of aggression, and its
practice now reaches internal conflicts, terrorism, proliferation, serious human rights violations, even Ebola. It almost always finds a threat; a breach only a few times, like Kuwait, and aggression hardly ever. Article
40 lets it call for provisional measures without prejudice to anyone's claims.

Article 41 covers measures without force: comprehensive sanctions, which hurt populations, and increasingly targeted
ones like asset freezes and travel bans, arms embargoes, ad hoc tribunals like the ICTY and ICTR, and referrals to the
ICC, as with Darfur. Article 42 is force, an exception to article 2(4) and a last resort. Because article 43 was never
implemented, the Council authorises coalitions, as in resolution 678 in 1990 — "all necessary means" to free Kuwait —
or regional organisations under article 53. The 2003 invasion of Iraq relied on a claimed revival of 678, which most
states and scholars rejected.

Peacekeeping isn't in the Charter at all. It keeps the peace rather than enforcing it, and rests on consent,
impartiality and force only in self-defence, though missions in the DRC and Mali now have robust mandates. Finally,
the General Assembly, 193 states with one vote each, has a subsidiary role: it can discuss and recommend, but not while
the Council is dealing with a matter, unless Uniting for Peace applies because the permanent members are deadlocked.
The Secretary-General can raise threats under article 99 and offers good offices.
