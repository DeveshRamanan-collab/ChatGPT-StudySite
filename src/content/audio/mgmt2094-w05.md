Week five is a tour of creativity theories, and the exam will want us to name them, tell them apart and apply them. Start with Herrmann's Whole Brain Model: four quadrants. A is logical and quantitative, B organised and planned, C interpersonal and feeling based, D holistic and synthesising. Our profile scores each one: one for preferred, two for competent, three for avoided, and two quadrants can share a number. A and B carry preparation and verification; C and D carry incubation and illumination. The task decides which quadrant we need, so if we avoid it, we push ourselves there or lean on a teammate. And the quadrants are a model, not a map of the brain.
 Wallas' model gives the creative process five stages: preparation, incubation, intimation, illumination, verification. Sadler-Smith shows Wallas wrote twelve pages on intimation, the feeling that a solution is coming before we know what it is. Helmholtz found ideas on slow walks up wooded hills; Poincaré warned that an idea that feels certain can still fail the test of verification.
 Then the theories of where creativity comes from. Psychoanalytical: creativity as a reaction to hardship and repressed emotion. Mental illness: a mild disorder, not a severe one. Addiction: substances remove stress, though the counter-argument says creative people are just more prone to addiction. Psychoticism, from Eysenck: halfway to psychosis, rejecting norms. The humanistic theory flips all of this: once our needs are met, at self-actualisation, we can choose to be creative. Maslow adds primary, secondary and integrated creativity. Grace says genius is a gift; accident says chance; association says borrow procedures from another area; cognitive says creativity is a normal human activity. Remember chance favours the prepared mind.
 Creative problem solving runs objective, fact, problem, idea, solution and acceptance finding, diverging to list and converging to choose. Markman tells us the problem statement is the cue to memory: Dyson stopped thinking about bags and asked how to separate dirt from air. Claman reminds us everyone is creative, but ruts kill it, so think new, look for intersections, capitalise on obstacles and share what we know.
 Finally, the four preferences: Clarifier, Ideator, Developer, Implementer. Developers can get stuck perfecting; Implementers can leap too soon. Mix them, and a team gets complementary ideas.
