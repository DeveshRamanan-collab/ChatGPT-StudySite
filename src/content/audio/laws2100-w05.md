This week is Dworkin against Hart. In Taking Rights Seriously Dworkin attacks what he calls the ruling theory of law, which comes from Bentham and has two parts: positivism, a theory of what law is, and utilitarianism, a theory of what law ought to be. He rejects both parts, and the assumption that they are independent. For Dworkin a general theory of law must be normative as well as conceptual.

He gives positivism three tenets. Rules are identified by their pedigree, which is Hart's rule of recognition. Judges must sometimes exercise discretion. And there is a legal obligation only if a valid rule applies. His attack starts from how lawyers and judges actually argue. They use rules, but also principles and policies. A policy sets a goal, like reducing car accidents. A principle is a requirement of justice or fairness, like no one may profit from their own wrong. Rules apply all or nothing; principles have weight, so when they conflict we balance them. And principles cannot be identified by pedigree.

On discretion, think of the doughnut: discretion is the hole left by a belt of standards. Weak discretion means judgment, or final authority. Strong discretion means not being bound by standards at all. Riggs v Palmer is the key case. Elmer poisoned his grandfather to inherit under a valid will. The majority said laws are controlled by maxims of the common law, so he could not profit from his crime. For Dworkin those principles were law, so the judges were bound, and legal obligations can come from a constellation of principles, not only from rules.

Hart's Postscript replies. Law is identified by social sources, unless the law itself incorporates moral criteria. That is inclusive positivism. Raz's exclusive positivism says morality is never a condition of validity.

In Law's Empire the new argument is theoretical disagreement. Judges disagree about what the law is even when the statute is clear. If the rule of recognition rests on officials' agreement, how can they disagree about content?

Dworkin's own answer is constructive interpretation: examples, the point of the practice, then revision, as with courtesy. For him law's point is to justify state coercion. Law as integrity means consistency in principle, tested by Judge Hercules on fit and justification, with one right answer even in hard cases. We should ask whether that is a general theory, or one that fits the United States.
