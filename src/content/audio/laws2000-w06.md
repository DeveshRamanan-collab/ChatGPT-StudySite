This week is about the times a state can't use its courts as normal because another state, or someone
representing it, is involved. There are separate regimes, with separate conditions, so always start by asking
who is claiming immunity.

State immunity comes from sovereign equality. States are juridically equal, so one can't sit in judgment on
another without its consent. It bars adjudication — the state can't be sued — and enforcement — its assets can't
be seized. It's procedural, so it says nothing about whether the conduct was lawful. The source is custom. The
2004 UN Convention has twenty-five of the thirty ratifications it needs, so we use its articles as a guide only.
A state loses immunity by express consent, by taking part in the proceedings, or on a counterclaim.

Immunity used to be absolute. Now it's restrictive. Sovereign acts, jure imperii, are immune; commercial acts,
jure gestionis, aren't. Trendex: a central bank that failed to honour a letter of credit got no immunity. We look
at the nature of the transaction, not its purpose — so in Playa Largo, Cuba's politically motivated diversion of a
sugar ship was still commercial. Article twelve adds personal injury and property damage in the forum state.

But seriousness alone doesn't remove immunity. In Germany v Italy the ICJ upheld Germany's immunity in civil
claims over Second World War crimes, because immunity is procedural and distinct from the substantive law.

State officials have two immunities. Functional immunity, ratione materiae, covers official acts and survives
office. Personal immunity, ratione personae, covers everything, even private acts, but only for heads of state,
heads of government and foreign ministers, and only while they're in office. Only the state can waive either. In
Arrest Warrant, Belgium's warrant for a sitting Congolese foreign minister breached his immunity — but immunity
isn't impunity: he could be tried at home, after waiver, after leaving office, or before an international court.
And in Pinochet the House of Lords held a former head of state had no immunity for torture under the Convention
against Torture. The ILC's draft article seven would go further, but that's still debated.

Diplomats sit under the Vienna Convention of 1961. Article thirty-one gives full criminal immunity and civil
immunity with three private exceptions; the diplomat's person and home are inviolable, and so is the embassy — no
entry without the ambassador's consent, which is the answer to the Assange question. Consuls get less. Abuse is
met by persona non grata, not arrest.

Finally, international organisations: treaty-based, functional immunities, from Charter article one hundred and
five and the 1946 Convention. Haiti and Srebrenica show the accountability gap that leaves.
