This week asked when the state may use coercion against our freedom. Five ideas do the justifying work: preventing harm to others, preventing offence, preventing harm to self, preventing immoral conduct, and the general welfare. Keep popular morality, what society conventionally thinks right, apart from critical morality, what is objectively right.

Mill's harm principle comes first. The only purpose for which power can rightfully be exercised over anyone against their will is to prevent harm to others. Our own good, physical or moral, is not enough. Harm is narrow: infringing interests we have a duty not to infringe. So the state can stop A assaulting B or stealing B's property, but not stop A destroying her own property or protect someone from being offended. Mill grounds this in autonomy and the presumptive case for liberty, and adds a utilitarian point: experiments in living make everyone happier in the long run. The problems are that harm is too narrow, no one's affairs only affect themselves, and indirect harms are ignored.

Feinberg's offence principle supplements Mill, but only if two tests are met: universality, meaning almost anyone chosen at random would be offended, and reasonable avoidability. Legal paternalism protects people from self-inflicted harm; all states use some soft paternalism, like banning slavery contracts. Its rationale weighs probability, seriousness, the value of the goal and whether the risk is necessary.

Legal moralism says immorality alone justifies criminalisation. Devlin, answering the Wolfenden report, asked three questions and said yes to the first two: without a shared morality society breaks down. The content comes from the man in the jury box, through intolerance, indignation and disgust, and there are no more limits than on laws against treason. George keeps the moralism but enforces critical, objective morality instead.

Hart replies that morality is not a seamless web. He asks whether the conduct is actually harmful, and whether failing to criminalise it would really threaten society. And moral claims must be rationally assessed: is popular morality built on ignorance or superstition, and do we understand the misery punishment causes?

Rawls separates the right from the good and keeps the state neutral, acting only against rights violations. Raz rejects neutrality: the state should promote worthy lives, but never through criminal law.

Finally, the case studies. Traditional pornography bans were moralistic; MacKinnon and Dworkin recast the issue as harm to women and their equality. For euthanasia, keep voluntary and non-voluntary, passive and active apart.
