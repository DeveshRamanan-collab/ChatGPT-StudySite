This week is about the torts that protect our interests in land: trespass to land, and nuisance, both public and private.

Trespass to land is direct interference with land in someone's possession. We need a direct and positive act, done intentionally or negligently, on land the plaintiff possesses, without consent or lawful excuse. It's actionable per se, so no damage needs to be proved, and mistake is no defence — if you intentionally build a fence on what you honestly think is your own land, but it's actually your neighbour's, you've still trespassed. Land includes the soil, fixtures, and the airspace above it, but only up to the height needed for ordinary use and enjoyment — that's why flying an aircraft high above someone's estate in Bernstein v Skyviews wasn't trespass, while a sign poking twenty-four centimetres into the airspace in Kelsen was. And you need exclusive possession, not ownership, to sue — factual control plus an intention to exclude others, as the fifty-year occupier in Newington v Windeyer showed through hosting parties, gardening and paying rates.

Then there's the implied licence: any unlocked, unobstructed driveway carries a licence for lawful communication, as in Halliday v Nevill. But that licence is narrow — it doesn't cover a breath test in Munnings v Barrett, or serving a summons in Plenty v Dillon. Roy v O'Neill adds the twist that having an extra purpose in mind, alongside a licensed one, doesn't automatically make you a trespasser.

Remedies for trespass include damages — nominal, compensatory, aggravated or exemplary, usually measured by diminution in value, sometimes reinstatement — injunctions, and self-help using reasonable force.

Nuisance is different: it's unreasonable interference with the use or enjoyment of land, and it needn't be direct. Public nuisance is a crime, but a private plaintiff can sue in tort if they show special damage — delay and inconvenience dissimilar to the general public's, as in Walsh v Ervin — interference with a public right, and substantial, unreasonable interference.

Private nuisance needs title to sue — traditionally a proprietary interest, as Oldham v Lawson shows, tested by Khorasandjian v Bush and settled by Hunter v Canary Wharf — plus substantial and unreasonable interference, judged objectively as in Munro v Southern Dairies, and proof the defendant created, authorised or continued it. Victoria Park Racing v Taylor reminds us that being overlooked, or losing customers to lawful competition, isn't nuisance, though motive can matter, as the malicious gun-firing in Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett shows.

Defences include a twenty-year prescriptive right, the plaintiff's own conduct or consent — though coming to the nuisance is no defence — statutory authorisation, and contributory negligence. Remedies are damages, injunction, and abatement, used cautiously and only with reasonable methods.
