This week is about two ways to innovate in short cycles, and one warning about them.

Agile innovation is a set of methods that began in software development. It means high-intensity, self-managed team
work, rapid prototyping, testing and learning, methods more open-ended than traditional project management, and tools
like design thinking. The slides name two key roles: the team leader and the process facilitator. Its values come from
the 2001 Agile Manifesto, written against waterfall planning: people over processes and tools, responding to change
over following a plan, working prototypes over excessive documentation, and customer collaboration over rigid
contracts.

Rigby, Sutherland and Takeuchi show agile in practice through scrum. A team of three to nine, cross-functional and
self-managing. An initiative owner who ranks the backlog but never tells the team who does what. A process facilitator
who protects the team. Sprints of less than a month, daily stand-ups, and prototypes tested with a few customers. But
agile is not a panacea. It suits complex problems with unknown solutions, changing markets, modular work and mistakes
that teach; it suits routine work like accounting far less. And it fails most often because of executives, so leaders
should learn it, know where it fits, start small as John Deere did, let master teams customise as Spotify does,
practise it at the top, and destroy the barriers. The tutorial adds the Scrum Guide: a Product Owner, a Development Team and a Scrum Master, and sprints of a month or less that run from planning to review and retrospective.

Lean start-up comes from Steve Blank and Eric Ries. Blank says founders are executing on guesses, start-ups are not
small big companies, and the facts live outside the building. So we build a minimum viable product, just enough to
sell to early adopters and validate demand, and run the build, measure, learn loop as fast as we can. Validated
learning plans it backwards: what do we need to learn, how do we measure it, and what is the simplest thing to build?
Each result is validated, invalidated or inconclusive, and then we pivot or persevere. The business model canvas lays
out nine blocks, each a hypothesis to test.

Finally, Collis. Strategy without entrepreneurship is central planning; entrepreneurship without strategy is chaos.
His lean strategy process sets a vision, then a deliberate strategy of objective, scope and advantage, and lets
experiments run inside those bounds, with strategy as the screen and the yardstick. Groupon and Nasty Gal show what
happens without it; Southwest shows it working. In the exam, argue for experiments inside strategy, not instead of it. Together: agile organises the team, lean start-up tests value, and lean strategy keeps the experiments on course.
