This week is pure economic loss: economic loss that doesn't come from any physical injury to a person or
property. Lost wages after an injury are consequential and easy. Lost profits when someone cuts the cable to your
shop are pure, and pure economic loss needs more than reasonable foreseeability. The reasons are policy:
indeterminate liability, respect for what parties have bargained for in contract, and the fact that lawful
competition causes loss all the time.

Lecture one is negligent words. Derry v Peek said deceit needs an intentional or reckless statement, so careless
words had no remedy until Hedley Byrne v Heller created liability for negligent misstatement where there's a special
relationship: trust, reasonable reliance, and the speaker knowing or having reason to know of that reliance. Hedley
Byrne itself lost, because the reference was given without responsibility. In Evatt, Barwick CJ put it as a serious
matter, a speaker who realises they're trusted, and reliance that's reasonable.

The Australian cases then test reliance. Shaddock found a duty for a council's wrong information about road widening:
information counts, special skill isn't separately required. San Sebastian failed because relying on a statement of
intention isn't reasonable. Tepko failed because the Board didn't know of the reliance and a reluctant ball-park
figure can't reasonably be relied on. Esanda, the leading case, found auditors owed a lender nothing: foreseeability
isn't enough, and there was no request, no intention to induce, and no assumption of responsibility.

So the working list is foreseeability, indeterminacy, the category of relationship, the defendant's knowledge,
reasonable reliance, assumption of responsibility, special skill and vulnerability — weighed, not ticked. A
disclaimer counts against a duty, while a warning goes to reasonable reliance. Mallonland calls assumption of
responsibility imprecise, and Anchorage says sophisticated commercial parties aren't vulnerable. In trade or commerce,
Australian Consumer Law section 18 is often easier, because no duty of care is needed.

Lecture two applies the same salient features approach to acts. In Perre v Apand, diseased seed sold to a neighbour
cost the Perres their WA market; the twenty kilometre rule made the class determinate, the Perres were vulnerable,
Apand knew the risk, and a duty was owed. Caltex Oil had already found a duty for the cost of trucking oil around a
damaged pipeline.

Finally, defective buildings. Bryan v Maloney gave a later homeowner a duty against the builder; Woolcock refused one
to a commercial buyer who could protect itself. In New South Wales, the Design and Building Practitioners Act now does
the work: section 37 gives every subsequent owner a statutory duty, which can't be delegated or contracted out.
