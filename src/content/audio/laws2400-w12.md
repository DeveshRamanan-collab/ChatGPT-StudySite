This week is defences to negligence, and our only source is the textbook's two flowcharts, read with the
Civil Liability Act. Contributory
negligence reduces the damages. Voluntary assumption of risk defeats the claim.

Start with contributory negligence. A finding of contributory negligence results in a partial reduction in the damages
otherwise recoverable, based on what is just and equitable having regard to the plaintiff's share in the
responsibility for the damage. That is the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, section 9(1). On what it means,
the flowchart points to Davies v Swan Motor Co and Jones v Livox Quarries. Three questions do the work. First, the
standard: section 5R of the Civil Liability Act applies the ordinary principles of negligence, so we ask what a
reasonable person in the plaintiff's position would have done, on what they knew or ought to have known at the time.
Second, causation: the plaintiff's lack of care must be causally linked to the injury, Caswell v Powell Duffryn, on
ordinary principles. Third, the size of the reduction: look at the whole conduct of each party and their relative
blameworthiness, Pennington v Norris.

Some situations soften the picture. For employees, inattention bred of familiarity and repetition, the urgency of the
task and preoccupation with the work may be taken into account, McLean v Tedman and Staveley Iron v Jones. In the
agony of the moment, action justified in a perceived emergency may not be contributory negligence, Avram v Gusakoski.
Rescuers get statutory protection: in New South Wales, section 56 defines a good samaritan.

Intoxication has its own rule in section 50. If the plaintiff's capacity for care was impaired by self-induced
intoxication, there are no damages unless the harm was likely anyway. If it was, contributory negligence is presumed
unless the intoxication did not contribute in any way, and damages fall by at least twenty-five per cent.

Now voluntary assumption of risk. It is a complete defence, and a narrow one. The defendant must show the plaintiff
perceived the risk, fully appreciated its nature and extent, and voluntarily accepted that specific risk. Knowing the
general nature of the risk is not enough: Banovic v Perkovic, Carey v Lake Macquarie, ICI v Shatwell. Courts are
reluctant to apply it to passengers of drunk drivers, and some legislation excludes it. Section 5L goes the other way:
no liability for an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity, whether or not the plaintiff knew of it, with
Tapp for guidance. Finally, illegality: in a joint criminal enterprise where no standard of care can be set, the
defence is available, Gala v Preston; where one can be set, it is not, Progress and Properties v Craft.
