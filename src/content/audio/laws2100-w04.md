This week sets natural law against legal positivism, and the first thing to hold on to is exactly where they disagree. Natural law theories dispute the positivist claim that law and morality are conceptually separable. For positivists, law is a matter of social facts. For natural lawyers, law is social facts plus conformity to certain moral standards, of form, content, or both. Fuller is the procedural example, Finnis the substantive one.

Hart defines positivism as the simple contention that it is in no sense a necessary truth that laws satisfy morality, though in fact they often have. He reads the natural law camp as claiming a necessary connection, in two versions. The Thomist version says there are principles of justice discoverable by reason, and that conflicting human laws are not valid law. The other says a legal system needs a widely shared recognition of a moral obligation to obey.

Hart gives natural law a little. His minimum content of natural law is protection of persons, property and promises, a natural necessity drawn from Hobbes and Hume, and his reply to Kelsen's thesis that law may have any content. Then he breaks the necessary connection into six claims and concedes two: morality does influence law, and general, public, prospective rules realise a minimum of justice. But that inner morality, he says, is compatible with very great iniquity.

Fuller answers that a definition of law must make fidelity to law meaningful, and that the real issue is order versus good order. Law is the enterprise of subjecting human conduct to the governance of rules, so laws must be general, public, prospective, clear, coherent, practicable and stable, with officials acting in line with them. Rex fails not because his laws are unjust, but because nobody could follow them.

Finnis attacks method. No theorist can describe social facts without also evaluating. Hart and Raz improved on Bentham, Austin and Kelsen through practical point, central case and viewpoint, but choosing what is central is already a judgement of importance. Hart's internal point of view is a mix of attitudes; Finnis wants its central case, the practically reasonable person.

That gives Finnis his reading of lex iniusta non est lex. The strong thesis says unjust laws are not legally valid, and for Radbruch only extremely unjust ones. Finnis holds the weak thesis: unjust laws are not laws in their central case, like a fallacious argument, defective morally and legally, but not simply void. For the essay, name the precise claim you are testing.
