Week one sets up the whole unit with one line from Theodore Levitt: creativity is thinking up new things, and
innovation is doing new things. That is how the semester runs too. Weeks one to seven are for thinking up new things,
and weeks eight to thirteen are for doing them, along a path from vision, to imagination, to creativity, to innovation.

The unit is about a different mindset: zig when everyone else is zagging. The slides call it the flip, repeatedly
moving between different perspectives and paradigms, in real time and on demand. It is flexible and counter-intuitive,
and the lecture says it matters now because business is fast moving, and because AI can hand anyone technical
knowledge, so technical knowledge alone is not enough.

What is creativity? Einstein's definition: seeing what everyone else has seen, and thinking what no-one else has
thought. Creative thinking is simply the ability to think, imagine or invent something new, even taking a different
route to campus. It isn't just about the arts. In business we look at creative methods like dropshipping, where you
never hold the stock and the supplier ships straight to your customer. And can it be learnt? Ken Robinson says people
who think they aren't creative just haven't learnt what is involved.

So how do we become more creative? Six methods: mind mapping, brainstorming, sketching, free writing, thinking
opposites and seeking inspiration. Even more important is unleashing creativity in a group. Skill builds like a
workout at the creativity gym: starting off, learning the skills; being proficient, training your mind and getting
inspiration; and becoming an expert, where creative mode is your default and you seek feedback.

Then the competencies. Individual: exploring problems, willpower, self-awareness, and being open to failure and
feedback. Team: supporting each other's ideas, psychological safety, shared visions and pushing the frontiers.
Psychological safety means feeling safe not to relax, but to take risks, so nobody holds back an idea for fear of
looking stupid.

The reading, Empire of Tech, is our worked example. Bob Iger ran Disney as CEO and, in effect, as its CTO. He bet early
and against the business model, putting ABC shows on iTunes in 2005. He gave every business its own CTO so it could
experiment and fail without a watchdog. Imagineering's virtual reality Dish is creativity, still too costly for the
parks; MagicBands, rolled out to millions of guests, are innovation. And not every bet paid: the interactive division
lost over a billion dollars before it learnt its lesson.
