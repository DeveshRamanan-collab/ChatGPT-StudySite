Let's recap Week 1. Tort law comes from the Latin *tortum*, something twisted or wrong, and it deals with civil
wrongs: avoid harming others, and if you do, remedy the harm, usually through compensation. There's a long
debate about whether it's the "law of tort" or the "law of torts" — Salmond said it's just a miscellaneous
list of causes of action, while Pollock and Winfield argued there's a set of unifying principles underneath it
all. Tort law is mostly judge-made, but statutes like the Civil Liability Act 2002 in New South Wales are
increasingly important, often responding to problems like the cost of insurance. Statutes don't replace the
common law entirely — they modify it, abolish bits of it, and add new causes of action, so cases and statutes
now co-exist.

We also placed tort law next to its neighbours. Against criminal law: tort is about private wrongs and
compensation, crime is about public wrongs and punishment, and the standards differ too — balance of
probabilities for tort, beyond reasonable doubt for crime — though the line blurs, since something like assault
can be both a tort and a crime. Against contract law: tort duties come from the general law and bind everyone,
contract duties come from agreement and bind only the parties, and tort damages are unliquidated where contract
damages are liquidated. And tort overlaps with human rights law in protecting bodily integrity, reputation and
property, even though Australia has no comprehensive federal human rights statute.

Then we looked at theories of tort law's role in society. The compensatory function puts the victim back in the
position they'd have been in, money allowing — though future-earnings calculations and legal costs make that
messy in practice. The deterrence function says liability makes people avoid the harmful conduct — Calabresi and
Posner push this into a systemic, economic argument about efficiently allocating the cost of accidents — but
it's genuinely unclear how well this deters individuals, and insurance might blunt it further. Weinrib's
corrective justice says a wrongdoer should personally correct the harm they caused, resting on individual moral
responsibility, though that can ignore the many other people and systems really involved. Distributive justice
treats tort as a way of spreading risk and loss across society, like a no-fault road accident scheme that only
needs proof of causation, not fault. And feminist theory asks whether tort law reflects women's experiences,
pointing to the reasonable person standard and how women's unpaid work gets valued in damages. We also met
vicarious liability early: an employer is liable for an employee's tort in the course of employment, and
Fleming's reasons are that the employer profits from the enterprise, is a better source of recompense than an
employee who's often uninsured, spreads losses through insurance and prices, and is well placed to prevent
accidents by supervising staff.

Finally, the elements of intentional torts. Two families developed historically: trespass, for direct
interference with the person, property or goods, and action on the case, for indirect, consequential damage.
All trespass claims share four elements: a voluntary, positive act — not an epileptic fit, third-party force, or
an omission; directness — Hutchins v Maughan shows baits laid before the dogs arrived were only consequential,
not direct, so trespass failed, because directness is really an issue of timing; being actionable per se, so no
damage needs to be proved; and fault — intention or recklessness, which are subjective, or negligence, which is
objective, judged by the reasonable person. Intention only needs to cover the act itself, not its consequences,
so a mistaken touch is still intentional. The plaintiff just proves the direct interference, and the onus shifts
to the defendant to disprove fault — except on the highway, where the plaintiff must prove fault instead,
because road users accept some shared risk. Williams v Milotin shows all of this still has real consequences
today, since the same accident could be pleaded as trespass or negligence, each with a different limitation
period.
