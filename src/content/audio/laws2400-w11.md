This week is the second half of causation. Section 5D(1) of the Civil Liability Act gives two elements: factual causation, meaning the negligence was a necessary condition of the harm, and scope of liability, meaning it is appropriate for the negligent person's liability to extend to the harm so caused. For scope of liability, section 5D(4) tells the court to consider whether or not and why responsibility should be imposed. That is openly policy-based, and courts have to say what the policy is, as Wallace v Kam puts it; and it is normative, a question of whether the defendant ought to be liable, as in Ruddock v Taylor.

Scope of liability then splits into two common law ideas. The first is the new intervening act. 'But for' causation is there, but something happened later. Does it break the chain? The Oropesa asks whether the later event is ultroneous, unwarrantable, a new cause, unreasonable or extraneous. To sever the chain, the later act must ordinarily be voluntary human action, meaning volitional conduct, or an extremely unlikely, causally independent coincidence, which is Haber v Walker. The flowchart's volitional cases that severed the chain are Rickards v Lothian, Yates v Jones and CES v Superclinics. Its non-volitional cases that left the chain intact are Urbanski v Patel, Haynes v Harwood, Medlin and Hirst v Nominal Defendant.

When the later act is itself negligence, by a third party or even the plaintiff, it's a matter of fact and degree under Mahony v Kruschich, and it does not necessarily break the chain, with Burns v Pearce. That's especially so when the defendant created the very risk that the later negligence brought about, and the injury happened in the ordinary course of things. That is March v Stramare.

The second idea is remoteness, which governs the extent of liability for losses. The Wagon Mound number one is the leading case: the test is reasonable foreseeability. The object is different from duty and breach, because we ask what damage was foreseeable, but the meaning is the same. The Wagon Mound number two says a real risk is one a reasonable person wouldn't brush aside as far-fetched. The kind of harm has to be foreseeable; its extent and the precise manner it happened do not, as in Hughes v Lord Advocate and Jolley.

Last, the egg-shell skull rule from Nader: the defendant takes the plaintiff with all their weaknesses, beliefs and reactions, and their capacities and attributes, physical, social and economic. Once liability is established, it governs the damages.
