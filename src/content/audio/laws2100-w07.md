This week connects legal reasoning with the big question, what is law. We start with a surprise. Raz, the positivist, says he rejects the strong thesis of the autonomy of legal reasoning: legal reasoning is an instance of moral reasoning, though sometimes based on mistaken moral principles. Finnis, the natural law thinker, says legal reasoning is technical reasoning, at least in large part not moral reasoning, and that natural reason cannot claim the one right answer when the sources are unclear. The lesson is that specific claims are separable from general theories of law, and that the real question is whether legal reasoning differs in kind from other practical reasoning.

We then sort the tools. Six types of reasoning: deductive, inductive, analogical, abductive, purposive, and reasoning by principle. Five types of rules: mandatory, prohibitive, permissive, power-conferring and constitutive. None of them is unique to law.

Schauer asks whether lawyers think differently from ordinary people. His answer is that legal reasoning is distinctive by concentration: methods found everywhere but concentrated and dominant in law. And he says that claim is, in the end, empirical. He also calls the vehicles-in-the-park exchange a debate within a debate: can we interpret a word in a statute without knowing its aim?

Fuller's Speluncean Explorers gives us five options on one clear statute. Truepenny applies it and hopes for clemency. Foster says positive law did not apply in a state of nature, and alternatively reads the statute by its purpose, like self-defence. Tatting withdraws. Keen applies the plain meaning because the legislature is supreme. Handy uses common sense and public opinion, the realist. Fuller says these are permanent problems of the human race.

Next, rules and standards. Schauer's convergence hypothesis says that over time rules are avoided, for example by exceptions, and standards are made concrete, so the choice manages discretion less than we think. Hart replies to Llewellyn that a rule ending with unless is still a rule. A defeasible rule's conclusion can be defeated by further criteria, as in Church of the Holy Trinity, where a minister was not a foreign labourer, and as in Riggs.

Finally, discretion. For Hart, formalism and rule-scepticism are Scylla and Charybdis. In partly unregulated cases judges make law, but interstitially, under constraints, and with general reasons. Dworkin says this is false, undemocratic and retrospective. Hart replies that judges keep their law books, the democratic cost is small, and in hard cases there are no settled expectations to protect.
