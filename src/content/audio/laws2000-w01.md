This week we asked a simple-sounding question with three different answers: what is international
law? The Attorney-General's Department calls it rules and principles that facilitate how states and
international organisations relate to one another, and sometimes to individuals and companies too. The
Practitioner's Guide stresses that states recognise it as law only because they voluntarily consent to
it. And the Permanent Court of International Justice, in S.S. Lotus, gave us the sharpest version: the
rules that bind states come from their own free will, expressed in conventions or accepted usage, so
restrictions on a state's independence can never just be presumed. Keep all three in your back pocket
— they each do different work in an exam answer.

We also saw that international law is genuinely everywhere, not just in headline disputes: the
Greenwich meridian that sets our clocks, the vehicle standards our cars meet, even the internet
infrastructure carrying this lecture all rest on treaties most of us never think about. That's the
'general' international law this unit teaches — sources, treaties, statehood, jurisdiction, state
responsibility, dispute settlement — sitting alongside 'special' regimes like human rights, the law of
the sea, or environmental law, which rarely operate alone: a migrant rescue at sea pulls in migration
law, the law of the sea and human rights law all at once.

Then we went back in time. From the Greek polis and Roman jus naturale, through Grotius separating
natural law from jus gentium, and Vitoria insisting natural law protected the peoples of the New World,
we arrived at 1648 and the Peace of Westphalia — the real hinge of the story, where sovereignty as we
know it was born. From there, nineteenth-century positivism made state consent everything, building
institutions like the Universal Postal Union and the Hague Conferences, before two world wars produced
the League of Nations, the Kellogg-Briand Pact, the Permanent Court of International Justice, and
finally the UN Charter itself in 1946, with its Security Council, General Assembly, sovereign equality
and ban on the use of force.

Two more ideas tie the week together. First, international law is a living process — new treaties,
amended treaties, protocols, and custom built from state practice and belief, illustrated perfectly by
environmental law's growth from almost nothing in the 1960s to the Paris Agreement, and by the ozone
layer's dramatic recovery once the Vienna Convention and Montreal Protocol took effect. Second, and
maybe most importantly for exam answers: international law has no legislature, executive or police, yet
states comply almost all of the time, almost entirely because of reputation, quasi-enforcement like
suspension from an organisation, and a shared sense that the system is worth keeping — not because
anyone can force them to. As Philippe Sands put it, no one is starry-eyed about international law, but
it's what we have.
