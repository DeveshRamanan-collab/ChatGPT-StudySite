This week is about defences to intentional torts — consent, self-defence, legal process, provocation, contributory negligence and necessity — and a thread running through all of them: is a defence really outside a tort's elements, or part of what the plaintiff must prove?

Consent is available for battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass to land and interference with chattels, resting on respect for autonomy. Australian law hasn't settled whether it's a defence or an element — McHugh J in Marion's Case thought it a defence the defendant proves, while Leeming JA in White v Johnston thought it an element the plaintiff disproves — and that changes who loses on unclear evidence. To be valid, consent must be voluntary, come from a competent person, and match the act actually agreed to. A false promise of marriage doesn't nullify consent if you understood the nature of the act, but exceeding what was agreed to does — McNamara v Duncan's deliberate football blow, and Dean v Phung's fifty-three unnecessary dental consultations, both went beyond the consent given. Capacity matters too: adults are presumed competent, but Rossiter shows even a paralysed, dying patient's competent refusal must be respected, while Gillick and Marion's Case let a sufficiently mature minor consent — though X v Sydney Children's Hospital Network shows the court's parens patriae power can still override even a competent minor's wishes.

Self-defence needs a reasonable apprehension of force plus a proportionate response — Zecevic v DPP's two limbs — and Fontin v Katapodis shows how strictly proportionality is policed. The Civil Liability Act's section 52 runs alongside the common law with its own test, adding a requirement that the conduct responded to was unlawful; NSW v McMaster shows both tests can be satisfied by police using force in a chaotic, dangerous moment.

Legal process covers lawful arrest — LEPRA's section 99 needs both reasonable suspicion and necessity for a listed reason, with force limited to what's reasonably necessary — and entering or remaining on land, building on Halliday v Nevill's implied licence and extended in Roy v O'Neill, where observing intoxication gave independent authority to stay for a breath test.

Provocation and contributory negligence are both largely rejected as tort defences: Fontin v Katapodis says provocation can't cut compensatory damages, only exemplary ones, and Horkin v North Melbourne Football Club says intentional wrongdoing and mere carelessness differ in kind, not just degree, so a plaintiff's carelessness shouldn't excuse a defendant's deliberate wrong.

Finally, necessity — narrow, as Southwark v Williams shows for homelessness, but real for protecting property, even a stranger's, as in Proudman v Allen, and for medical treatment under In re F's best-interests test, which treats temporary and permanent incapacity differently.
