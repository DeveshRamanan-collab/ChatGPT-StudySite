This week we moved from creativity to innovation. The slides define an innovation as an idea, practice or object perceived as new by an individual or other unit of adoption — a use of new knowledge to offer a new product or service that customers want. In short, invention plus commercialisation. If you cannot commercialise it, it is creativity, not innovation. Invention creates something new and may never leave the R and D centre; innovation introduces it into the marketplace, has an economic motive and changes the organisation.

The Venn diagram turns that into a test. Innovation sits where three circles overlap: desirable to users, possible with technology, viable in the marketplace. The jetpack is desirable but only now technically arriving, and extremely expensive. Lego's plastic-free brick, in Reed's article, is held up by the material: around two hundred alternatives, and most have fallen short.

Why innovate? Because in a turbulent economy survival depends on it — the slide's example is Apple. The goals run from improving quality and creating new markets to reducing materials, energy and environmental damage, and conforming to regulations. The sources are organic structure, long managerial tenure, slack resources and intra-communications — and remember that tenure and slack cut both ways: legitimacy can become complacency, and plenty of money can make a firm feel unbeatable.

The innovation map has two axes. Operational innovation is internal and continuous; management innovation internal and discontinuous; product and service innovation external and continuous; strategic innovation, where disruption sits, external and discontinuous. The class activity warns that not every successful or radical innovation is disruptive. Christensen's disruption starts with overlooked, low-end or new-market customers and a simpler, cheaper offer, then moves toward the mainstream. Walk the questions: incumbent, initial customer, value proposition, market movement, verdict.

The innovation process runs from scanning the environment and aligning strategy, through acquiring or generating technology, exploring and selecting, executing, learning, and developing the organisation. Innovation management is the search for the most appropriate solution, effective routines and managed learning.

Value innovation creates superior buyer value in existing markets, and technology only counts if it is cheap enough for mass buyers — think Uber. What-if questions, like IKEA, Skype and Grameenphone, are starting points: the work is finding the business model. Mattimore's seven tools help: questioning assumptions, opportunity redefinition, wishing, triggered brainwalking, semantic intuition, picture prompts and worst idea. Finally, Brown and Wyatt's design thinking works through overlapping spaces — inspiration, ideation and implementation — grounded in what people actually do, as Shanti's water shows.
