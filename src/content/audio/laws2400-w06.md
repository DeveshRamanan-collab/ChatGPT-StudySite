This week is about the factors that push a fact pattern into, or out of, an accepted category of duty of
care. We start with a three-part checklist: how the harm was caused, whether by an act or an omission; who
the defendant is, a private individual or a public authority, and whether there's a special relationship
like occupier and entrant; and what kind of harm resulted, physical, psychiatric, or pure economic loss.
Getting this classification right tells us which rules and which part of the Civil Liability Act to reach
for next.

Omissions need more than foreseeability. Hargrave v Goldman adds a special relationship, knowledge of the
danger, and control over it. Control does most of the work: in Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre v Anzil,
the shopping centre controlled its car park lighting but not the criminals who attacked Anzil there, so its
duty didn't extend that far. We also met the good samaritan provisions, sections 55 to 58, which protect
someone who helps in good faith unless they caused the risk themselves.

Occupiers get their own obvious-risk rules. Romeo v Conservation Commission says duty isn't owed in the
abstract — the real question is its scope, and there's no duty to warn of an obvious danger like an
unfenced cliff. Sections 5F, 5G and 5H codify this: obvious risk is judged objectively, the plaintiff is
presumed aware of it, and there's no proactive duty to warn. Product liability picks up the same cost-benefit
thinking: in Graham Barclay Oysters, the risk of contaminated oysters was foreseeable, but eliminating it
entirely wasn't a reasonable response given the cost.

Sport gets its own version of the same idea. Rootes v Shelton separates the inherent risk of a sport from a
distinct failure of care, like the observer's failure to warn of a stationary boat. Agar v Hyde shows a duty
won't extend to a remote regulatory body just because a sport is inherently dangerous. Sections 5L and 5M
give dangerous recreational activities their own liability shield, and a risk warning removes the duty
altogether.

Finally, psychiatric harm. Tame and Annetts, decided together, killed off the old control mechanisms —
normal fortitude, sudden shock, direct perception — as fixed preconditions, leaving foreseeability of a
recognised illness as the real test. For NSW cases, section 32 now sets that duty test, section 31 needs a
recognised illness, and section 30 limits pure mental harm from shock to scene-witnesses and close family.
Wicks tells us to apply section 32 before section 30, and that peril from something like a train derailment
can stretch out over time, not just an instant.
