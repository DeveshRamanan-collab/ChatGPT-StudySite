Week twelve is about leading an innovative culture. Start with the overlap: innovation plus culture gives an innovation culture, and the slides give it five characteristics. It shows organisation-wide commitment to the business and to innovation, it experiments proactively, it manages risk, it can implement ideas quickly, and it produces and cultivates innovators. We see commitment when people care about their work, their co-workers and the company; we see resistance as lack of follow-through and lack of engagement. Companies that don't experiment say things like "if it ain't broke, don't fix it" or "we've always done it this way". And a culture that doesn't produce innovators punishes failure, leaves people reluctant to speak up, and keeps innovation top-down.

Each culture trait has a leader trait. Commitment matches passion about innovation, experimenting matches willingness to experiment, managing risk matches being realistic and informed, quick implementation matches rapid project execution, and cultivating innovators matches attracting and nurturing them. Steve Jobs puts it simply: innovation distinguishes between a leader and a follower.

Then come seven challenges of managing innovation: why change, what to change, understanding innovation, building an innovation culture through routines, continuous learning, high involvement innovation, and managing connections, which means working with others rather than in splendid isolation.

Barriers are a separate list. External ones are market-related, government and its policies, and others: technical, societal and inter-organisational. Internal ones are people, structure and strategy. To overcome them, the slides give eight components: shared vision and leadership, appropriate culture, key individuals, effective team working, continuing individual development, extensive communication, a creative climate and a learning organisation. The conclusion: flexible planning and control, respect for individual initiative, and tolerance for mistakes.

The reading gives us Gilbert and Tobin. Sam Nickless saw NewLaw firms like LegalVision doing low-value, high-volume work, and expected disruption to move up the market. Instead of building its own version, which would have clashed with the firm's incentives and culture, Gilbert and Tobin bought a twenty per cent stake in LegalVision for about four million dollars, after checking it with mystery shoppers and its own top lawyers. That is managing connections, and learning from disruption.

Finally, Shelley Simpson's guest lecture puts it all in one story: experimenting widely, learning logistics and compliance as she grew, building relationships and her own stores, staying careful about investors and growth, and asking her team to lean in.
