Week three is about legal positivism, and it starts with the two most influential positivists of the twentieth
century, Hart and Kelsen.

Hart makes a fresh start from Austin's failure. The coercive-orders theory got one thing right: where there is law,
conduct is made non-optional. But being obliged, as by a gunman, is not the same as having an obligation, as with the
tax officer. To explain the difference Hart uses the social rule. Unlike a habit, a rule attracts criticism when broken,
is treated as a good reason for that criticism, and has an internal aspect, a critical reflective attitude to a pattern
as a common standard. Habits cannot confer rights or authority on anyone.

A society with only primary rules of obligation has three defects: uncertainty, a static character and inefficiency.
The remedies are secondary rules: the rule of recognition, rules of change and rules of adjudication. Their union is the
heart of a legal system. International law, for Hart, is law but not a legal system.

The rule of recognition gives authoritative criteria for identifying the rules, and it exists as a practice, kept alive
by officials. A rule is valid if it passes its tests, so validity is membership. The rule itself is neither valid nor
invalid, like the standard metre in Paris. A legal system exists when valid rules are generally obeyed and officials
accept the secondary rules as common public standards.

Kelsen sees law as a coercive order that regulates its own creation. The tax officer differs from the gangster because
his command is authorised by a valid norm. Validity is binding force, running up a chain of authorisation to the
historically first constitution and then to a presupposed basic norm: obey the first constitution. For Hart the rule of
recognition is a fact whose content varies; for Kelsen the basic norm is a presupposition with the same content
everywhere. Kelsen's theory is pure: it asks what law is, not what it ought to be.

So what is legal positivism? Green and Adams say the existence and content of law depend on social facts, not merits.
Hart says it is no necessary truth that laws satisfy morality. Bentham and Austin asserted two simple things: immorality
alone does not stop a rule being law, and moral desirability alone does not make it law. Hart lists five meanings of
positivism; Bentham and Austin held the first three, Kelsen the second, third and fifth. Finally, keep three senses
apart: a general theory of law, a methodology, and a political theory. The essay's first question is about the second.
