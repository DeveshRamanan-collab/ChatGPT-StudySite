International criminal law is the branch of public international law about crimes for which individuals are
personally, criminally liable. It has two limbs: substantive law, which governs the four core crimes and demands that
states prosecute them, and procedural law, which runs the proceedings — trial, appeal, evidence, sentencing and
victims' rights. It is new, still rudimentary because the elements and penalties are not always clear, and hybrid,
drawing on humanitarian law, human rights law and national criminal law.

It began after the Second World War. The London Agreement, with the IMT Charter annexed, created the Nuremberg
tribunal, which tried crimes against peace, war crimes and crimes against humanity. In Tokyo, MacArthur's proclamation
set up the IMTFE: twenty-eight defendants, fifty-five counts, seven death sentences. In 1946 the General Assembly
affirmed the Nuremberg Principles in Resolution 95(I): any person who commits an international crime is responsible;
internal law is no excuse; nor is being head of state or an official; nor are superior orders, if a moral choice was
in fact possible.

In the 1990s the Security Council created two ad hoc tribunals under Chapter VII. The ICTY came from Resolution 827 in
1993, and all states had to cooperate, because members carry out Council decisions under article 25 of the Charter.
The ICTR followed in November 1994, for Rwanda in 1994; Akayesu found that sexual violence can be the actus reus of
genocide. Hybrid courts like the Extraordinary Chambers in Cambodia work differently: they came from an agreement with
the state, sit in its courts, and mix national and international prosecutors and judges.

The ICC is a treaty court under the Rome Statute. For a problem, walk four gates. Article 5: is it genocide, crimes
against humanity, war crimes or aggression? Piracy is not. Article 11: was it committed after the Statute entered
into force for that state, unless there is an article 12(3) declaration? Article 13: was it referred by a State Party
or by the Security Council, or opened by the Prosecutor? And for a state referral or the Prosecutor's own
investigation, article 12: is the territorial or flag state, or the accused's nationality state, a party or a
declarant? Then admissibility under article 17: the Court is complementary, so a case genuinely handled by a state is
inadmissible, unless that state is unwilling — shielding, delay, partiality — or unable because its system has
collapsed. Finally, the Court applies its Statute first, then international law, then general principles, and it
relies on states' cooperation under articles 86 and 88.
