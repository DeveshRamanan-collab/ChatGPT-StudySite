This week is outsider jurisprudence, an umbrella term for theories that look at law from the standpoint of those it marginalises. Inside and outside of what? Of law. On Hart's account a legal system exists when officials accept its secondary rules and citizens generally comply with its primary rules, perhaps only out of fear. So many people are subjects of the law, even its victims. The same distinction applies to legal theory: there is a canon, analytical jurisprudence, and there are challengers. That is why outsider jurisprudence helps us rethink both what is law and what is legal theory. These theories combine analysis with political commitment, so we judge them partly by their practical achievements.

Feminism comes in four main versions. Liberal feminism wants equal treatment and the removal of formal barriers, which gave us the Sex Discrimination Act. But formal equality did not become substantive equality, and it quietly used the male norm as its benchmark. Difference feminism, with Gilligan's ethics of care, values women's perspective, at the risk of reinforcing stereotypes. Radical feminism, with MacKinnon, says women have no voice at all, because women as a class are dominated by men as a class; critics ask whether it sees women only as victims. Postmodern feminism rejects essentialism and stresses intersectionality, but Naffine asks when we know enough, and who will speak for women.

Separate spheres put men in public and women at home; Bradley J in Bradwell said the domestic sphere belonged to womanhood. The answer is that the personal is the political: private inequality causes public disadvantage, power runs through the home, and the family is a political institution. Reforms followed: provocation tightened, the marital rape exception removed, and NSW consent reforms in 2021.

Naffine says feminist legal theory challenges the ways law is not impartial, using consciousness raising, naming injuries and getting them legally recognised, like sexual harassment. Feminist theorists are the outsider within, with an epistemic privilege of marginality and a generous, revisable methodology: largess, not orthodoxy.

Lacey lists the conventional assumptions under challenge, such as law's neutrality, autonomy and unity, and asks what work the idea of community does. Every theory we studied assumes one, from Austin's political society to Dworkin's integrity.

Finally, critical race theory, from the US in the 1970s: race is constructed, racism can coexist with formal neutrality, storytelling gives voice, whiteness works as property, and the theory is normative as well as descriptive. Moreton-Robinson shows how white Australian feminism silenced Indigenous women.
