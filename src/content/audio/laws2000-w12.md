Human rights started as domestic texts — Magna Carta, the English Bill of Rights, the 1789 declarations in France and
the United States — and as Enlightenment ideas: Locke's natural freedom and equality, with rights to life, liberty and
property that don't depend on any society's laws, and Kant's dignity, treating people as ends and never merely as
means. International protection began issue by issue, with slavery and limited women's rights, and after the Second
World War the UN Charter made human rights one of the UN's purposes, in articles 1(3), 55 and 56.

The International Bill of Rights is the Universal Declaration of 1948 plus the two Covenants of 1966, the ICCPR and the
ICESCR. Watch the numbering: article 6 is life in one and work in the other, article 11 is contract debts in one and
food, clothing and housing in the other. Around them sit nine core treaties, from the racial discrimination convention
in 1965 to the disability and enforced disappearance conventions in 2006. Obligations come from treaties and from
custom — torture, genocide, racial discrimination — and some of these are jus cogens.

There are three generations of rights: civil and political, economic social and cultural, and collective rights like
self-determination. They are categories, not ranks, because rights are indivisible and interdependent. Universality
says rights apply to everyone; relativists call that cultural imperialism, and universalists answer that relativism
often masks oppression and that culture changes.

States are the main duty bearers. Vertically they must respect rights themselves; horizontally they must protect people
from private actors, as General Comment 31 explains. International organisations can be bound too. Obligations follow
territory and jurisdiction, whatever the victim's nationality, and they travel abroad — the Wall opinion, and Uganda as
occupying power in Ituri, answering for its own soldiers and for failing to watch the rebels.

In an emergency, article 4 allows derogation only if the emergency threatens the life of the nation and is proclaimed,
only as far as strictly required, without discrimination, and never from articles like 6 and 7. Aksoy shows fourteen
days without a judge going too far. Outside emergencies, limits need law, a legitimate aim and democratic necessity.

Finally the machinery: the Human Rights Council, its Universal Periodic Review and special procedures, and the treaty
committees with their reports, complaints and general comments. Their views aren't binding, but, as Diallo says, they
deserve great weight.
