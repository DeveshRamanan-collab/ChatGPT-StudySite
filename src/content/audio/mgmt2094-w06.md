Week six is about two things: writing a reflection, and the two patterns of creative thinking, diverging and converging.

Start with why we reflect. Having an experience is not enough to learn from it. Without reflecting, it may be quickly forgotten, or its learning potential lost. Moon calls reflection a form of mental processing with a purpose, and the slide adds the line attributed to John Dewey: we learn from reflecting on experience. The lecture's picture is Dumbledore's Pensieve. When his mind is crammed with thoughts, he pours them into a stone basin and looks at them at leisure, and patterns and links become easier to see. Reflective writing is our basin.

Reflection has five purposes: recognise how we learn, critically review behaviour, ours or others', build theory from what we observe, develop ourselves, and make decisions or resolve uncertainty. For us, all of that is applied to the group project and the theory behind it.

Then, reveal information wisely. A reflective paper is personal, but we don't reveal everything; we ask whether something is appropriate. If we are uncomfortable about a reflection, we leave it out, unless it changed the final outcome, and then we say what concerns us about revealing it. The tone stays professional and organised, and we never drag a teammate down; we describe their influence with detachment.

On style: we can use I, but it is not a diary. Feelings need specific evidence. No slang or jargon, no fragments, no rambling, one idea per sentence, varied sentence length, paragraphs, and transitional phrases like for example, as a result, or a different perspective is, to link an experience to a conclusion.

And the warning at the end: do not just describe; analyse and reflect. Ask what I learnt, what I now know, what insights I gained, how well we handled the challenges, and what I would do differently next time.

In the tutorial we were the product team for the Boring, Drab and Awful Floor Company: a hundred floor covering ideas in ten minutes, and no just adding soft or blue. The creativity tool gives four columns of prompts: try to, make it, add or subtract, and imagine that you. Diverging means defer judgement, lots of ideas, accept them all, stretch, simmer and combine odd things. Converging means look for what is really new and exciting, avoid premature closure, face the sneaky spots, judge affirmatively, and keep the objective in view. Picking our top three was converging.
