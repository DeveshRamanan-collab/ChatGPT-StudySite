This week is breach of duty, the second element of negligence. Once a duty is established, the court measures the defendant's conduct against a standard of care, on the balance of probabilities. There are no degrees of carelessness: marginally careless and completely reckless are both breach. The duty is to take reasonable care, not to prevent harm, so leftover risk is not breach by itself. Swain says so, and so did Gummow J in Dederer. Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks gives Alderson B's definition: failing to do what the reasonable man would do, or doing what he would not.

At common law, Wyong Shire Council v Shirt set two limbs: a reasonably foreseeable real risk, and a reasonable response. In New South Wales, section 5B of the Civil Liability Act now governs breach, despite its duty of care heading, and it presupposes the common law.

Benic gives the method. Step one, identify the risk of harm, as a class of injury, specific enough to match the precautions. In Dederer, the Court of Appeal's "spinal injury from diving off the bridge" obscured the true source, shallow water over shifting sands. Step two, the three elements, each separately.

First, the risk was foreseeable: one the defendant knew or ought to have known of, at the time. Mason J in Wyong said foreseeability and likelihood are different things, so even an unlikely risk can be foreseeable if it isn't far-fetched or fanciful.

Second, the risk was not insignificant. That's a deliberate step up from not far-fetched or fanciful, though below a substantial risk, as the Ipp Report explains. Rail Corporation v Donald weighs obviousness, likelihood and seriousness.

Third, a reasonable person in the defendant's position would have taken the precautions. Subsection 2 is the calculus, weighed together. Probability, judged prospectively, low in Dederer. Seriousness, raised by known vulnerability in Paris. Burden, which in Woods included the availability and safety of helmets. Social utility, the blood supply in the Red Cross case. Section 5C adds that later fixes and other ways of doing things don't prove liability by themselves. Obvious and inherent risks, under sections 5F to 5L, also shape the response.

Finally, the reasonable person. Children are judged as normal children of their age, McHale v Watson. Mental illness doesn't lower the standard, Carrier v Bonham, and neither does inexperience, Imbree. Professionals are judged by their skill, Rogers v Whitaker, but section 5O is a defence: apply it first, and only if it fails go to section 5B, as Peterkin v Tadros explains.
