This week is about the torts that protect our bodies, our liberty and our belongings. Start with battery: a
direct, intentional or negligent act that makes physical contact with someone's body without consent or lawful
excuse. You need five things — a positive act, directness, intention or negligence, physical contact, and
unlawful touching. Standing still in a doorway isn't a positive act, that's Innes v Wylie, but if you've
already driven onto someone's foot and then just don't move, that failure to move still counts, from Fagan.
Contact doesn't need to be skin on skin either — tear gas touching someone's eyes was enough in Binsaris. And
the trickiest bit is unlawful touching: it doesn't have to be angry or hostile, Rixon v Star City tells us the
real question is whether the contact goes beyond what's generally acceptable in everyday life, like a tap on
the shoulder to get someone's attention.

Assault is different — no touching required at all. You just need a positive, intentional, direct act that
creates a reasonable apprehension of imminent contact. The intention is to create that apprehension, not to
actually follow through, again from Rixon. And imminent doesn't mean immediate — Zanker v Vartzokas shows a
threat can be imminent if you can't escape before it happens, even if the harm is a little way off.

False imprisonment protects liberty, not the body. Total restraint, with no reasonable means of escape, and no
lawful justification. No physical walls needed — you can even be imprisoned without knowing it at the time. But
a condition on entry, like paying to leave a wharf in Balmain New Ferry, gives a lawful excuse.

When trespass doesn't fit because the harm is indirect, we turn to the action on the case: malicious
prosecution needs initiation of proceedings, a favourable outcome, malice, and no reasonable and probable
cause — A v New South Wales says these last two are separate. And Wilkinson v Downton covers a wilful act
calculated to cause harm, like a cruel joke that triggers nervous shock — Nationwide News v Naidu adds that
reckless indifference is enough, you needn't specifically intend the harm.

Remedies can be nominal, compensatory, aggravated or exemplary, each doing a different job — restoring the
plaintiff, punishing deliberate affronts, or punishing outright egregious conduct.

Finally, goods. Trespass to goods needs direct interference with something you actually possess — Penfolds
Wines shows possession, not ownership, counts. Conversion needs a serious dealing inconsistent with the
owner's dominion, and only a right to immediate possession, like Bunnings v CHEP shows with its line between
storing and transporting pallets. Detinue just needs a demand and a wrongful refusal to get the item back.
