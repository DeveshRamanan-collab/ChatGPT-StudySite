State responsibility is the set of secondary rules that tells us when a state has breached a primary rule —
something in a treaty or in custom, like the prohibition on the use of force — and what follows. The ILC codified
it in the Articles on State Responsibility, ARSIWA, adopted on 10 August 2001. They are not a treaty and not
binding as such, but many articles reflect customary international law.

Article 1 says every internationally wrongful act entails the state's responsibility, and article 2 gives us two
elements: conduct, an act or an omission, that is attributable to the state, and that breaches an international
obligation owed by it. For breach, the source does not matter, it just has to be international; the state must
be bound at the time; small acts can add up to a composite breach; and omissions count — Iran failing to protect
the embassy in Tehran, Albania failing to warn about the mines in Corfu Channel.

Attribution starts from the rule that a state answers for its own conduct. Article 4 covers all its organs,
including states within Australia, and exceptionally de facto organs in complete dependence. Article 5 covers
entities empowered by law to exercise governmental authority, when acting in that capacity. Article 6 covers
organs lent by another state, and article 7 keeps ultra vires conduct attributable, as in Velásquez Rodríguez.
Article 10 catches insurgents who become the government, and article 11 catches conduct the state acknowledges
and adopts, like Iran's decree in Tehran.

Private groups come under article 8: specific instructions, or effective control over the operation. Nicaragua
said even decisive funding, training and targeting was not enough. Tadić tried a softer overall control test,
but the ICJ in the Genocide case rejected it, so effective control is the law now.

Then the six circumstances precluding wrongfulness: consent, self-defence under article 51 of the Charter,
proportionate countermeasures — the Danube diversion in Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros failed that — force majeure, which
needs absolute and material impossibility, distress, and necessity, which is exceptional and failed in the Wall
opinion. None of them excuses breaching a peremptory norm, under article 26.

If the act is wrongful, the state must cease it and, where needed, guarantee non-repetition, and it must make full
reparation — wiping out the consequences, as Chorzów says — through restitution, compensation for material loss,
and satisfaction for moral damage. Finally, the injured state invokes responsibility, and under article 48 other
states can too, for collective-interest obligations and for obligations erga omnes like the prohibition of
genocide.
