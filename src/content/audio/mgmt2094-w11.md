This week is about innovation models: the different ways of seeing innovation. Start with types. Innovation isn't only radical breakthroughs; minor, incremental advances count too, and they're far more common, like the Braille Rubik's cube. The slides list seven types: product; process, like online recruiting; organisational, like a new venture division; management, which is TQM; production, which is quality circles and just in time; commercial or marketing, like guerrilla campaigns; and service, like internet-based financial services.

Next, views of where innovation comes from. The social deterministic school says external social factors drive it: when conditions are right, it happens, the way Covid pushed offerings online. The individualistic school says innovators are born, and it links to serendipity. Resource-based innovation grows from the firm's own resources, like Starbucks and its coffee drinks; market-based innovation comes from market conditions the firm has to recognise, like Share a Coke.

Then the models. Serendipity is unexpected discovery, but chance favours the prepared mind. The linear model runs from science and technology to the needs of the market, through four gates: project charter, business plan, launch and proposal, sanity check, then commercialisation. Simultaneous coupling joins everything at once, so we can't say where innovation began. In the closed model, implementation waits until the concept is frozen; in the open model it starts midway, so the market's response reshapes the concept. And architectural innovation changes a product's structure, phone to smartphone, while modular innovation changes one component, like a car's steam engine becoming a fuel engine.

Yellow Tail shows the four actions at work in a crowded US wine market. Eliminate terminology, ageing and above-the-line marketing. Reduce complexity, range and vineyard prestige. Raise price above budget wine, and retail involvement. Create easy drinking, ease of selection, and fun and adventure. That's value innovation: superior buyer value for the mass market, found by following non-customers rather than beating rivals, and technology only counts when mass buyers can afford it.

Finally, BCG's six models: creator, solution builder, leverager, expander, defender and fast follower. Choosing is about context. Sears defended in an industry that online players upended, while Amazon and Costco both win in retail with different models. Our guest founder, Mark, put it simply: keep your eyes open for waves, stay flexible, and stick to your knitting.
