This week is the law of treaties, and it all runs through the Vienna Convention on the Law of
Treaties of 1969 — the treaty on treaties. It only applies to treaties between states, and even states that
have not joined it are bound by the provisions that reflect customary international law.

The definition is in art 2(1)(a): an international agreement concluded between states, in written
form and governed by international law, in one instrument or several, whatever it is called. A press communiqué in Aegean Sea, the minutes of a meeting in Qatar v Bahrain, and a memorandum
of understanding in Somalia v Kenya were all capable of being treaties — even though Australia itself treats
its MoUs as less than a treaty.

Then unilateral statements. These are non-treaty acts, but a state can still bind itself by a clear, public
declaration of intention that does not depend on anything in return, as in Eastern Greenland and the Nuclear
Tests cases. No reciprocity, no writing needed.

Who can bind a state? Each state decides under its own law. The UK Crown concludes treaties but must lay them
before Parliament; the US needs two-thirds of the Senate for a treaty, yet the President can use executive
agreements, as with the Paris Agreement. In Australia, s 61 of the
Constitution gives the power to the executive. Under art 7, heads of state, heads of government and foreign
ministers need no full powers; everyone else does. Australia's process runs from a negotiating mandate,
through ExCo approval and signature, to JSCOT scrutiny with a National Interest Analysis, as with AUKUS.

Consent to be bound is not entry into force. We focus on signature and ratification, with art 14 telling us
when ratification is needed, and art 24 leaving entry into force to the treaty itself — UNCLOS took twelve
years. In between, art 18 says do not defeat the object and purpose, while art 25 lets parties apply a
treaty provisionally.

Invalidity comes in two kinds: relative, for error, fraud and corruption; absolute, for coercion, force and
conflict with jus cogens under art 53. And art 46 means you almost never escape a treaty because of your own
internal law — Cameroon v Nigeria.

Reservations, defined in art 2(1)(d), let a state join a multilateral treaty without every provision, unless
art 19 rules them out, especially for incompatibility with object and purpose. Art 20 tells us what
acceptance and objection do, and an impermissible reservation is void, with three competing solutions.

Interpretation is sequential: art 31 first — good faith, ordinary meaning, context, object and
purpose — and art 32's preparatory work only when needed, as in the South China Sea Arbitration.
