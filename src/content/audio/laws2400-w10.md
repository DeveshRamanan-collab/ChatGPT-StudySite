Causation week, and it starts with damage. Damage, not damages, is the gist of negligence: no damage, no action. The plaintiff must prove, on the balance of probabilities, that the breach caused the damage, under section 5E. Causation is a factual question; remoteness, or scope of liability, is a legal question about policy, and that is next week.

Factual causation first. At common law it is the but for test: would the plaintiff have suffered the damage but for the negligence? In Barnett, the doctor was negligent, but timely treatment would not have saved the patient, so no causation. Under section 5D(1)(a) the question is whether the negligence was a necessary condition of the harm. Same test, different words; Strong v Woolworths calls it a statutory statement of but for. Use both labels and cite the section.

How did we get here? In March v Stramare, Mason CJ said but for was never the exclusive test and causation is decided by common sense. McHugh J wanted but for to be exclusive, with policy in remoteness. The Ipp Report said common sense hides policy choices. So section 5D(1) splits causation in two, and Adeels Palace says factual causation and scope of liability are separate and distinct. Security might have stopped the gunman; might have is not proof. In Strong, the chip was more probably dropped in the long morning period than in the last twenty minutes, so causation was proved.

Sometimes but for is inconclusive. Multiple sufficient causes, where each event alone was enough: Cook v Lewis held both hunters liable, and market share liability spreads the loss. Loss of a chance: in Tabet v Gett a less than even chance of a better outcome was a possibility, not a probability, and not recoverable. Dust disease: increased risk is not enough, Amaca v Ellis; material contribution, more than negligible, is enough, Bonnington Castings and Amaca v Booth. Fairchild's risk test comes from the UK and is not our law.

Section 5D(2) is the exceptional case provision the Ipp Report recommended. It is rarely used; Adeels Palace said it did not apply; and section 3B(1) keeps dust and tobacco claims out of the Act altogether. The flowchart adds a clear breach and an evidentiary gap.

Scope of liability is the normative question in section 5D(1)(b), with section 5D(4) asking whether and why responsibility should be imposed. A novus actus can break the chain: McKew's unreasonable trip down the stairs did; the drunk driver in March did not, because the truck created the very risk.

Last, contribution. A plaintiff can recover everything from one jointly and severally liable tortfeasor, who can then claim contribution from the others. It never affects the plaintiff.
