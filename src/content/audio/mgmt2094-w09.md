This week is one long real-world example, told by a chemical engineer who lived it: how Dow Corning fixed the way it
commercialised new products.

Start with the problem. Dow Corning was a heavy R&D investor, ten per cent of revenue, with world-class analytical labs.
Yet its key measure, New Product Market Share, the share of revenue coming from newly commercialised products, fell
from about twenty-five per cent in the seventies and eighties to thirteen per cent by 1995, and a new product took about
four years to reach the market. So the lesson is that R&D spending is not innovation. The bottleneck was
commercialisation.

Then the false start. Under the pressure of the breast implant litigation, Dow Corning stopped building its own
software, chose SAP, and launched a transformation to become world class, customer focused and process oriented.
Commercialisation was one of three areas to re-engineer. In 1997 consultants redesigned the process, and it failed,
because poor change management meant nobody took it up. In 1999 the company switched to Lean Six Sigma, and in 2001 to
Design for Six Sigma.

Keep the Six Sigma triangle straight. DMAIC improves existing processes. Design for Six Sigma creates new processes,
products, services or plants. Process Management sustains the gains. Leaders sit in the middle and drive it. The design
process Dow Corning used was DMEDI: Define, Measure, Explore, Develop, Implement. Measure captures the voice of the
customer as critical-to-customer requirements; Explore generates concepts and picks the best fit; Develop uses tools
like design of experiments; Implement pilots and scales up.

The pilot was the ArmorAll Deck Protector, a silicone modified organic product to take on Thompson's WaterSeal within
eighteen months. The team ran a six-factor, two-level design of experiment on silane, wax and surfactant, compiled the
results with the economics, and did the process engineering at the same time, so at one meeting ArmorAll only had to
choose. Result: over four and a half thousand tonnes and forty million US dollars in twelve months, two awards, and the
model for every later project.

And then it stopped. ArmorAll's TV ads were challenged by Thompson's, ArmorAll lost in court, and Clorox took it over.
No matter how good your process, poor risk management by your customer can sink the project. That is the line between
creativity, making the product, and innovation, getting it to market.

Last, the human side. Six Sigma is discipline, but you can be disciplined and still be creative: protect time to play,
bring in people who think differently, and reuse solutions that already exist.
