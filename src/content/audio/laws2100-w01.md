Week one asks two questions that sound alike but are not: what is the nature of jurisprudence, and what is the
nature of law? The first is about the theory; the second is about its object. Keep them apart and most essays stay
on topic.

The word jurisprudence has several meanings: a body of judicial decisions, religious law, medical jurisprudence. For
us it means legal theory, a general theory of law. Bix uses jurisprudence, legal theory and legal philosophy
interchangeably, though Cotterrell thinks they should be distinguished. General jurisprudence deals with puzzles
thrown up by all legal systems, like what is law, or is there a general obligation to obey it. Particular
jurisprudence deals with puzzles of some systems only, like in what sense Parliament is sovereign.

Analytical jurisprudence relies on conceptual analysis, which defines terms by necessary and sufficient conditions.
Bix says drawing those boundaries builds common ground, but where they go is often contested. Twining keeps the
method but wants a much broader conception than Hart's or Raz's.

Then the six kinds of questions: doctrinal, normative, empirical, metatheoretical, methodological and conceptual.
Normative claims are justificatory or critical; empirical claims are explanatory or about effects; metatheory asks
for a theory's success criteria.

Theory is not idle. In Milirrpum, the Gove land rights case, Aboriginal claims were questioned because customary
rules could not be described as legal rules, an assumption from Austin. Madzimbamuto used Kelsen to consider
Rhodesia's declaration of independence, and Miller used Hart's rule of recognition.

Hart's line is that in law, as elsewhere, we can know and yet not understand. Most people can cite examples of law,
yet the debate never ends, because beside the paradigm cases, modern state legal systems, sit borderline cases such
as primitive and international law. Hart's three persistent questions are law and orders backed by threats, legal and
moral obligation, and rules. Lawyers are like someone who can cross a familiar town but cannot give directions; they
need a map.

A definition per genus et differentiam, general kind plus distinguishing features, works for triangles and
elephants, but Hart says it does not work for law. Separate the claim that it is impossible from the claim that
better definitions exist.

Finally, Hart claims a theory that is general, tied to no legal system, and descriptive, morally neutral with no
justificatory aims. Those are criteria to test him on his own terms. Finnis says law cannot be assessed without
premises about true human goods, and Dickson notes that elucidating law means choosing its most important features.
Read with curiosity, charity and clarity.
