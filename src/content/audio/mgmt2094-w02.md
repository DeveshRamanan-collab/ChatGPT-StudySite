Week two is about exploring the creativity problem, and it starts with a model. Creative problem solving has
three steps. First, identify an interesting problem: find problems, identify needs. Second, divergent thinking: find
solutions. Third, convergent thinking: find the solution, or at least a good one. The first two make up the
prospective phase; convergent thinking is the analytical phase. And it loops — great advances are usually a long
process, a sum of intuitions and successive progresses.

The first step is the one creative people are known for: identifying meaningful problems, asking the right questions.
The slide gives Einstein's line — with one hour to save the world, fifty-nine minutes defining the problem — and
Picasso's: computers are useless, they can only give answers. Two qualities help: listen to the unusual, and identify
the consequences that make the subject essential. Three questions to remember. Why is the speed of light the same
everywhere, which led to relativity. How to use colours differently, which led to impressionism. And how to make bus
stop shelters viable, which led to the worldwide success of Decaux.

For divergent thinking, the example is the quota method. Always ask at least three ideas from your team, and from
yourself — or refuse to decide. Once three good ideas are found, many more usually follow. It breaks linear thinking:
settling for the first idea, and logic that locks you into the first solution. Convergent thinking is the opposite
move: weigh benefits and risks, keep the best, eliminate wrong answers.

Why can't we test creativity? Because it's a process, it's often a group phenomenon, and it implies domain knowledge.
IQ tests and emotional intelligence reach convergent thinking; divergent thinking tests give mixed results; and there
is no standard test at all for formulating interesting problems.

Then the techniques, in four families. Rational, or serious creativity: TRIZ from Altshuller, lateral thinking and
the six hats from de Bono, and mind maps from Buzan. Gaming: frame games, improvisation, Hohmann's innovation games,
constructions with LEGOs. Idea gathering: brainstorming — as many ideas as possible, no censorship, sort them
afterwards — plus idea boxes, from the rewarded box of 1721 to quality circles, idea software and crowdsourcing. And
irrational: Synectics, making the familiar strange with direct, symbolic and fantasy analogies, and detour
techniques that step away from the problem before coming back to converge.

Finally, mind maps. A central image, connected curved branches, at least three colours, one key word per line,
lines thick at the centre and thinner outward. We think in radial associations — remember the windows. And the Part A
mind map is due in Week 4.
