This week is legal realism, and then critical legal studies. Realism is an umbrella term, with an American family, Holmes, Pound, Oliphant, Frank, Llewellyn and Cohen, and a Scandinavian family, with Alf Ross the most influential. The target was formalism: law as a self-contained system of rules, judges applying rules to facts by syllogism, mechanical jurisprudence, reasoning sealed off from morals, politics and economics. Holmes answered that the life of the law has not been logic; it has been experience.

In The Path of the Law Holmes tells us to look at law through the eyes of the bad man, who does not care two straws for axioms or deductions and wants to know what the courts will do in fact. The prophecies of what the courts will do, and nothing more pretentious, are what he means by the law. That is the prediction theory.

Next, the distinction that does the most work. Conceptual rule-scepticism is about the nature of law: we have a legal duty not to do something if and only if the courts say so. Empirical rule-scepticism is about adjudication: judges respond to the facts, not to the rules, because there is always support for both sides. You can hold the second without the first.

Hart replies in chapter seven. Realism ignores the internal point of view, cannot say a court made a mistake, prefers the bad man to the puzzled man, and cannot tell us who a judge is. His own account is open texture. A motor-car is plainly a vehicle; roller skates are not clearly one. Uncertainty at the borderline is the price of general classifying terms, and we labour under ignorance of fact and of aim. Formalism and rule-scepticism are the Scylla and Charybdis of juristic theory; the truth lies between them. In the same spirit Hart calls realism the Nightmare, judges always make law, and Dworkin the noblest dreamer, judges never make law.

Leiter pushes back on Hart. Realism is not a rival theory of law but a descriptive theory of adjudication, resting on empirical rule-scepticism, which Hart never refutes. Realists even presuppose a positivist account of what counts as law. What is left is an empirical question: how often do rules in books cause decisions?

Finally, critical legal studies takes realism further, adding postmodernism and Marxism. Law is radically indeterminate, contradictory because it comes from political conflict, and an ideology that serves the powerful while presenting itself as neutral. For these critics the line between law as it is and law as it ought to be makes no sense, and that is the point to test in the essay.
