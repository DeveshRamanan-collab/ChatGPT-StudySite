This week has two halves. First, how international law and national law relate. From international law's side it is simple: a state cannot use its own law as an excuse. Article 27 of the Vienna Convention says a party may not invoke its internal law to justify failing to perform a treaty, and article 46 only lets a state escape its consent if the breach of its internal rules on treaty-making was manifest and concerned a rule of fundamental importance. Article 3 of the ILC Draft Articles on State Responsibility widens that to all obligations: whether an act is wrongful is decided by international law, not by domestic law. Still, national law matters to international law, as evidence of state practice and opinio juris, as subsidiary sources, and as the way obligations are fulfilled.

From the domestic side we have two theories. Monism sees one legal order, with international law applying directly, as in article 25 of the German Basic Law. Dualism sees two separate orders, so international law must be transformed first. Most systems blend them, like the Netherlands, and Australia is primarily dualist.

In Australia, section 51(xxix), the external affairs power, lets the Commonwealth legislate to implement treaties, even on state matters, as in the Tasmanian Dam Case, though not as a mere device. But treaties have no direct effect: Dietrich tells us ratification of the ICCPR created no domestic rights. Treaties still help interpretation: incorporated text takes its treaty meaning, section 15AB lets a treaty confirm or clarify an Act if Australia was in it or contemplating it before enactment, ambiguous statutes are read consistently with international law, and clear words always win. For custom, transformation and incorporation are both recognised, but Nulyarimma held genocide could not be tried without legislation. On the Constitution, Kirby J was open to international law and McHugh J called it heretical.

The second half is jurisdiction. There are three forms: to prescribe, to enforce and to adjudicate. Lotus gives states wide discretion to prescribe, but a state may never enforce in another state's territory without permission, which is why seizing Eichmann in Argentina was unlawful. Lockerbie shows adjudication abroad by agreement, with a Scottish court sitting in the Netherlands.

Then the grounds. Territoriality is fundamental, objective where the offence is completed and subjective where it is planned. Active nationality follows the offender, passive personality follows the victim, as in Yunis, but it is controversial. Protection covers threats to vital interests, like counterfeiting. Universality covers piracy, genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity and torture, usually with the accused present. When claims overlap there is no binding rule, so the closest link usually wins.
