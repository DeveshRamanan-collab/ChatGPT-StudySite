This week tests one claim: that the key to understanding law is the simple notion of an order backed by
threats, which Austin called a command.

Start with Austin. Law in general is a rule laid down for an intelligent being by an intelligent being having power
over him. There are four kinds: divine law, human law, positive morality such as international law, and laws only by
metaphor, like the laws of motion. Jurisprudence is about positive law, set by political superiors to political
inferiors. A command is a wish, an evil threatened for non-compliance, and the expression of the wish. Command, duty
and sanction are inseparable, and superiority just means might.

Which commands are positive laws? Those of a sovereign. A society is political when the bulk of its members habitually
obey a determinate common superior, and independent when that superior obeys no one else. No territory is needed.
Austin also gives us two positivist ideas: the existence of law is one thing, its merit another; and principles
abstracted from every particular system, though illustrated from Roman and English law.

Now Hart. The gunman orders the clerk to hand over the money. The clerk is obliged, but has no obligation. Hart
avoids the word command, because commanding is exercising authority, and talks of coercive orders. He adds four
elements: generality, enduring character, a general habit of obedience, and supremacy and independence. That gives the
gunman situation writ large, built in Austin's strongest form. That is charitable interpretation.

Then he attacks it. Chapter three: the content of laws, since power-conferring rules like wills and contracts are not
orders backed by threats; their range, since legislators are bound by their own laws; and their origin, since custom
is not issued by a sovereign. Chapter four goes deeper. Sovereignty rests on the habit of obedience, which cannot
explain the continuity and persistence of law. Habits and rules both show convergent behaviour, but only rules attract
criticism, supply the reason for it, and have an internal aspect. Habits cannot confer rights or authority. We need
the idea of a rule.

Finally, is coercion necessary? Lamond lists five views, from Austin's coercion as genus to Dworkin's law as
constraining state coercion, and says coercion is at best the most salient aspect of certain legal systems. Raz's
society of angels suggests law without coercion is conceivable. Schauer replies that law as we experience it is
overwhelmingly coercive, and that what theory should focus on depends on what a theory of law is supposed to
accomplish.
