International disputes are about facts, the application of law and jurisdiction, and the whole point of peaceful
settlement is to resolve them before they escalate. First question: is there even a dispute? Mavrommatis gives the
definition — a disagreement on a point of law or fact, a conflict of legal views or interests. Whether one exists is
decided objectively, so a respondent's denial proves nothing, as the Peace Treaties opinion says. South West Africa
gives the test: one side's claim is positively opposed by the other, and the respondent knew or could not have been
unaware of it. That means more than friction, some communication between the parties, and a low threshold, and
Georgia v Russia tells us to examine the facts — statements and diplomatic notes. It matters because without a
dispute when proceedings start, the court has no jurisdiction.

Article 2(3) of the Charter obliges states to settle disputes peacefully, and article 2(4) bars force. The
obligation is customary too, according to Nicaragua, and it is one of conduct, not result: states must keep trying,
but they never have to settle, which is why Canada and Denmark could argue over Hans Island for fifty years.

Article 33(1) lists the means, and the list is open-ended. The diplomatic ones don't bind: negotiation, which keeps
control with the parties and needs good faith; good offices, where a neutral third party helps the parties start
talking; mediation, where the third party proposes a framework, like Holbrooke in Bosnia; inquiry, for disputed
facts; and conciliation, a report with recommendations, like the Timor Sea commission. The adjudicatory ones do
bind. Arbitration rests on consent, by treaty or by compromis, and the states choose the arbitrators, the rules and
confidentiality; awards are final and binding.

At the ICJ, fifteen judges, only states as parties, and no compulsory jurisdiction. Consent comes four ways: a
compromis, a treaty clause like article IX of the Genocide Convention, optional clause declarations under article
36(2), or forum prorogatum, and the Court decides its own jurisdiction. Provisional measures under article 41 need
jurisdiction that is not manifestly absent, plausible rights, a link, and a real and imminent risk of irreparable
harm, and LaGrand says they bind. Judgments are final, without appeal and binding only on the parties, with article
94 sending non-compliance to the Security Council. Advisory opinions, like Chagos, carry weight but don't bind.
Finally, article 52 puts regional bodies first for local disputes, and the WTO settles trade disputes under Annex 2.
