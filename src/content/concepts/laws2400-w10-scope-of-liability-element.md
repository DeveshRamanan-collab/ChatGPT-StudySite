Causation is not finished when factual causation is satisfied. Section 5D(1)(b) asks whether it is appropriate for the scope of the negligent person's liability to extend to the harm so caused — a **normative** question. Section 5D(4): the court is to consider (amongst other relevant things) whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed on the negligent party. The test comes from the common law (remoteness), covered in Topic 11.

The flowchart gives scope of liability two parts joined by AND: **new intervening acts** and **remoteness**.

**Intervening acts** (introduced this week). A *novus actus interveniens* is a later event that exacerbates the original harm or causes new harm — the plaintiff's own act, a third party's, or a natural event. If the chain is broken, the original wrongdoer is not liable for harm after the break. Not every later careless act severs it: in *March v Stramare* the truck created the very risk that materialised, so the driver's own negligence did not break the chain. In *McKew v Holland Hannen & Cubitts Ltd* the plaintiff, whose injured leg was prone to give way, went down a steep staircase without a handrail, unaided; his unreasonable conduct was a novus actus, and the defendant was not liable for the second injury.

Section 5D(4) asks the same question as s 5D(2), but at a different stage.

Exam use: after factual causation, deal with scope expressly — any intervening act, then remoteness — and frame the conclusion with s 5D(4).
