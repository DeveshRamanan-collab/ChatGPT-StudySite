An occupier of land owes entrants a duty to take reasonable care against foreseeable risks of injury, but
*Romeo v Conservation Commission (NT)* shows the duty is "not owed in the abstract in practice" — the
critical question is usually its scope, not its existence. Romeo fell from an unfenced cliff in a reserve
the Commission managed; the High Court held the duty did not extend to fencing the coastline, because the
danger was assessed "according to its obviousness and the care ordinarily exercised by the public". There
is no duty to warn of, or guard against, an obvious risk — only a concealed one.

The CLA now codifies this. Section 5F defines an "obvious risk" objectively — obvious to a reasonable
person in the plaintiff's position, even if it has a low probability of occurring or is not conspicuous.
Section 5G presumes the plaintiff was aware of an obvious risk unless they prove otherwise on the balance
of probabilities. Section 5H removes any proactive duty to warn of an obvious risk, subject to three
exceptions: the plaintiff asked for advice, a written law requires a warning, or the defendant is a
professional and the risk is one of death or personal injury from a professional service.

Exam use: once the defendant is an occupier (or otherwise controls premises), run the obvious-risk analysis
before breach — if the danger was obvious, there is usually no duty to warn or eliminate it, so breach
never needs to be reached.
