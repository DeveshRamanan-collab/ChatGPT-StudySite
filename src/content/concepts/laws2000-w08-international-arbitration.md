Arbitration is the first of the adjudicatory modes and, like all of them, is founded upon the principle of consent.
Consent can be given:

- by becoming party to a treaty (bilateral or multilateral) that provides for arbitration of future disputes — e.g.
  UNCLOS; or
- through a special agreement (*compromis*) by which the parties submit a particular dispute to arbitration after it
  has arisen.

States maintain close control over the whole process:

1. **Composition** — the number and identity of the arbitrators (often three: one chosen by each party, the third
   agreed between them);
2. **Rules to be applied** — e.g. a specific treaty or national law;
3. **Confidentiality** — they may choose to keep proceedings confidential.

Procedural rules are flexible, so arbitration can be faster. Arbitral tribunals are ad hoc (temporary), unlike a
standing court. And unlike the diplomatic modes, the final decisions — arbitral awards — are final and binding upon
the parties. The lecturer's example: the South China Sea arbitration, brought by the Philippines against China under
UNCLOS.

Exam use: when comparing arbitration with the ICJ, line them up on consent, who chooses the judges, which rules
apply, confidentiality and permanence — and note that both bind the parties.
