Who can bind a state internationally is not settled by international law — you look at each state's own
domestic law. In the **UK**, the Crown (government, under the royal "foreign relations" prerogative)
concludes treaties, but s 20 *Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010* (UK) requires a treaty to be
laid before Parliament before ratification. In the **USA**, a "treaty" needs two-thirds Senate consent, but
the President can make "executive agreements" binding without the Senate — shown by the Paris Agreement,
joined and left by successive Presidents (Obama 2016, Trump 2017, Biden 2021, Trump 2025) purely by
executive order. In **Australia**, art 61 of the Constitution gives the executive the power to conclude
treaties, exercised by the Foreign Minister, DFAT, and the Attorney-General's Office of International Law.

Article 7 VCLT solves the practical problem of knowing who, at the negotiating table, actually has
authority: a person who produces "full powers" during negotiation is taken to represent the state. Three
officials are presumed to already have that authority and need not produce anything: a head of state, a
head of government, and a minister for foreign affairs. Everyone else should produce a letter of full
powers.

Exam use: identify the state's constitutional arrangement first (Australia = executive/DFAT; US = President
can bind without the Senate via executive agreement; UK = Crown, but Parliament must see it before
ratification), then check whether the individual negotiator is one of the three art 7 exempt officials or
needed full powers.
