Content: the literature identifies generations of rights that build upon each other.

- **First generation**: civil and political rights, eg the right to life, equality before the law, freedom of speech and of religion, property rights, a fair trial and voting rights.
- **Second generation**: social, economic and cultural rights, eg to be employed (under just conditions), to food, to housing and to health care.
- **Third generation**: group and collective ("solidarity") rights, eg self-determination, economic and social development, a healthy environment, natural resources, and intergenerational equity and sustainability.

The lecture: the third generation is more problematic in its authority, justification and implementation; it is much harder to move from recognising a right to a healthy environment to implementing it.

History: Magna Carta (1215); English Bill of Rights (1689); US Constitution (1789) and Bill of Rights (1791); French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789); UDHR (1948); ICCPR and ICESCR (1966); CEDAW (1979); Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989); UK Human Rights Act 1998.

The earlier documents were national; the UDHR puts universality at its centre, and international recognition followed through covenants and treaties. History shows how our understanding of rights developed, and it raises the foundational question: have human rights always existed?

Exam use: date a right's legal recognition separately from the moral claim behind it.
