Two boxes on the *volenti* flowchart show legislation reshaping the common law defence in opposite
directions.

**Intoxicated drivers and passengers — narrowed.** The courts are generally reluctant to allow the *volenti*
defence against passengers who rely on an intoxicated driver. Legislative intervention in some Australian
jurisdictions specifically excludes the *volenti* defence in these circumstances — see, eg, Motor Accidents Act 1988
(NSW) s 76 (its text is not in the materials).

**Legislative reform — expanded.** Some jurisdictions have enacted legislation which expands the voluntary
assumption of risk defence available to defendants — see, eg, CLA s 5L. Under s 5L(1), a defendant is not liable in
negligence for harm suffered by the plaintiff as a result of the materialisation of an obvious risk of a dangerous
recreational activity engaged in by the plaintiff; s 5L(2) says this applies whether or not the plaintiff was aware
of the risk. For guidance on applying these provisions, the flowchart cites *Tapp v Australian Bushmen's Campdraft &
Rodeo Association Ltd* (2022) 273 CLR 454.

The contrast is the point: common law *volenti* requires the plaintiff to have perceived, fully appreciated and
voluntarily accepted the specific risk; s 5L does not depend on the plaintiff's awareness at all.

Exam use: for a recreational injury, consider s 5L alongside common law *volenti*; for a passenger hurt by a drunk
driver, note the courts' reluctance and the statutory exclusion before relying on *volenti*.
