For Hart, legal theory has either ignored or exaggerated the indeterminacies of legal rules. Systems compromise between two social needs: rules individuals can safely apply to themselves without fresh official guidance, and leaving open issues that can only be settled "when they arise in a concrete case" (CL 130). "Formalism and rule-scepticism are the Scylla and Charybdis of juristic theory"; the truth lies between them (CL 147).

In the Postscript, the "sharpest direct conflict" with Dworkin is Hart's contention that there will always be "legally unregulated cases" in which the law is "partly indeterminate or incomplete" (CL 272). For Dworkin law includes explicit settled law and implicit legal principles, so the judge never steps outside the law, making new law.

For Hart, in hard cases judges exercise law-making powers, but unlike a legislator's: constrained (no large-scale reforms or new codes), **interstitial**, and not arbitrary: judges must justify decisions by general reasons and act as a conscientious legislator.

Dworkin's three criticisms and Hart's replies:

1. A false description of what judges do in hard cases → judges "do not just push away their law books"; even Dworkin's theory incorporates choices.
2. Undemocratic, as judges are not elected → a necessary, small price if judicial power is limited; delegated legislative power is familiar in modern democracies.
3. Unjust, as retrospective legislation → in hard cases, by definition, there is no clear established law to justify expectations.

Exam use: the first criticism is descriptive, the others normative; answer each on its level.
