A case is "novel" when it falls outside an established category of relationship — reasonable foreseeability
alone is not enough, and courts take an incremental approach, weighing the "salient features" of the
relationship between plaintiff and defendant (*Caltex Refineries v Stavar*).

The salient features are non-exhaustive and need not all be satisfied. They include: foreseeability of harm;
the defendant's degree and nature of control over the risk; the plaintiff's vulnerability and ability to
self-protect; the plaintiff's reliance on the defendant; any assumption of responsibility by the defendant;
proximity between the parties; the category of relationship; the nature and danger of the defendant's
activity; the defendant's actual or constructive knowledge of the risk of harm; the risk of indeterminate
liability; and coherence with statute and the common law.

In *Caltex Refineries v Stavar*, an employer's duty to an employee's wife — exposed to asbestos while
washing his contaminated work clothes — was novel because it ran beyond the established employer/employee
relationship; weighing the salient features, the Court of Appeal found a duty was owed. In *Sullivan v
Moody*, weighing coherence with statutory child-protection duties and the risk of indeterminate liability
instead, the High Court found no duty was owed to a father under investigation for suspected abuse.

Exam use: name the case as novel, then run through the salient factors the facts actually raise — do not
simply list all of them.
