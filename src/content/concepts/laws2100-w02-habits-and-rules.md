Chapter IV makes a more fundamental criticism: "the whole conception of a supreme and independent sovereign
habitually obeyed, on which the model rests, is misleading, since there is little in any actual legal system which
corresponds to it" (CL 27). Austin's idea of sovereignty conceptually depends on a habit of obedience, so Hart
attacks that pillar.

Chapter IV's outline: (1) can the idea of a habit of obedience explain two salient features of most legal systems,
the continuity of law and the persistence of law? (2) the legally unlimited power enjoyed by the sovereign; (3) the
position occupied by the sovereign above the law.

Habits vs rules: in both we observe convergence of behaviour — most people go to the cinema at the weekend; men remove
their hats in church. But there are three salient differences: deviations from a rule are met with criticisms; the
rule is regarded as a good reason for making such criticism; and social rules have an internal aspect. For Hart,
"habits are not 'normative': they cannot confer rights or authority on anyone" (CL 60).

Hart's conclusion: replace the habit of obedience with the idea of a rule. Without it, a theory like Austin's cannot
explain legal obligation, the normative force of law. Law is a matter of authority, not only power: legal obligation
is more than coercion, but different from moral obligation.

Exam use: habits versus rules is the hinge between Austin's theory and Hart's own.
