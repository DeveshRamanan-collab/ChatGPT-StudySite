Two other liberal approaches to the moral limits of the law.

**John Rawls (1921–2002)** distinguishes the **right** from the **good**:

- the right is the sphere of principles of justice, including the protection of rights;
- the good is the sphere of value.

The state should be "neutral" on all matters of the good life. The only justification for state interference is to prevent rights violations and to punish those who do violate the rights of others.

**Joseph Raz (1939–2022)** rejects Rawls's idea of state neutrality. The state is entitled to (and should) promote intrinsically worthy ways of life and discourage intrinsically unworthy ways of life. Raz is a **liberal perfectionist**:

- perfectionist, because the state has an active role in helping people live a good life;
- liberal, because the state should **not** use the criminal law to promote worthy ways of life or pursue perfectionist ideals.

Raz has no problem with the state promoting virtue by **non-coercive means**, for instance by taxing activities of which the majority disapproves.

Compare the legal moralists: Devlin and George would use the criminal law against vice; Raz shares their concern with the good but not their means; Rawls shares neither.

Exam use: Raz lets you argue that the state may care about the good life without criminalising harmless vice.
