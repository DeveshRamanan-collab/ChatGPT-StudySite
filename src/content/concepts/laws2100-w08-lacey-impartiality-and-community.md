The terms 'rational', 'impartial' and 'objective' recur in legal discourse and legal theory, often defined in terms of their opposites. Law appears committed to reasoning that favours objective, impartial and disinterested thought over emotion or personal opinion. The justification: everyone will be treated equally before the law, regardless of race, sex or religion.

Nicola Lacey (*Unspeakable Subjects*, 1998; LSE) frames the feminist challenge to conventional legal scholarship: the neutral framework of legal reasoning; law's autonomy and discreteness; its neutrality and objectivity; its centrality; law as a system of enacted rules or norms; its unity and coherence; its rationality.

In chapter 5, 'Community in Legal Theory: Idea, Ideal or Ideology?', community is "one particular discursive figure in contemporary Anglo-American legal theory" (p 128). Lacey (1) explores the liberal–communitarian debate's relevance for law; (2) assesses the rhetorical function of appeals to "community"; (3) draws implications for CLS, socio-legal studies, postmodernism and critical theory.

Liberals: autonomous agents, individual rights, the primacy of justice, universal norms. Communitarians: socially embedded selves shaped by communities, traditions and social roles; identities formed through shared practices and narratives.

The lecture: every theory studied assumes a community. Austin identifies the sovereign within an independent political society; Hart's system has officials and citizens, with the modern state as central case; Finnis's central case promotes the common good; Dworkin's integrity rests on a community's political morality. Law is the law of a community, for a community.

Exam use: ask whose community a theory of law presupposes.
