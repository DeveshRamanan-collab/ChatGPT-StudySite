In 'The Path of the Law' (1897), often seen as foundational to American legal realism, Holmes urges lawyers to view law
through the eyes of the **bad man**, who cares only about the practical consequences of breaking rules.

"The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law." (10(8)
*Harvard Law Review* 460–461)

This is the **prediction theory**: law is a prediction of what courts will do in fact. On the realist view we are under
a legal duty not to do X because doing X is likely to result in an adverse court ruling. Unlike Austin, the focus is not
commands and sanctions but the behaviour of officials.

Ross, the leading Scandinavian realist, reached realism through a criticism of Kelsen. In "Tû-Tû" (1957) ownership has
no semantic reference: it is a placeholder linking factual statements to legal consequences.

Counterarguments (slide 10):

- it misses the **internal point of view** some participants take;
- it makes it impossible to say a court has **made a mistake** about the law;
- why prefer the bad man to the **puzzled person** who wishes to arrange their affairs according to law?
- if there are no legal rules, **how do we know who a judge is**?

Exam use: the puzzled man is Hart's answer to the bad man, and the finality/infallibility point is his sharpest
objection to the prediction theory.
