Four theories trace creativity to a departure from the "normal" state. The lecture framed normality as
stability and security; to be creative you have to move away from it in one way or another.

- **Psychoanalytical theory** (Jung; Freud, Adler and Hammer): people become more creative as a reaction to difficult
  circumstances and/or repressed emotions. After a traumatic event people pull back and rely on their creative side as
  an outlet; a sense of inferiority can contribute, and creativity can make them feel superior and move forward.
  Freud: "unsatisfied wishes are the driving power behind fantasies".
- **Mental illness theory** (Briggs; Eisemann; Goodman): some form of mental illness needs to be present — not severe,
  as that can impede. Associated disorders: bipolar, manic-depressive, severe mood swings. Verhaeghen and colleagues: a
  reflective mode makes people more creative but can lead to depression. Stress, by contrast, impairs creativity by
  distracting us.
- **Addiction theory** (Lapp, Collins, Norlander and Wallas): addiction to substances contributes to creativity,
  perhaps by removing the stress that inhibits it. Counter-argument: addiction doesn't cause creativity; creative
  people are more prone to addiction.
- **Psychoticism** (Eysenck): creative people have a disposition for psychotic tendencies — halfway between "normal"
  and psychosis: rejecting social, cultural or authoritarian norms; risk taking, impulsive, spontaneous.

Exam use: name the theory from its trigger (hardship, mild illness, substances, an anti-convention personality) and
state its weakness.
