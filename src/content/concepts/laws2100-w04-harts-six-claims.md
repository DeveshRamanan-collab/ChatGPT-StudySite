Hart breaks the "necessary connection between law and morality" into six claims (CL ch IX): (1) power and
authority; (2) influence of morality; (3) interpretation; (4) criticism of law; (5) principles of legality and
justice; (6) validity and resistance. Of (2) and (5) Hart says: if this is what the
necessary connection means, its existence should be conceded.

**Power and authority.** The coercive power of law presupposes its accepted authority — the internal point of view.
But "allegiance to the system may be based on many different considerations: calculations of long-term interest;
disinterested interest in others; an unreflecting inherited or traditional attitude; or the mere wish to do as others
do" (CL 203). Acceptance need not be moral approval. A legal system exists when valid rules are generally obeyed and
officials effectively accept the secondary rules (CL 109).

**Influence of morality.** Some systems (the United States) explicitly incorporate principles of justice into the
ultimate criteria of validity; in others (England) legislation may no less scrupulously conform to morality. "No
'positivist' could deny that these are facts" (CL 204) — but the link is contingent.

**Principles of legality.** Perhaps "a minimum of justice is necessarily realized whenever human behaviour is
controlled by general rules publicly announced and judicially applied" (CL 206). Fuller called these the inner
morality of law; Hart accepts that — but it "is unfortunately compatible with very great iniquity" (CL 207).

Exam use: name the specific claim; never argue "the necessary connection" in general.
