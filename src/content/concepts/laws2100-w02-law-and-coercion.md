Lamond: "There are two sources of continuing interest in the relationship between law and coercion, one
clarificatory, the other evaluative". His list of views: coercion is the genus of which law
is a species (Austin); law is a distinctive form of social ordering because of its reliance upon coercion (Kelsen); coercion is the most salient aspect of law (Bentham, Holmes, Raz); law regulates the
use of coercion (Bobbio); the point of legal practice is to guide and constrain the coercive power of the state
(Dworkin).

Lamond argues that "the right to regulate coercion lies within the scope of law's distinctive claim to authority"
and that coercion "is not the most salient aspect of law – at best it is the most salient aspect of certain legal
systems" (p 56).

Raz's society of angels: angels comply with the rules, so there is no need for coercion, yet coordination problems
still call for law. If a legal system without coercion can be imagined, coercion is not a necessary feature of the
concept of law.

Schauer, 'Was Austin Right After All?': "The phenomenon of law as it is experienced is overwhelmingly coercive"; a
legal theory that ignores this may "fail to 'fit the facts'". The question then shifts to what legal theory is for:
"the focus of legal theory is dependent on a conception of what a legal theory is supposed to accomplish" (p 16).
Theory should capture law's salient features too.

Exam use: separate necessary from salient before arguing either way.
