Section 5B(1)(a): a person is not negligent in failing to take precautions against a risk of harm unless the
risk was foreseeable — "a risk of which the person knew or ought to have known". Actual or constructive knowledge
suffices, judged at the time of the conduct, not with hindsight.

It corresponds to the first limb of the common law test, but the inquiry differs from the duty stage: it examines the
defendant's **actual conduct** and the actual risk of injury in the circumstances — a **more specific test**.

*Wyong Shire Council v Shirt* (1980) 146 CLR 40 (a water skier misled by an ambiguous "deep water" sign) remains good
authority: a risk which is not far-fetched or fanciful is real and therefore foreseeable. Mason J: foreseeability of the
risk and the likelihood of it occurring are two different things — a risk quite unlikely to occur may nevertheless be
plainly foreseeable.

The plaintiff need not show that the precise manner of the injury, or the particular injury, was foreseeable — only a
reasonably foreseeable risk of injury of some kind to someone such as the plaintiff. The defendant must also guard
against inadvertent, negligent or even extremely foolhardy conduct by the plaintiff: *Nagle v Rottnest Island Board*
(1993) 177 CLR 423; *McLean v Tedman* (1984) 155 CR 306.

Exam use: say what the defendant knew or ought to have known, then ask whether the risk was far-fetched or fanciful —
leaving likelihood for (b) and (c).
