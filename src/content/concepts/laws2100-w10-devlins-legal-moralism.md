For legal moralism, the fact that behaviour is immoral is a legitimate reason to criminalise it, even if it causes no harm or offence.

**Devlin** (Maccabean Lecture, 1959, answering the 1957 Wolfenden report's recommendation to decriminalise homosexual behaviour between consenting adults in private) claims that if behaviour is immoral by common standard, the law-maker has a prima facie right to prohibit it. The majority may translate **popular** morality into criminal law; contra Mill, there is no principled line between private and public spheres. The criminal law already punishes harmless conduct (voluntary euthanasia threatens the sanctity of life).

His three questions:

1. Has society the right to pass judgement on moral matters?
2. If so, can it use the weapon of the law to enforce its judgements?
3. Should it do so in all cases, or only in some?

Yes to (1) and (2): without a shared morality the social order would break down. (3) is practical: adultery cannot feasibly be enforced, bigamy can.

The content of morality comes from the man in the jury box (on the Clapham omnibus): common sense and feelings of **intolerance, indignation and disgust**. There are no limits to legislation against immorality, as none against treason and sedition.

**Robert George** (*Making Men Moral*, 1993) agrees the law should prohibit vice even if harmless, but enforces **critical** (true, objective) morality, since majority beliefs may not be sound.
