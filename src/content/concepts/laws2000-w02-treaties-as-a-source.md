A treaty is "an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by
international law" (VCLT art 2(1)(a)) — an agreement between sovereign states, and in some cases
international organisations (e.g. the EU), that is binding at international law. Treaties are also called
'agreements', 'conventions', 'protocols' or 'covenants', and less commonly 'exchanges of letters': the
label doesn't matter, what counts is whether the parties consented to be bound. Treaties may be bilateral or
multilateral.

Pacta sunt servanda: states are bound to honour their treaty-based obligations. Becoming a party implies
consent to be bound by the treaty's obligations, which is why a treaty is the most direct evidence of a
legal obligation — it is easy to see whether a state accepted it by checking whether it ratified or became
a party. A state party must ensure its own domestic law and practice are consistent with what the treaty
requires; treaties are also the standard way international organisations are created (the UN Charter
creates the UN and sets out its powers; NATO the same way).

Exam use: once a treaty applies between the relevant states, it is usually the first and strongest point of
reference — check ratification or accession before reaching for custom or general principles, since a
state's consent to a specific written obligation is the clearest evidence of what it agreed to be bound by.
