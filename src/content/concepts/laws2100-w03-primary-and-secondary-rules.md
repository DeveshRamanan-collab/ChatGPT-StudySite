Imagine a community governed only by primary rules of obligation — a thought experiment. According to Hart, such a simple social structure is uncertain, has a static character, and is
inefficient. "The remedy for each of these three main defects … consists in supplementing the primary rules of
obligation with secondary rules which are rules of a different kind" (CL 94).

1. **Uncertainty → rule of recognition**: authoritative criteria fix the content of the rules (the elders who know the
   customs).
2. **Static quality → rules of change**: someone is empowered to change or create rules (an assembly deciding how to
   fish; a parliament).
3. **Inefficiency → rules of adjudication**: a third party is authorised to settle disagreements (a jury here, a judge
   there — the content differs, the rule type is the same).

The result is "not only the heart of a legal system, but a most powerful tool for the analysis of much that has puzzled
both the jurist and the political theorist" (CL 98).

The union is the centre of a legal system but not the whole. Hart's paradigm is the law of the modern state; unlike
Austin, who focused on the individual law, he looks at the system. International law, for Hart, is law but not a legal
system: separate primary rules, similar in function and content to municipal law, different in form.

Exam use: say whether a point concerns law in general or a legal system — Hart's book is mostly about the second.
