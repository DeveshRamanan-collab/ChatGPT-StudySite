Reasonable foreseeability is not a single test — it is asked three times in negligence, and means something
different each time. At the duty stage, the question is whether injury of some kind to the plaintiff, or to
a class of persons of which they were one, was reasonably foreseeable as a possibility; it is not necessary
to foresee the precise manner or sequence of events (*Chapman v Hearse*). At the breach stage, the question
is whether the kind of carelessness charged against the defendant might foreseeably cause damage of some
kind. At the remoteness stage, the question is whether the kind of damage the plaintiff actually suffered was
foreseeable as a possible outcome of that carelessness. The inquiry moves, as Glass JA put it, "from the
general to the particular".

In *Chapman v Hearse*, Chapman argued he owed Dr Cherry no duty because he could not have foreseen the exact
chain of events that led to Cherry's death; the High Court rejected this, holding it was enough that injury
to a class of person including Cherry — a bystander rendering aid at an accident scene — was reasonably
foreseeable.

Exam use: when a defendant argues "I couldn't have foreseen exactly what happened", identify which of the
three foreseeability questions they are really disputing, and answer only that one.
