Art 51's right is triggered "if an armed attack occurs". But what about anticipatory self-defence, where an attack
has not yet occurred but is imminent — for instance, a build-up of armed forces at the border?

There is serious controversy between proponents arguing that anticipatory, unilateral self-defence is customary
international law and those rejecting it as incompatible with the strict wording of art 51. The lecturer warned that
a pre-emptive strike taken too early can itself amount to an act of aggression.

The **US National Security Strategy 2002** (the George W. Bush administration's "War on Terror" after 9/11) claimed
the option of pre-emptive action "to counter a sufficient threat to our national security … even if uncertainty
remains as to the time and place of the enemy's attack". There was little support for this expanded definition.

There is **more support if a specific attack is manifestly imminent** — and in any case all the other restrictions
apply: the response must still be necessary and proportionate, and reported. The debate continues in light of modern
technologies that allow attacks to be launched with high speed and great destructive potential. It remains a grey
area.

Exam use: when the facts show preparations rather than an attack, start from the words "if an armed attack occurs",
argue imminence on the facts — how specific, how close, how certain — and apply necessity and proportionality either
way.
