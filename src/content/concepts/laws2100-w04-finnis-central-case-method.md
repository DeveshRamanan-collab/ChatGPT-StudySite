Finnis's is a **substantive** natural law theory, based on practical reasonableness and basic human goods.
"No theorist can give a theoretical description and analysis of social facts without also participating in the work
of evaluation, of understanding what is really good for human persons, and what is really required by practical
reasonableness" (NLNR 3). Evaluation is needed even for a descriptive theory.

Finnis credits Hart and Raz with greater explanatory power than the "rather naive methodologies of Bentham, Austin,
and Kelsen", with three features: attention to practical point; selection of central case and focal meaning;
selection of viewpoint (NLNR 6).

Hart uses a central case (the union of rules as law's 'essence', CL 155) and says Austin "failed to fit the
facts". But descriptive theorists disagree about what is important and significant. By what
criteria is a meaning focal or a case central, and from what viewpoint?

Hart gives explanatory priority to the internal point of view, yet treats it as an amalgam of very different
viewpoints (CL 203). Finnis says Hart should have focused on the **central case of the internal point of view** —
the practically reasonable person — which cannot be identified without asking what practical reasonableness
really requires in relation to the basic goods.

For Finnis, in the central case of law, authority "depends … on its justice or at least its ability to secure justice"
(NLNR 260).
