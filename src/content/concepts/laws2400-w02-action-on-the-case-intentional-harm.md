Where the harm is not a direct interference actionable as trespass, an action on the case may still lie for
malicious prosecution or for the intentional (or indirect) infliction of personal injury.

Malicious prosecution requires: the defendant initiated proceedings against the plaintiff; those
proceedings terminated in the plaintiff's favour; the defendant acted maliciously in initiating them; and
the defendant acted without reasonable and probable cause. Because it is an action on the case, the
plaintiff must also show damage to life, liberty, reputation or property. Malice (a positive requirement —
acting for a non-legitimate purpose) and the absence of reasonable and probable cause (a negative
requirement, tested both subjectively and objectively) are distinct elements that must both be met (*A v
New South Wales*).

Intentional infliction of personal injury covers intentional but indirect harm that neither trespass (which
needs directness) nor negligence (which needs a duty of care) may adequately remedy. The cause of action
lies in wilfully doing an act calculated to cause physical harm and actually causing it (*Wilkinson v
Downton*, a practical joke that caused nervous shock). An actual subjective intention to harm is not
required — reckless indifference to the result will suffice (*Nationwide News v Naidu*, workplace bullying
causing depression and PTSD).

Exam use: reach for this tort when the facts show indirect or psychiatric harm that trespass to the person
cannot capture because the injury is only consequential on the defendant's act.
