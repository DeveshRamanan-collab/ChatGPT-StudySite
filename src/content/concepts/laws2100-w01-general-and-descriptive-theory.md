In the Postscript Hart says: "My aim in this book was to provide a theory of what law is which is both general
and descriptive" (CL 239).

- **General**: not tied to any particular legal system or legal culture.
- **Descriptive**: morally neutral, with no justificatory aims.

He carries out this descriptive enterprise with concepts such as duty-imposing and power-conferring rules, rules
of recognition and change, acceptance of rules, internal and external points of view, and legal validity
(CL 240). The two claims are criteria to assess the theory on its own terms — a metatheoretical exercise.

Finnis disagrees about what legal philosophy can be: making, acknowledging and complying with law involves acts
of rational judgment whose reasonableness "cannot be assessed without premises about true human goods, the nature
of persons … and the contours of the common good and human rights". For Finnis, a natural law thinker, analysis
is connected with what we ought to do — not what Hart or Raz try to do.

Dickson notes challenges to the very possibility of identifying law's nature: some doubt that a human-made social
construction can have a nature; others doubt conceptual analysis as a method. Her own view: legal philosophy
elucidates law, an active and never-ending process of identifying law's most important and significant features,
which can help us decide whether to take law as having authority over us.

Exam use: test "general" and "descriptive" separately — a theory can pass one criterion and fail the other.
