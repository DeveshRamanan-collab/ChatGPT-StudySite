Four questions, in order.

1. Crime (art 5). Jurisdiction is limited to "the most serious crimes of concern to the international community as a
   whole": genocide (art 6), crimes against humanity (art 7), war crimes (art 8) and the crime of aggression (art 8
   bis). Nothing else — piracy, for example, is not on the list.

2. Time (art 11). Only crimes committed after the Statute's entry into force. For a state that becomes a Party later,
   only crimes after the Statute entered into force for that state, unless it has made an art 12(3) declaration.

3. Trigger (art 13). A situation may be referred to the Prosecutor by a State Party (art 14) or by the Security Council
   acting under Chapter VII of the UN Charter (as with Libya in 2011), or the Prosecutor may initiate an investigation
   proprio motu (art 15).

4. Preconditions (art 12). A state that becomes a Party thereby accepts jurisdiction over the art 5 crimes. For a State
   Party referral or a proprio motu investigation, one of these states must be a Party or have accepted jurisdiction:
   the state where the conduct occurred (or the state of registration of the vessel or aircraft), or the accused's
   state of nationality. A non-party may accept jurisdiction by declaration lodged with the Registrar (art 12(3)) and
   must then cooperate without delay or exception. A Security Council referral needs no such link.
