When lawyers reason or dispute about legal rights and obligations, they use standards that do not function as
rules, but as principles, policies and other standards. Positivism is "a model of and for a system of rules", and its
single fundamental test for law misses them (TRS 22).

- **Principle**: a standard observed "not because it will advance or secure an economic, political, or social
  situation deemed desirable, but because it is a requirement of justice or fairness or some other dimension of
  morality."
- **Policy**: a standard "that sets out a goal to be reached, generally an improvement in some economic, political,
  or social feature of the community".
- Example: "the standard that automobile accidents are to be decreased is a policy, and the standard that no man may
  profit by his own wrong a principle." (TRS 22)

Principles vs rules is "a logical distinction": both point to decisions about legal obligation, but they differ in the
character of the direction they give (TRS 24).

- A rule applies in an all-or-nothing fashion (if A then B); a principle states a reason that argues in one direction.
- Principles have the dimension of weight/importance; rules do not. Conflicting rules: one must go. Conflicting
  principles: weigh them.
- Form does not always tell you which it is: look at how legal actors reason with it.

Principles cannot be identified by pedigree: Dworkin's counterexample to the rule of recognition.

Exam use: classify a standard by how it works in argument.
