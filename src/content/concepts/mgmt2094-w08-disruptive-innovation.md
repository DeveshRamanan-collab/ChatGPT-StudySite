Not every successful or radical innovation is disruptive. In Clayton Christensen's theory, as the class activity
summarises it, disruptive innovation usually **begins by serving an overlooked, low-end or new-market customer
segment** with a **simpler, cheaper or more accessible offering**, and then **improves enough to challenge
established firms**.

The activity turns the definition into five questions:
1. **Incumbent** — what established company, industry or business model is potentially being disrupted?
2. **Initial customer** — who did it first appeal to? Was this customer underserved, overlooked or a non-consumer?
3. **Value proposition** — what made it cheaper, simpler, more convenient or accessible?
4. **Market movement** — did it start outside the mainstream and then move toward mainstream customers?
5. **Verdict** — disruptive innovation, potentially disruptive, or not disruptive? Defend the decision.

Its cases are open questions, not settled answers: Netflix vs video rental, Airbnb vs hotels (disruptive, or simply a
new platform business model?), Uber vs taxis, Canva vs professional design software (a new market of non-designers?),
Spotify vs CDs and downloads (where did the disruption actually occur?), Tesla vs traditional car makers, and ChatGPT
vs traditional search (could it become disruptive, and to whom?).

On the innovation map, disruptive innovation sits with strategic innovation: external and discontinuous.

**Exam use:** never call something disruptive because it is new, big or successful — walk the questions and defend a
verdict.
