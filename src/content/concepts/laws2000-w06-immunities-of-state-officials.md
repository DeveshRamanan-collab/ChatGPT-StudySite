State representatives are immune because they 'personify' the state and need protection to fulfil their
functions. The immunity belongs to the state, not the person: in Arrest Warrant (DRC v Belgium) [2002] the ICJ
said a foreign minister's immunities are "not granted for their personal benefit, but to ensure the effective
performance of their functions". The source is customary international law, not the 2004 Convention.

Functional immunity (ratione materiae) attaches to the act: only official acts taken while in office, done with
official authority ('under the colour of authority'); it continues after the official leaves office and can be
waived only with the state's consent. Example: a soldier who kills an enemy soldier on the battlefield.

Personal immunity (ratione personae) attaches to the office: complete immunity, even for private acts, but only
for very high-ranking officials — heads of state, heads of government and foreign ministers (Djibouti v France
2008 confirms their privileged status in custom). It lasts only while in office, after which functional immunity
remains; it can be waived by the state. Example: a sitting Greek president who drives drunk on holiday in
Australia and kills someone is immune there; once out of office, the private act is uncovered.

Neither immunity applies in the official's own state's courts.

Exam use: ask rank, then official or private act, then whether the person is still in office.
