International law has no legislature, no executive and no police, and disputes need not go before a
compulsory court — states must agree to a court's jurisdiction before they can be brought before it.
Yet almost all states obey almost all of international law almost all of the time. Enforcement is
"irrelevant to the binding quality of international law as a system of law": states operate within it
not because it is peremptory, but because it is necessary if anything meaningful is to be achieved --
"the basis of obligation is the same in every legal order: a consciousness among the subjects that
this order is needed" (Hoffman, 1987).

Compliance instead comes from fear of "loss of reputation", which leads other states to exclude a
non-complying state from future opportunities to cooperate (Chayes & Chayes, 1995) — other states
becoming reluctant to trade with, or ally with, a state that regularly disregards its commitments.
There is also quasi-enforcement: participation in international organisations is contingent on
compliance and trust — Arts 5 and 6 of the UN Charter allow suspension or expulsion from membership,
and bodies like the IMF and World Bank can exert economic leverage over members' conduct.

Against this, realism holds that what matters is power, especially military power, and that a
militarily powerful state will only obey international law when it perceives this to be in its
interest.

Exam use: never explain non-compliance as proof international law "doesn't work" — explain it against
reputation, quasi-enforcement and self-interest.
