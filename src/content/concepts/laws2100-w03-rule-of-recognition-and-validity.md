Where a rule of recognition is accepted, "both private persons and officials are provided with authoritative criteria
for identifying primary rules of obligation" — an authoritative text, legislative enactment, customary practice, general
declarations of specified persons, or past judicial decisions (CL 100). Its existence is shown in the way primary rules
are identified: it is manifest in a general practice, kept alive especially by officials and judges. For Austin
sovereignty came first; for Hart the rule comes first.

**Validity is membership**: "To say that a given rule is valid is to recognize it as passing all the tests provided by
the rule of recognition and so as a rule of the system" (CL 103). The rule is ultimate and supreme (CL 105–107); there
may be several rules of recognition and one ultimate rule.

Is the rule of recognition itself valid? "No such question can arise … it can neither be valid nor invalid but is simply
accepted as appropriate for use in this way" — like the standard metre bar in Paris (CL 109).

**Existence of a legal system** — two minimum conditions, necessary and sufficient: valid rules must be generally
obeyed; and the rules of recognition, change and adjudication must be "effectively accepted as common public standards
of official behaviour by its officials" (CL 109). The first is factual; the second is what Hart adds to Austin. Both
concern existence, not legitimacy.

Exam use: *Miller* shows judges reasoning with the rule of recognition.
