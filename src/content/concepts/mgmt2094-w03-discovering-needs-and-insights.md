The guest lecture's first stages are **discover** (go out and understand customers' needs) and **distil** (turn the
research into needs and insights). Fall in love with the problem, not the solution, and get out of the building:
customers may not know the solution, but they do know their pain points and what they like, so ask about their lives,
not for solutions.

Pursue multiple lines of inquiry, like a detective:

- **be the customer** — experience the situation yourself;
- **be with the customer** — observe them, then interview them;
- **learn about them** — from people around them (partners, hairstylists, nutritionists);
- **talk to extreme users** — people who use the product a lot or not at all. Their experience is heightened, so needs
  surface more easily, and a solution built for them can still sell to the mainstream. Innovation is not polling.

Distil with an **empathy map** (what customers say and do, then — more importantly — think and feel, all from real
research) and then a **customer narrative**: a descriptive customer, a **need written as a verb** (nouns are often
solutions) and an **insight** that goes deeper than the reason, to the root cause. A Stanford d.school team asked for a
low-cost incubator for Nepal found the incubators empty: the real need was to keep a premature baby warm without the
means to get it to hospital.

Exam use: rewrite a noun-shaped need as a verb, and push the insight to the root cause.
