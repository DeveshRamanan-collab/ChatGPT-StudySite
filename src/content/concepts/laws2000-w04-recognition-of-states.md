Recognition is the act by which a state officially acknowledges the existence of another state or government —
a complex mixture of politics and law. States sometimes recognise entities that fail the Montevideo criteria,
and refuse entities that meet them. No diplomatic relations does not necessarily mean no recognition.

Under the declaratory theory (the most widely held today) recognition only acknowledges a pre-existing
statehood; it is symbolic. Under the constitutive theory recognition creates the state and confers legal
personality: no recognition, no existence. Collective recognition happens through international organisations;
UN admission needs a Security Council recommendation (vetoable) and two-thirds of the General Assembly.

Non-recognition may prevent statehood where the criteria were achieved illegally (1970 Friendly Relations
Declaration; *Kosovo* Advisory Opinion 2010 on "the unlawful use of force or other egregious violations of norms
of general international law"). The TRNC meets the criteria but is recognised only by Turkey; Security Council
resolutions 541 (1983) and 550 (1984) called its declaration legally invalid. Kosovo (100+ recognitions),
Palestine (150+, UN non-member observer) and Western Sahara remain contested.

Exam use: state both theories, apply the Montevideo criteria first, then ask whether illegality blocks
recognition.
