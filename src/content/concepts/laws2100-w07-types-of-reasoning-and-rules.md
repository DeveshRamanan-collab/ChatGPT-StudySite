Main types of (legal) reasoning:

- **Deductive**: general rule → specific conclusion (eg syllogism: all documents with these characteristics are valid wills; this one has them; so it is a valid will).
- **Inductive**: specific cases → general principle (eg common law development).
- **Analogical**: comparing cases; similar facts → similar outcomes (precedent).
- **Abductive**: inference to the best explanation; plausibility, not certainty (from facts to the best available hypothesis explaining them).
- **Purposive/teleological**: reasoning in light of a rule's underlying purpose/rationale.
- **Reasoning by principle**: weighing and balancing competing principles/values (not fixed rules).

Main types of (legal) rules:

- **Mandatory**: impose a duty to act ('X must do Y').
- **Prohibitive**: impose a duty not to act ('X must not do Y').
- **Permissive**: grant a liberty/option ('X may do Y').
- **Power-conferring**: confer legal power to create/change/extinguish legal relations (making a will, passing legislation).
- **Constitutive**: create the activity/institution itself (contracts, marriage, corporations; outside law, a goal in football).

None of these is unique to law: we reason by analogy and precedent in sport and daily life. Some (analogical reasoning, precedent) are particularly important in law. Hart's duty-imposing/power-conferring distinction (chapter 3, against Austin) is the background: an invalid will is a nullity, not a punishment.
