"Land" for trespass includes the soil, fixtures such as a house, factory or (unharvested) crops, and the
airspace above the land — but only up to the height necessary for the ordinary use and enjoyment of the
land. Above that, an owner has no greater rights than any other member of the public: in *Bernstein v
Skyviews* an aircraft photographing the plaintiff's estate from several hundred feet up committed no
trespass. Low-level intrusions are different: a sign projecting 24 cm into the airspace (*Kelsen v
Imperial Tobacco Co*) and encroaching scaffolding (*LJP Investments v Howard Chia Investments*) were both
trespasses. The lecture leaves drones and minerals as open questions to work through using the same
height/purpose test.

To sue in trespass to land, the plaintiff needs *exclusive possession* at the time of the trespass — not
ownership. A tenant can sue; ownership merely helps prove possession where the owner's conduct shows
control. Possession has two elements: factual control, and an intention to exclude all others. In
*Newington v Windeyer*, a 50-year occupier who was never the registered owner could still sue in
trespass, because she showed exclusive possession through her conduct — hosting parties, maintaining the
garden and trees, and paying rates.

Exam use: when a problem raises "who can sue" or "is this actually part of the land", work through the
soil/fixtures/airspace-height test for what counts as land, then the factual-control-plus-intention test
for who holds the exclusive possession needed to sue.
