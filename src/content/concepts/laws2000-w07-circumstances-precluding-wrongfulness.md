Where a breach is attributable to a state, six circumstances (arts 20–25) may still preclude wrongfulness —
they act like a defence.

1. **Consent (art 20):** valid, freely given, clearly expressed before or at the time, and the act stays
   within its limits.
2. **Self-defence (art 21):** a lawful measure taken in conformity with the UN Charter, read with art 51 —
   an armed attack must have occurred.
3. **Countermeasures (arts 22, 49–53):** only against a responsible state, to induce compliance; not in
   breach of jus cogens (e.g. use of force); proportional; after procedural steps (negotiate, notify, stop
   once the wrong ceases). Czechoslovakia's diversion of the Danube was disproportionate
   (*Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros*, 1997).
4. **Force majeure (art 23):** an irresistible force or unforeseen event beyond the state's control making
   performance materially impossible; not if the state contributed or assumed the risk. "Absolute and
   material impossibility" (*Rainbow Warrior*, 1990; *Serbian loans*).
5. **Distress (art 24):** no other reasonable way of saving the author's life or lives in the author's care;
   not if the state caused it or the act creates a comparable or greater peril.
6. **Necessity (art 25):** framed in the negative — only way to safeguard an essential interest against a
   grave and imminent peril, without seriously impairing another's essential interest. The *Wall*
   construction was not necessary.

**Art 26:** none of these precludes the wrongfulness of an act contrary to a peremptory norm.

Exam use: after breach and attribution, test only the grounds the facts raise; finish with art 26.
