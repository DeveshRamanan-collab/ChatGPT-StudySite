Before a court or tribunal such as the ICJ can hear a case, a dispute must exist at the date proceedings are
instituted; otherwise it has no jurisdiction. So respondents often deny that any dispute exists — "a dispute whether
there is a dispute". Treaty law gives no test; the cases supply it.

- **Meaning:** "a disagreement on a point of law or fact, a conflict of legal views or interests between persons"
  (*Mavrommatis*, PCIJ 1924).
- **Objective determination:** whether a dispute exists "is a matter for objective determination", and "the mere
  denial of the existence of a dispute does not prove its non-existence" (*Peace Treaties* advisory opinion, 1950).
- **Clearly opposite views and awareness:** the claim of one party is "positively opposed" by the other, and the
  respondent "was aware, or could not have been unaware" of that opposition (*South West Africa*, 1962). More than
  mere friction; there must be communication showing the opposed views (*Marshall Islands v UK*, 2016). The
  threshold is low.
- **Examination of the facts:** the determination "requires an examination of the facts" (*Georgia v Russian
  Federation*, 2011, para 30) — public statements and other diplomatic exchanges; bilateral exchanges showing
  conflicting claims are usually the strongest evidence of a territorial dispute.

Exam use: before discussing consent, show from the facts that the applicant's claim was positively opposed by the
respondent, and that the respondent knew it, before proceedings began.
