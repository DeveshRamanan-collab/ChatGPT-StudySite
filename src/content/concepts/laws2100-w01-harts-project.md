"In law as elsewhere, we can know and yet not understand" (Hart, inaugural lecture, 1953). Hart's aim in *The
Concept of Law* is "to further the understanding of law, coercion, and morality as different but related social
phenomena" (CL 6).

The purpose is not a definition "by reference to which the correctness of the use of the word can
be tested", but "an improved analysis of the distinctive structure of a municipal legal system and a better
understanding of the resemblances and differences between law, coercion, and morality" (CL 17). The book "may also
be regarded as an essay in descriptive sociology" (Preface).

Debate in books is unending, yet most people can cite examples of law. "What is law?" expresses perplexities about
the general nature of law: beside paradigm cases (the legal systems of modern states) sit borderline cases
(primitive law and international law). Confusion may coexist with a firm knowledge of the law.

Three persistent questions (CL 6):
1. How does law differ from and relate to orders backed by threats? (coercion)
2. How does legal obligation differ from and relate to moral obligation? (morality)
3. What are rules and to what extent is law an affair of rules? (rules)

Together they ask for a definition — a map "exhibiting clearly the relationships dimly felt to exist
between the law they know and other things" (CL 14) — like crossing a familiar town without giving directions.

Exam use: knowing versus understanding is Hart's measure of what theory adds.
