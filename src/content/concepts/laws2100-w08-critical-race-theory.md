Critical Race Theory (CRT) emerged in the United States in the 1970s, gaining momentum in the 1990s (key scholars include Derrick Bell, Kimberlé Crenshaw, Richard Delgado, Cheryl Harris and Mari Matsuda). It was influenced by the trans-Atlantic slave trade and the civil rights movement, but many of its criticisms apply to other legal systems, including Australia. It critiques liberalism as the hegemonic ideology of the West and links to Critical Legal Studies and the Critical Philosophy of Race.

Main themes:

- race is a socially, culturally and legally constructed category: it exists because we have decided to make certain characteristics, like skin colour, relevant;
- against treating race as irrelevant and racial equality as mere equal treatment: racism can coexist with formal "objectivity" and "neutrality";
- storytelling and narratives give voice to unheard perspectives;
- rights matter, especially for historically disadvantaged minorities;
- intersectionality: race intersects with gender, class, sexuality and disability;
- whiteness operates as a form of property conferring legal and social advantages.

CRT questions liberal ideals such as meritocracy, equal opportunity and colourblindness, which can obscure structural racism. It is not just descriptive but normative: it aims to transform law and society to achieve racial justice.

Aileen Moreton-Robinson, *Talkin' Up to the White Woman* (2000): white Australian feminism positioned and silenced Indigenous women, and left their demands (maternity allowances, voting rights, labour rights) out.
