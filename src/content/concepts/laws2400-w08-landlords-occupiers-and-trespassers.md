Landlords once owed no duty for defective premises (*Cavalier v Pope*, 1906): the tenant, in control, was the one who
could see the defects. The High Court rejected that rule in *Northern Sandblasting v Harris* (1997).

*Jones v Bartlett* (2000): the tenant's son walked through a glass door that met the Australian standards when the
house was built but not when it was leased; the landlord did not know. A duty was owed but not breached. Gummow and
Hayne JJ: the key issue is the duty's nature and extent — premises reasonably fit for habitation as a domestic
residence. The landlord must not let premises with defects it knows or ought to know make them unsafe, and discharges
the duty by taking reasonable steps to ascertain defects and reasonable steps to remove them or make the premises
safe. The door was not a dangerous defect. Landlords also owe other entrants a duty, narrower than an occupier's.

An occupier is whoever controls the land, and owes a duty to lawful entrants; its extent depends on the relationship,
the purpose of entry and the type of premises. For
trespassers: "A person trespasses at their own risk and the occupier is under no duty to him except to refrain from
intentional or wanton harm" (*Commissioner for Railways v Cardy*, 1960). The position remains unresolved, but the
trend places more responsibility on the trespasser.

Exam use: a duty is usually there — argue its content and the reasonable steps.
