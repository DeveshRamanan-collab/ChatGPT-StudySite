The nineteenth century was dominated by positivism: the only true source of law was state will, not
fairness or divine justice, so states chose which obligations bound them. Positivism produced formal
institutions: the International Telegraph Union (1865), the Universal Postal Union (1874), the
Declaration of Paris (1856, limits on capturing private property at sea), the Declaration of St
Petersburg (1868, banning explosive bullets), and the Hague Conferences (1899, 1907), regulating war
and creating the Permanent Court of Arbitration. Colonialism continued: by 1914, 90 per cent of Africa
was under European control.

After the First World War (1914-1918), the League of Nations (1919) obliged states to submit disputes
likely to cause conflict to adjudication or inquiry, though it did not itself prohibit war; the 1928
Kellogg-Briand Pact went further, committing states to refrain from war as an instrument of national
policy. The Permanent Court of International Justice (1920) — dissolved in 1946, but still cited today
— was the direct precursor of the International Court of Justice.

After the Second World War (1939-1945), the Nuremberg trials (1945-46) began international criminal
law; the UN Charter (1946) replaced the League of Nations, entrenching sovereign equality,
self-determination and a ban on the use of force, and creating the Security Council and General
Assembly. The Assembly's 1960 Declaration on decolonisation affirmed self-determination — more than
80 former colonies have since gained independence.
