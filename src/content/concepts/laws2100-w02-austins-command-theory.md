For Austin, law in general is "a rule laid down for the guidance of an intelligent being by an intelligent
being having power over him" (PJD 18). There are four kinds of laws: divine law; human laws (laws set by political
superiors — positive law — and laws set by men not political superiors); objects improperly but by close analogy
termed laws (positive morality, including international law); and objects metaphorically termed laws (the laws
determining the movements of bodies). "The matter of jurisprudence is positive law" — law set by political superiors
to political inferiors.

Laws properly so called are a species of commands. Three ideas are captured by 'command': a wish that another shall
do or forbear; an evil to be incurred if the other does not comply; and an expression of the wish by words or other
signs. Command and duty are correlative: wherever a duty lies, a command has been signified, and wherever a command
is signified, a duty is imposed. Evil means sanction (punishments are a class of sanctions). Command, duty and
sanction are inseparably connected.

A law is a command which obliges generally to acts or forbearances of a class — not an individual command.
Superiority means might: the power to enforce compliance with a wish. Whoever can oblige another to comply is the
superior.

Exam use: this is the theory Hart builds his own against, and a live example of defining law per genus et
differentiam — law as a species of command.
