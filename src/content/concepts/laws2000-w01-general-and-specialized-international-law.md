"General" international law is the core this unit teaches: sources, treaty law, statehood and
recognition, jurisdiction and immunities, diplomatic protection, state responsibility, and peaceful
settlement of disputes. "Special" international law is the growing set of regimes built on that core:
use of force, international criminal law, human rights law, the law of the sea, air and space law,
environmental and climate change law, energy law, trade law, investment law, and health law.

These regimes rarely operate alone. A vessel rescuing migrants in distress at sea and seeking to dock
outside its flag state draws on migration law, the law of the sea and human rights law together; a
dispute over agrochemicals affecting another state's environment draws on trade law, environmental
law, health law and state responsibility together. Exam problems borrow across regimes rather than
sitting inside just one.

Two areas cut across the general/special divide. Global commons — the high seas, outer space,
Antarctica — belong to no state, so no state can impose its jurisdiction there, and every state (even
a land-bound one) retains freedom of navigation and access. Issues of shared concern — pandemics,
global trade, climate change — are transboundary by nature and can be tackled by "the nationalist
route" (each state acting alone) or "the internationalist route" (states acting together), the latter
increasingly the more effective of the two.

Exam use: name the specific regime(s) engaged before reaching for a rule — misidentifying the regime
is as costly as misapplying it.
