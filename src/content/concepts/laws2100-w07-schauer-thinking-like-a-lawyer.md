Schauer calls the vehicles-in-the-park exchange in the Hart–Fuller debate "a debate within a debate", with insights about legal rules, interpretation and language. Fuller's example: "It shall be a misdemeanor, punishable by a fine of five dollars, to sleep in any railway station." Can we interpret a word in a statute without knowing its aim? Do Hart and Fuller disagree about the concept of law, or about what judges do/should do?

*Thinking Like a Lawyer* (2009) asks: do lawyers and judges reason differently from ordinary people? Are there "techniques of reasoning" characteristic of legal decision-making? Take the sceptic (eg American legal realism) seriously.

Schauer's answer: perhaps, if we identify "methods of reasoning that are found everywhere but that are particularly concentrated and dominant in legal argument and decision-making, then the claim that there is something called legal reasoning will turn out to be justified" (p 8). Legal reasoning is special by concentration, not by being different in kind.

Legal reasoning and the Rule of Law:

- Legal reasoning goes with the inherent generality of law.
- The belief in "a moderately distinct form of reasoning we call 'legal reasoning' is in the final analysis an empirical claim" (p 11).
- "In a society governed by the wise and the good, legal reasoning is likely simply to get in the way", and the Rule of Law "would be at least superfluous, and quite possible pernicious" (ibid).

Exam use: separate the empirical question (do judges reason differently?) from the conceptual one.
