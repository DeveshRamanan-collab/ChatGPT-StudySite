When 'but for' gives an odd answer, how is factual causation decided? The answer moved.

**March v Stramare (1991) 171 CLR 506.** A truck parked mid-road was hit by a drunk, speeding driver. The trial judge found the defendant liable with 70% contributory negligence; the Full Court of South Australia blamed the plaintiff entirely. The High Court held the defendant's negligence was a cause, by different reasoning. Mason CJ: 'but for' was never the exclusive test; causation 'must be determined by applying common sense to the facts of each particular case'. McHugh J: 'but for' should be exclusive, with a 'scope of the risk' test bringing policy in as part of remoteness.

**The Ipp Report** criticised 'common sense': it hides policy choices — value judgments about who should be responsible and how far liability should extend — inside what looks like a factual question, making the law less transparent and predictable.

**CLA s 5D(1)** restructured and codified the common law (the cases still matter): (a) factual causation, a necessary condition; (b) scope of liability.

**Adeels Palace v Moubarak (2009)**: s 5D(1) 'treats factual causation and scope of liability as separate and distinct issues'; factual causation is the 'but for' test, and that security 'might have' made a difference does not prove it.

**Strong v Woolworths (2012)**: s 5D(1)(a) 'is a statutory statement of the "but for" test', applied on the balance of probabilities (s 5E).

Exam use: apply necessary condition first, on the evidence; keep policy for scope of liability.
