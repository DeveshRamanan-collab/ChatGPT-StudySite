Human rights are rights, so first ask what a right is. Many legal rights exist not because they are human rights but because we create them, eg by signing a contract. The literature offers two families of theories:

- **Will theory**: a person has a right if they have control or choice over whether others must or must not act in certain ways; rights are tools for exercising free will (autonomy).
- **Interest theory**: a person has a right if it serves to protect an important interest; rights are justified by the role they play in promoting or safeguarding well-being (welfare).

The lecture's objection to the will theory: it may focus too much on control. An infant cannot exercise choice as adults can; does the infant have no rights? The interest theory looks to welfare, not autonomy.

Human rights are a subset of rights: **plural** (how do we identify the set?), **universal** (a contract binds only its parties; human rights apply to every human being) and **high-priority** over the "general interest". A right can be constitutional without being a human right, and vice versa.

Qua human rights, they capture and protect our fundamental interests as human beings, resting on an idea of humanity. UDHR art 2: "Everyone is entitled to all the rights and freedoms set forth in this Declaration, without distinction of any kind". They include individual and group rights (eg self-determination).

Exam use: say which theory of rights your argument assumes before you ask who holds one.
