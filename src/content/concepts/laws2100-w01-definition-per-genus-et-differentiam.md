A definition *per genus et differentiam* gives a general kind plus distinguishing features:

- Triangle = three-sided rectilinear figure.
- Elephant = quadruped distinguished from others by its possession of a thick skin, tusks, and trunk.

It does two things at once: it provides a code or formula translating the word into other well-understood terms,
and it locates the kind of thing the word refers to, by indicating the features it shares with a wider family of
things and those which mark it off from others of that family. A good one captures all the essential properties:
a four-sided shape is not a triangle.

Some theorists, such as John Austin and Hans Kelsen, tried to define law this way — place law in a broader
category (with games, social etiquette, religion) and find the features that belong only to law.

Hart says this type of definition does not work when we are trying to define law. That is a big claim, and many
disagree. There are in fact two different claims:
1. it is impossible to define law per genus et differentiam; or
2. it may be possible, but there are better definitions of law.

Hart makes the first, stronger claim, so he must define law by another method — the end of chapter 1 of *The
Concept of Law*.

Exam use: separate the impossibility claim from the "better definition" claim before agreeing or disagreeing
with Hart — they need different arguments.
