"Lawsuits always raise, at least in principle, three different kinds of issues: issues of fact, issues of law,
and the twinned issues of political morality and fidelity." (LE 3)

1. What happened? — empirical disagreement.
2. What is the pertinent law? — "theoretical" disagreement about the law.
3. If the law denies compensation, is that unjust? — moral disagreement.

In *Riggs*, was Elmer legally entitled to the inheritance? How can judges disagree about what the law is, if the text
of the statute is clear? Two reasons for reading in principles of justice: legislators have a general and diffuse
intention to respect traditional principles of justice; and the statute should be interpreted to make the legal
system as a whole coherent in principle.

Law is a social phenomenon, but legal practice (unlike many other social phenomena) is argumentative. Dworkin "takes
up the internal, participants' point of view", studying legal argument "from the judge's viewpoint" (LE 14).

The argument against Hart: the rule of recognition exists because officials converge in accepting it. How can judges
agree on the criteria of validity and yet disagree on the content of the law? If Hart is right, there should be no
theoretical disagreements. In *Taking Rights Seriously* the counterexample was principles; in *Law's Empire* it is
theoretical disagreement.

Exam use: separate the three kinds of disagreement before you claim a theory cannot explain one.
