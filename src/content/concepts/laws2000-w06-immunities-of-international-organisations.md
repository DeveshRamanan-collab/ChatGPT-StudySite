International organisations such as the UN and EU also enjoy immunity from domestic jurisdiction, because they
need to carry out their functions without interference from the state where they operate, and to be treated alike
across countries. Unlike state immunity, IO immunities rest on treaty law — states grant them. IOs are immune from
adjudication (civil and criminal) and enforcement, while their officials' immunity is limited to acts in their
functions: functional, never personal.

UN Charter art 105 gives the organisation the privileges and immunities "necessary for the fulfillment of its
purposes", and member representatives and officials those necessary for the independent exercise of their
functions. The Convention on the Privileges and Immunities of the United Nations 1946 fills this in: the UN, its
property and assets are immune from every form of legal process unless expressly waived, and no waiver extends to
execution (art II s 2); premises are inviolable (s 3) and archives inviolable wherever located (s 4).
Representatives of members (art IV), officials (art V s 18) and experts on missions (art VI, continuing after the
mission) are immune for words and acts in their official capacity.

Accountability: IOs may have to provide alternative modes for settling private claims, and can waive immunity.
In the 2010 Haiti cholera outbreak (nearly 10,000 deaths) US federal courts upheld the UN's immunity in 2016. In
Mothers of Srebrenica the Dutch Supreme Court (2012) and the ECtHR (2013) recognised UN immunity, and the case
went on against the Netherlands alone.
