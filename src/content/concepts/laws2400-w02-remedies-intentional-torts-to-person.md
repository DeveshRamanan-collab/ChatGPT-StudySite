Damages are the most common remedy for intentional interference with the person; in suitable cases a court
may also grant an injunction to prevent an anticipated tort, such as a threatened battery, from occurring.

Damages for intentional torts to the person may be nominal, compensatory, aggravated or exemplary, and more
than one head can be awarded together.

Nominal damages are likely where the tort is made out but no real harm or damage was caused — because
trespass to the person is actionable per se, the plaintiff need not prove damage to succeed, but a token sum
reflects that little was actually lost.

Compensatory damages aim to put the plaintiff back in the position they would have been in had the wrong
not occurred — the ordinary loss-based measure.

Aggravated damages respond to the defendant's deliberate conduct where that conduct itself caused an affront
to the plaintiff, over and above the physical or economic loss — they compensate injured feelings.

Exemplary damages are different in purpose: they punish the defendant for conduct that was particularly
egregious, rather than compensate the plaintiff.

Exam use: after establishing liability, always address remedy separately — identify which heads are engaged
by the facts (was there real harm? was the conduct deliberate and humiliating? was it outrageous enough to
punish?) rather than assuming compensatory damages are the only available award.
