Generally there is no legal duty to rescue, and no duty to control the conduct of a third party who goes
on to harm the plaintiff. Where physical harm is caused by an omission rather than a positive act, more
than reasonable foreseeability is needed: *Hargrave v Goldman* shows the extra ingredients are a special
relationship (there, occupier-neighbour), the defendant's knowledge of the danger, and their practical
control over it.

Control does most of the work. In *Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre v Anzil*, the shopping centre
controlled its car park lighting but had no control over the criminals who attacked Anzil there — so its
duty as occupier, resting on "control over, and knowledge of, the state of the premises", did not extend
to their independent criminal conduct. Reliance and assumption of responsibility can also found a duty
over a third party's conduct, but neither was made out here.

Exceptions cut the other way: parents may be liable for failing to control their children, and an occupier
owes entrants a duty against third-party conduct with real control, or high foreseeability and
predictability. Sections 55–58 CLA protect a "Good Samaritan" — one who, in good faith and without
expectation of reward, assists a person apparently injured or at risk — from liability for their response,
unless they caused the risk or were relevantly impaired.

Exam use: for an omission, ask what gave the defendant knowledge and control over the harm's source —
foreseeability alone is never enough.
