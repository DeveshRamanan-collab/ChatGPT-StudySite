International law is "a process of continuous interaction between States grounded into practices or
expectations of sovereign governments" that "changes and evolves... by the existence of new interests
and technologies" — rights are asserted and duties relied on by reference to agreements or custom. It
evolves through new treaties, and through modifying existing ones: amending provisions, or adopting
protocols that form a whole with the parent treaty. Custom, built from general state practice plus a
belief that the practice is law, is the clearest illustration of law developing through time and
practice rather than legislation.

International environmental law shows a regime built almost from scratch since the 1960s. UN
conferences (the 1972 Human Environment Conference), agencies (the Environment Program, the Food and
Agriculture Organization) and, crucially, the General Assembly built non-binding momentum — the Rio
Declaration (1992) — because the Assembly cannot itself create binding obligations. States then turned
that momentum into binding treaties: the UNFCCC (1992), the Kyoto Protocol (1997), and the Paris
Agreement (2015), with the ICJ's 2025 climate advisory opinion following the same body of work. The
1985 Vienna Convention and 1987 Montreal Protocol on the ozone layer show the effect numerically:
emissions of ozone-depleting substances rose for decades, then fell sharply once the instruments took
effect, with the UN reporting in 2023 that the ozone layer is on track to recover by 2040.

Exam use: a "living process" answer should name the mechanism — treaty, amendment, protocol, or
custom — not just assert the law "changed".
