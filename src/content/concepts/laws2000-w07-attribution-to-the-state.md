In principle a state is only responsible for its own conduct, not that of private individuals. ARSIWA
chapter III (arts 4–11) sets out when conduct counts as the state's.

- **Organs (art 4):** any organ — legislative, executive, judicial or other, central or territorial,
  whatever its position. Status usually follows internal law, but entities acting in
  "complete dependence" on the state may exceptionally be de facto organs (*Genocide* case, 2007, paras
  392–393).
- **Governmental authority (art 5):** a non-organ empowered by the state's law to exercise elements of
  governmental authority, acting in that capacity in the particular instance — e.g. privatised prisons,
  military or police functions.
- **Organs lent by another state (art 6):** attributed to the receiving state when exercising its
  governmental authority.
- **Ultra vires (art 7):** still attributed if the organ or entity acts in that capacity, even if it exceeds
  its authority or contravenes instructions (*Velásquez Rodríguez v Honduras*, IACtHR 1988).
- **Insurrectional movements (art 10):** attributed only if the movement becomes the new government or
  establishes a new state.
- **Acknowledgement and adoption (art 11):** conduct becomes the state's if and to the extent that it
  acknowledges and adopts it as its own — Iran's decree endorsing the students in *Tehran*.
- **Other states (arts 16–18):** aid or assistance, direction and control, coercion.
