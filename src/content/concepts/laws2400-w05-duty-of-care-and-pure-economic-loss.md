Pure economic loss (PEL) is loss not consequent on any physical injury to person or property; the CLA's
definition of "harm" (s 5) expressly includes economic loss. Reasonable foreseeability of the loss is
necessary but never sufficient on its own to found a duty of care for PEL (*Perre v Apand*), because of the
risk of liability in an indeterminate amount (*Ultramares Corp v Touche*) and a disproportion between the
defendant's blameworthiness and the scale of potential liability.

For negligent misstatement, *Hedley Byrne v Heller* requires, beyond foreseeability: a special relationship;
a voluntary assumption of responsibility by the party giving the advice; the other party's reliance on it;
and the reasonableness of that reliance. Hedley Byrne itself lost because Heller had disclaimed
responsibility for its reference.

Vulnerability of the plaintiff — an inability to protect themselves from the defendant's want of care — is
also required in PEL cases generally (*Woolcock Street Investments v CDG*), assessed through the same
Hedley Byrne factors: known reliance, assumption of responsibility, and reasonable reliance given the
subject matter, the occasion, the parties' relative positions and their capacity to exercise judgment.

Exam use: for any negligent-advice fact pattern, run all four Hedley Byrne factors, then check separately
whether the plaintiff was actually vulnerable — could they have protected themselves another way?
