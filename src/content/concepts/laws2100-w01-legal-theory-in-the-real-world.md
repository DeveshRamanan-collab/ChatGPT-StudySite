Legal theory has generated concepts that go back into the world: judges use, more or less explicitly, theories of
law or their concepts to make sense of a case and decide it.

- *Milirrpum v Nabalco Pty Ltd* [1971] 17 FLR 141 (the Gove land rights case): Aboriginal claims to land were
  questioned on the basis that the customary rules of Aboriginal peoples could not be described by the court as
  legal rules — assumptions from Austin's theory. You cannot fully understand Mabo without it.
- *Madzimbamuto v Lardner-Burke* [1969] 1 AC 645: the Privy Council considered the legality of Rhodesia's 1965
  Unilateral Declaration of Independence in light of Hans Kelsen's theory of law.
- The *Miller* case (UK Supreme Court, 2017), on whether the Government could withdraw from the EU without an Act
  of Parliament, used Hart's rule of recognition — "the rule which identifies the sources of law in our legal
  system" [173].

Use your legal knowledge to put pressure on the theories.

Why study it? Gardner: jurisprudence contributes to excellence in argument and helps us recognise different
perspectives on a legal issue; "a philosophical education encourages us to look … for timeless problems
underlying topical problems". Green: legal philosophy offers "an escape from the 'default setting' of your own
little legal system" and shows "the difference between the way things are around here and the way things ought
to be".

Exam use: a real case is the quickest proof that a theoretical point matters.
