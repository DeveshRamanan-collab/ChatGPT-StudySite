Ngaire Naffine (Professor Emerita, Adelaide), 'In Praise of Legal Feminism' (2002): a "reflective appreciation of feminist legal theory" in four parts: aims and methods; female agency; the female legal actor/subject; feminist scholarship and the legal academy.

**Aims**: investigate and challenge the ways the law is not impartial and objective (and, when it appears impartial, is not applied equally), upholding patriarchal institutions and participating in the systematic oppression of women. Examples: sexual assault law, the defence of provocation, the marital rape exception, the historical reluctance to intervene in domestic violence.

**Methods** (theory and practice): consciousness raising; the naming and re-naming of the injuries done to women; the legal recognition of those injuries. Results: new expressions and legal categories ('sexual harassment', 'date rape', 'domestic violence').

**Epistemology**: feminist legal theorists are "neither stranger nor native"; they are the 'outsider within' (p 77). Naffine discusses the "epistemic privilege of marginality", a particular perspective on the deficiencies of law. The methodology is generous: receptive of other bodies of knowledge and open to revision. An epistemology of largess, versus "the jurisprudence of orthodoxy": "a willingness to concede that new points of view might be better than well-used ones; that one's view is necessarily limited, partial, and so consistently in need of revision" (p 81).

The lecture: contrast Kelsen's pure theory, which keeps law free of other disciplines. Ask why Kelsen does not use consciousness raising.

Exam use: explain why a method is used, rather than listing the methods.
