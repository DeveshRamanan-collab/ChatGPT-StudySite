"Tort" comes from the Latin *tortum*, "something twisted, wrong or crooked". Tort law is the body of rules
and principles for civil wrongs: it tells people how to behave towards others — avoid causing harm, and if
harm is caused, remedy it (usually through compensation). There are several well-known individual torts,
which raises a long-debated question: is it the "law of tort" (one set of unifying principles) or the "law of
torts" (a list of separate causes of action)? John Salmond argued tort law is merely a collection of
miscellaneous causes of action; Pollock and Winfield argued it rests on fundamental principles, such as
duties owed by everyone to everyone else.

In common law countries tort law is mostly judge-made, so cases are critical, but statutes are increasingly
important — often responding to a societal problem such as the price and availability of insurance. The
Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) is a leading example. Statutes have not replaced or codified the common law
outright; instead they modify or abolish parts of it and create new causes of action, so cases and statutes
now co-exist.

For the exam: always check whether a statute (like the CLA) has modified the common law rule before applying
it, and be ready to explain whether a problem fact pattern reflects "law of tort" style unifying principle or
a discrete "law of torts" cause of action.
