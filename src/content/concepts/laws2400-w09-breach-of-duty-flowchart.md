Breach is the second element of negligence. Once a duty is established, the court measures the defendant's
conduct against a standard of care; falling short — far short or just short — is a breach.

At common law, *Wyong Shire Council v Shirt* (1980) 146 CLR 40 set two limbs, which the textbook flowchart turns into
two questions:

1. **Was the RISK of injury to the plaintiff reasonably foreseeable?** — what standard of care does the defendant owe?
   The higher the degree of risk, the less reasonable it is to fail to take steps to prevent it occurring.
2. **Was the RESPONSE of the defendant to this risk reasonable?** — has the standard been met?

Civil Liability Act ss 5B and 5C now govern breach, although s 5B sits under the heading "Duty of care" — a trap: it
is the test for breach. Section 5B(1)(a) (foreseeable) and (b) (not insignificant) relate to the first question;
s 5B(1)(c) (a reasonable person in the person's position would have taken those precautions) and s 5B(2)(a)–(d) to the
second. The criteria are joined by "and". The Act's three elements were all in the common law but often conflated;
now each must be separately addressed (*Benic v NSW*, Garling J). The Act presupposes the common law and operates
against its background, so the cases still matter.

Exam use: after duty, identify the risk of harm (Benic step 1), then walk s 5B(1)(a), (b) and (c) in order — failing
any one ends breach.
