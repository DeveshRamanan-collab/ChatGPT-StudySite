Directness is the element that separates trespass (a direct act or interference) from action on the case (an
indirect or consequential act). *Hutchins v Maughan* [1947] VLR 131 is the key illustration: the defendant
laid poisonous baits on unfenced land; the complainant's dogs found them days later and died. Because the
baits were laid before the complainant brought his dogs onto the land, the injury was not immediate but
consequential upon an intervening event (the complainant himself bringing the dogs there), so trespass did
not lie — the remedy lay in case. Herring CJ traced this to the old distinction in *Reynolds v
Clarke*, *Scott v Shepherd*'s "log thrown into the highway" example, and *Leame v Bray*: if the log hits
someone as it is thrown, that is trespass; if someone is later injured tripping over it lying in the road,
that is merely case.

Directness is often described as "an issue of time" — had the defendant thrown the baits directly at the
dogs, trespass would have been made out. *Williams v Milotin* (1957) 97 CLR 465 shows the distinction still
has bite: a pedestrian hit directly by a car could plead either trespass to the person or
negligence (action on the case) from the same facts, and each has different elements and a different
limitation period.

For the exam: ask whether the plaintiff's own (or a third party's) intervening act was needed to turn the
defendant's act into harm — if so, it is consequential, not direct.
