A rule of international law may be contained in a treaty and be a rule of custom simultaneously — the two
are not mutually exclusive. The Nicaragua case identifies three ways a treaty and a rule of custom can
relate: a treaty may codify a pre-existing rule of custom; a treaty may crystallise a developing rule of
custom, prompting the intense state practice needed to complete its formation; or a treaty may give rise to
an entirely new rule of customary law, where a practice on a genuinely new issue develops around a treaty
provision. North Sea Continental Shelf confirms the same possibilities.

The clearest example is the right to self-defence: it exists as a rule of customary international law, and
it is also written into the UN Charter at Article 51. Because the two exist in parallel, a state does not
need to be a party to the UN Charter to have a right of self-defence — it can rely on the customary rule
instead, which may carry a different specification of the right than the treaty version.

Exam use: don't assume a treaty provision displaces a parallel customary rule (or vice versa). Where a
state is not party to the relevant treaty, check whether the same rule also exists in custom before
concluding it has no obligation or entitlement at all.
