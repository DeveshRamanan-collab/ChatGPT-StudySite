Trespass requires fault: the defendant's direct interference must be accompanied by intention, recklessness,
or negligence. Intention and recklessness are subjective (the defendant's own state of mind); negligence
(failure to exercise reasonable care) is objective, judged by the standard of a reasonable person in the
defendant's position — though negligent trespass is rare today, since such cases are now usually brought
under the tort of negligence instead. Intention is only required as to performing the act itself, not its
consequences: if the defendant touches someone mistakenly believing it is their partner, that still counts
as an intentional act, and the mistake is no defence (see p 46). Recklessness applies where the consequences
are so likely that the defendant is taken to have foreseen them — this is rare, since most trespass falls
under intentional conduct.

On the onus of proof, the plaintiff need only prove the direct interference occurred; the onus then shifts to
the defendant to disprove fault, typically by proving the interference was an "inevitable accident" (see p
43). The exception is harm arising out of use of the highway: there, the plaintiff must prove the
defendant's fault, because the public's right to use the highway means road users and adjoining property
owners must accept some risk and so bear the onus. In an action on the case, by contrast, the plaintiff
always bears the onus of proving fault.
