For Kelsen, law is by its very nature a definite type of norm; "the law is a coercive order" (p 1) — coercion
distinguishes it from other normative orders — and "it is a peculiarity of the law that it regulates its own creation,
application and execution" (p 5). 

His gangster is Hart's gunman: the two commands have the same subjective meaning, but "the command of the tax officer is
authorized by an objectively valid legal norm, whereas the command of the gangster is not" (p 6). The search for the reason of validity reveals the hierarchical structure of a legal order (the
*Stufenbau*).

If a norm is valid, it is binding; validity cannot be a fact; only a competent authority can create a valid norm. A
council regulation is valid because a higher norm authorised the council, and so up to the constitution, then back
through earlier constitutions to the historically first constitution — the chain of validity. Why is that valid? We
must presuppose a norm: you ought to obey the historically first constitution. This basic norm (*Grundnorm*) is a
transcendental-logical presupposition. Validity is binding force, not membership.

The theory is pure because it "answers the question of what the law is, not what it ought to be. The latter question is
one of politics, while the pure theory of law is science" (1941).

Exam use: purity is the strictest statement of the value-free ideal Question 1 tests.
