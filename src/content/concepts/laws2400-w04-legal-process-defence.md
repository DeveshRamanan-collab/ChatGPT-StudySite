Legal process — acting under a lawful power — is a defence to trespass to the person and to land. Lawful
arrest is the clearest example: under the Law Enforcement (Powers and Responsibilities) Act 2002 (NSW),
arrest without warrant under s 99(1) requires both a reasonable suspicion that the person is committing
or has committed an offence, and satisfaction that arrest is reasonably necessary for a reason the Act
lists — suspicion being more than a mere possibility but less than a belief. Sections 230–231 confine the
force used in effecting an arrest to what is reasonably necessary.

The defence also covers entering and remaining on land under a lawful power. *Halliday v Nevill*
establishes the implied licence for anyone, including police, to use an unobstructed path or driveway for
lawful communication with an occupier. In *Roy v O'Neill*, the High Court held (3:2) that police checking
compliance with a domestic violence order acted within that implied licence, and that observing the
occupier's intoxication then independently enlivened a statutory power to remain on the land to conduct a
breath test.

Exam use: for an arrest, check both limbs of s 99(1) and whether the force used was reasonably necessary;
for entry or remaining on land, start from the implied licence and ask whether a separate statutory power
justifies staying beyond it.
