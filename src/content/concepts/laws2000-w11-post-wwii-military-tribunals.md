The Allies shared an intention to punish Axis war criminals but were divided over purpose and procedure. The
London Agreement, with the IMT Charter annexed, created the Nuremberg International
Military Tribunal (IMT), providing for the individual criminal responsibility of leaders, organisers, instigators and
accomplices. Article 6 listed three crimes "for which there shall be individual responsibility":

- crimes against peace — planning, preparing, initiating or waging a war of aggression or a war in violation of
  treaties, or conspiring to do so;
- war crimes — violations of the laws or customs of war, such as ill-treatment of civilians or prisoners of war,
  killing hostages and plunder;
- crimes against humanity — murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation and other inhumane acts against any
  civilian population, or persecutions on political, racial or religious grounds, whether or not in violation of
  domestic law.

The International Military Tribunal for the Far East (IMTFE) was established by proclamation of US General Douglas
MacArthur, implementing the Potsdam Declaration. Twenty-eight defendants were charged with 55 counts under art 5(a)–(c):
crimes against peace, conventional war crimes and crimes against humanity. All were found guilty, though not on all
counts; seven were sentenced to death.

Control Council Law No 10 allowed domestic prosecutions in the Allied zones of occupation (twelve US trials by the
Nuremberg Military Tribunals), and the Allies — Australia among them — held thousands of trials in the Asia-Pacific
region and beyond.
