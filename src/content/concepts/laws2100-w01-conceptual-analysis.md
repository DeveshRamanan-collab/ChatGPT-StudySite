Analytical jurisprudence understands jurisprudence as the analysis of general ideas relevant in the legal
domain; the tradition predates Hart (Bentham, Austin). Hart combined it with the analytical philosophy practised
at Oxford, which focused on the analysis of ordinary language.

Conceptual analysis has been a key element/method of analytical philosophy. Conceptual theories generally define terms by necessary and sufficient conditions. Bix: "conceptual
theories and claims set the boundaries of categories. The drawing of such boundaries can be helpful in
establishing a common ground for investigation and discussion, but the placement of the boundaries is often
contested" (p 18).

According to Bix, purely conceptual theories can be contrasted with non-conceptual theories in the natural
sciences and non-conceptual theories in the social sciences. Those theories use concepts, but they are not
conceptual theories.

When a concept is contested, ask what kind of disagreement it is: semantic (about the meaning of the term) or
conceptual (not sharing the same concept). 'Football' in the UK versus Australia is easily solved — "I meant
soccer" — and context does the rest.

William Twining keeps conceptual analysis but widens it: it "has an important part to play in reviving general
jurisprudence, but … what is needed is a much broader conception of what is involved than those of Hart or Raz or
even Stone" (p 39); conceptual elucidation is as important for social scientific investigation as for legal
exposition (p 57).

Exam use: Twining is the ready counterargument to treating conceptual analysis as self-sufficient.
