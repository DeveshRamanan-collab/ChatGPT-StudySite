International criminal law (ICL) is a branch of public international law. International crimes are breaches of
international rules entailing the personal criminal liability of the individual(s) concerned — the person answers
for them, which is a different question from a state's responsibility for an internationally wrongful act (Week 7).

ICL has two limbs. Substantive law governs the four core international crimes and demands that states prosecute
them: the acts amounting to core crimes; the subjective elements required for those acts to be prohibited; the
circumstances in which an accused may nevertheless not be held criminally liable; and the conditions under which
states may prosecute or bring accused persons to trial. Procedural law regulates the stages of international criminal
proceedings — pre-trial, trial and appeals processes, rights of the accused, rules of evidence, sentencing and
victims' rights (victims have a prominent part). It is found in the statutes and rules of the various
international(ised) criminal tribunals.

Three features. ICL is relatively new, gradually expanding to cover new crimes. It is rudimentary: broadening its
substance has been complex, the actus reus and mens rea of the crimes have not always been clear, and penalties are
not laid down precisely. It is hybrid, drawing unique characteristics from international humanitarian law,
international human rights law and national criminal laws.

Exam use: when a problem asks who answers for an atrocity, keep the individual's criminal liability (ICL) separate
from the state's responsibility.
