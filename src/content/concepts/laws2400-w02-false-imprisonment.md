False imprisonment is the direct total restraint of the liberty of the plaintiff by an intentional or
negligent act of the defendant without lawful justification.

Elements: a positive act; direct interference; an intentional or negligent act; and total restraint of the
plaintiff's liberty without lawful justification.

Total restraint does not require physical boundaries or a prison — mental coercion, an assertion of
authority or a threat can imprison as effectively as a locked room, and a plaintiff can even be falsely
imprisoned without knowing it at the time (*Meering v Graham-White Aviation*; *Symes v Mahon*). Restraint is
total only where there is no reasonable means of escape (*Bird v Jones*); a condition attached to a lawful
entry — such as paying to leave a wharf — is a lawful excuse and defeats the claim (*Balmain New Ferry v
Robertson*).

Directness includes a defendant who directs, promotes or causes another person to detain the plaintiff, not
only one who detains personally (*Coles Myer Ltd v Webster*).

Fault can be intentional or negligent. An intentional act of imprisonment can still attract liability even
where the defendant reasonably but mistakenly believed they were acting lawfully — a reasonable mistake
about the right to detain is not itself a lawful justification.

Exam use: separate the "total" question (was there any reasonable escape?) from the "lawful justification"
question (was there a valid legal power or an accepted condition of entry?) — problem questions often plant
a partial exit or a plausible-looking authority to test both.
