Where there is a "foreign element", a state must justify prescriptive jurisdiction on a recognised ground.

1. Territoriality — the fundamental ground: jurisdiction over all acts on the territory and everyone located
   there, citizen or not. For cross-border crimes, objective territoriality covers an offence completed on the
   territory though elements occurred abroad; subjective territoriality covers offences initiated or planned on
   it.
2. Active nationality — a state may prosecute its nationals for acts abroad; dual nationals can face both
   states. States largely decide who their nationals are; *Nottebohm* describes nationality as "a genuine
   connection of existence, interests and sentiments". Ships and aircraft take their state of registration.
3. Passive personality — jurisdiction because the victim is a national (*US v Yunis*). It remains controversial
   and is better as a subsidiary ground.
4. Protection — acts abroad by non-citizens posing a genuine threat to a vital security interest:
   counterfeiting currency, espionage, coups, attacks on diplomats.
5. Universality — offences so serious that any state may act, with no direct link: piracy originally; now
   genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity and torture (Geneva Convention IV art 146; Criminal Code
   Div 268). Without a treaty it is controversial, so states usually require the accused's presence.

Concurring jurisdiction has no universally binding rule; treaties like the Cybercrime Convention (art 22(5))
require consultation, and usually the state with the closest link is best placed.

Exam use: for each state, name the link — place, offender, victim, security interest or the crime itself.
