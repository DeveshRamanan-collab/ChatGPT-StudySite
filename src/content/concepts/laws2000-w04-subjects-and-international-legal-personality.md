A subject of international law is "an entity possessing international rights and obligations and having the
capacity (1) to maintain its rights by bringing international claims; and (2) to be responsible for its
breaches of obligation by being subjected to such claims" (Crawford). Who is a subject is the question of who
has international legal personality.

Capacities ascribed to subjecthood include claiming rights under international law, entering diplomatic
relations, bearing obligations imposed directly by international law, creating international law (e.g. binding
treaties), and enjoying immunities from other states' jurisdiction — Australia cannot try New Zealand in front
of its courts, because there is no authority above a sovereign state.

States are the only subjects with full legal capacity; others have only partial capacity. In *Reparation for
Injuries* (1949) the ICJ held the UN to be an international person although the Charter never says so — "not
the same thing as saying that it is a State" — and recognised that the subjects of law "are not necessarily
identical in their nature or in the extent of their rights".

Exam use: never ask only "is X a subject?" Ask which capacities X has, and where they come from.
