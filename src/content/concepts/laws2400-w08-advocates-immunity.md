As a matter of public policy, barristers are immune from liability for negligence in the conduct of cases in court,
and in pre-trial matters intimately connected with the case in court. *Giannarelli v Wraith* gave the policy reasons:

1. the advocate's duty to the court may conflict with the duty to the client;
2. court time may be wasted if advocates present numerous arguments to protect themselves against negligence
   allegations;
3. the finality of judgment — a negligence case about how the first case was run disrupts that judgment and
   undermines the court process. Later cases treat this as a key reason.

The immunity is narrow. It is about the courtroom, not legal advice: advice from lawyers and other professionals falls
under pure economic loss caused by negligent misstatement (Topic 7). And solicitors can definitely be sued for
negligence in acting for clients — the lecturer's example is a solicitor who failed to diarise a limitation period,
leaving a $10 million claim statute barred and the client with a claim against the solicitor.

Exam use: ask who the lawyer is, what they did and where — the conduct of a case in court or work intimately connected
with it (immunity), or advice and file management (ordinary negligence or negligent misstatement).
