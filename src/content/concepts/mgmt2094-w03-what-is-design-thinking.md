In essence, design thinking is a process that harnesses creativity to resolve problems and create solutions with
the intent of making a product, a service, or a business, better (the reading). Tim Brown of IDEO: "a system that uses
the designer's sensibility and methods to match people's needs with what is technologically feasible and what a viable
business can convert into consumer value and market opportunity". Gerda Gemser: practices that help companies see
problems from a new perspective and solve them in an atypical way.

The guest lecture calls it a **human-centred, creative and experimentation-driven approach to innovation**: the
customer at the core, creative thinking to generate ideas, and quick, cheap tests of them. Why use it? More innovations
and start-ups fail from a lack of customers than from a failure of the product — people make something that works, but
nobody wants it.

It suits problems where neither the problem nor the solution is known, and VUCA (volatile, uncertain, complex,
ambiguous) conditions. Gemser: it is not suited to incremental improvement — use it for something radically different.
You need not be a trained designer: Deloitte calls it 'designful thinking', a disposition and a mindset. Verganti adds
why it matters: innovation is useful only when it creates value for people, and value comes from meaning — "no design,
no meaning, no value, no innovation, no business".

Exam use: define it with an authority, name its three features, then say why this organisation needs it (customer risk,
uncertainty, radical change).
