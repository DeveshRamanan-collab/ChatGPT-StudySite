The principle of legal paternalism maintains that state coercion is justified to protect individuals from **self-inflicted harm**, or to guide people, whether they like it or not, toward their own good. Both the harm and the offence principles reject it: they look to harm or offence to others, paternalism to the well-being of the individual.

All states employ some elements of **(soft) paternalism**: eg prohibiting people from consenting to their own disablement or violent killing; the law of contract forbids selling oneself into slavery. **Hard paternalism** is where the state intervenes for the person's own good. Some drug laws may be classified as either soft or hard examples.

The rationale for legal paternalism weighs five things:

1. the degree of probability that harm to oneself will result from a course of action;
2. the seriousness of the harm being risked;
3. the degree of probability that the goal we want to reach will in fact result;
4. the value or importance of achieving that goal (how worthwhile is it?);
5. the necessity of the risk (is there a less risky way of achieving that goal?).

The lecture: the more sophisticated versions weigh the interference with freedom against the value of the goal, and ask whether measures short of public coercion would do. These questions return in Raz's liberal perfectionism.

Exam use: say whether a law is soft or hard paternalism, then run the five factors.
