Jurisdiction is "the lawful power of a State to define and enforce the rights and duties, and control the
conduct, of natural and juridical persons" (Oxman). It is linked to sovereignty — supreme authority within a
territory — which also implies sovereign equality, territorial control, non-intervention (ius excludendi alios),
sovereign immunity and diplomatic immunity.

Three forms. Jurisdiction to prescribe: the power to make national law applicable to persons, property and
activities, by legislation, executive act, regulation or court determination. In *Lotus* the PCIJ said
international law leaves states "a wide measure of discretion" to extend their laws beyond their territory,
limited only by prohibitive rules. Jurisdiction to enforce: the executive putting laws into operation — arrest,
seizure, freezing assets, fines. It is the most intrusive form, and *Lotus* sets the "first and foremost
restriction": failing a permissive rule, a state "may not exercise its power in any form in the territory of
another State". Israel's 1960 seizure of Eichmann in Argentina was unlawful; the Security Council requested
compensation. Jurisdiction to adjudicate: domestic courts hearing and bindingly deciding disputes — in principle
territorial, though Lockerbie saw a Scottish court sit in the Netherlands applying Scottish law.

Theft illustrates all three: criminalising theft is prescriptive; arresting, remanding and imprisoning the thief
is enforcement; prosecuting them is adjudicative.

Exam use: prescription reaches abroad unless prohibited; enforcement abroad is prohibited unless permitted.
