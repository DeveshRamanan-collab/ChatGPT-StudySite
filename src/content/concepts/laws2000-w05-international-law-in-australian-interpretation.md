International law shapes how Australian law is read. Where a statute directly
incorporates a treaty's text, it is interpreted in line with the treaty's meaning unless Parliament expressed a
contrary intention (*Applicant A*). Under s 15AB(2)(d) of the *Acts Interpretation Act 1901* (Cth), a treaty
can assist interpretation only if (a) Australia actually or contemplatedly participated in it before the Act
was enacted, and (b) it is used to confirm the ordinary meaning, or the Act is ambiguous or obscure or would
otherwise produce a manifestly absurd or unreasonable result. But a treaty cannot modify words that are
otherwise clear (*Yager*, Mason J).

Two common law presumptions: in a case of ambiguity, courts favour a construction that accords with
international law (*Jumbunna*) — a presumption only, which does not apply to clear contrary legislation
(*Polites*); and the principle of legality — courts will not impute to Parliament an intention to curtail
fundamental rights and freedoms unless it is clearly manifested by unambiguous language (*Coco*).

The Practitioner's Guide adds that obligations are identified as they exist when the interpretive question
arises, and that in conflict, international law cannot override clear and valid Australian law — Parliament
may legislate in breach, "taking the risk of international complications".

The Constitution? No majority support. Kirby J (*Newcrest*) saw it as accommodating international law "to the
full extent that its text permits"; McHugh J (*Al-Kateb*) called that "heretical" — amending the Constitution
without a referendum.

Exam use: clear words always win.
