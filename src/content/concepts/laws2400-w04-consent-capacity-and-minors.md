Consent must come from a competent person — someone with sufficient understanding to comprehend the act
and its consequences. Adults are presumed competent, but this is rebuttable: an adult who is drunk,
asleep or unconscious may lack capacity. In *Brightwater Care Group v Rossiter*, a paralysed but mentally
competent patient directed staff to stop his nutrition and fluids; continuing against his wishes was held
to be a trespass, on the basis of his right to autonomy and self-determination.

Minors can also have capacity. *Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech Area Health Authority* held that a
minor can consent once she has "sufficient understanding and intelligence to enable her to understand
fully what is proposed". The High Court approved the *Gillick* test in *Secretary, Department of Health
and Community Services v JWB (Marion's Case)*, but ruled that a child's disability does not itself defeat
capacity, and that parents cannot consent on a child's behalf to non-therapeutic treatment not in the
child's interests.

Even a competent "mature minor" is not always the final word: in *X v Sydney Children's Hospital
Network*, the parens patriae jurisdiction was held to extend to any person under 18 and overrode a
competent 17-year-old's refusal of a blood transfusion, since s 174 of the Care and Protection Act did
not exclude it absent clear words.

Exam use: work through the presumption of capacity, then Gillick competence for minors, then check
whether a statute or the parens patriae jurisdiction can override even a competent refusal.
