*Esanda Finance Corp v PMH* (1997): Esanda lent to companies associated with Excel, relying on accounts PMH
had audited and certified; Excel went into receivership. **No duty** — no circumstances establishing a
relationship of proximity. Being one of a class who might rely on audited accounts was not enough: there was no
request, no intention to induce Esanda to act, and no assumption of responsibility; an audit informs the company
and its shareholders, not lenders.

Dawson J: "the mere foreseeability of harm does not, where the only harm is pure economic loss, give rise to a
duty of care"; the law requires "a special relationship between the parties which is described as a relationship
of proximity". "Proximity" is the historical label; the modern approach treats the factors as salient features
(*Sullivan v Moody*).

McHugh J's policy reasons against auditors' liability: higher auditing costs, more complex litigation,
indeterminacy, and difficulty proving reliance.

The lecture's closing list:

1. Reasonable foreseeability (necessary but not sufficient)
2. Indeterminacy of liability
3. Category of relationship
4. Defendant's knowledge (of matters affecting the plaintiff, including reliance)
5. Reliance by the plaintiff (reasonable in the circumstances?)
6. Assumption of responsibility by the defendant
7. Special skill of the defendant
8. Vulnerability of the plaintiff

Not a rigid checklist: factors cross over and courts look at the circumstances as a whole. Foreseeability must
always be ticked; the rest are weighed, with reliance and assumption of responsibility especially important.

Exam use: start with Esanda, then weigh the factors.
