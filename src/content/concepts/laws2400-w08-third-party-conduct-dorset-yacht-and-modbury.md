A defendant may owe a duty for a third party's conduct that injures the plaintiff — a triangle in which the third
party causes the harm but the plaintiff sues the defendant. Such a duty is found only in certain circumstances:
usually a pre-existing relationship (defendant–plaintiff or defendant–third party) or a situation of control.

*Home Office v Dorset Yacht* [1970]: boys escaped from Home Office detention and damaged yachts in the nearby harbour.
A duty was owed: damage on escape was reasonably foreseeable and the officers had control over the boys. Policy
limited its scope to damage in the vicinity immediately after the escape — not further damage while the boys remained
at large (indeterminate liability).

*Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre v Anzil* (2000): a video store employee was attacked in the car park after the
lights went off. No duty to protect him from a third party's criminal conduct: the centre had not created the risk,
it had merely failed to prevent harm caused by someone else, and there was no special relationship.

There is a general reluctance to make defendants protect others from third parties' criminal acts, especially where
the defendant did not create or control the risk. But *RTA v Refrigerated Roadways* found a duty about objects dropped
from an overpass: in Modbury the harm could only arise through criminal conduct, and the RTA had a unique role.

Exam use: draw the triangle — find the control or relationship edge, or say there is none.
