In most negligence cases, whether the defendant owed the plaintiff a duty of care is uncontroversial,
because the relationship between them has already been recognised by the courts as giving rise to a duty.
Recognised categories include: a driver of a vehicle owes a duty to other road users; an employer owes a
duty to its employees; a doctor owes a duty to their patients; a manufacturer owes a duty to the consumers
or users of its products (*Donoghue v Stevenson*; *Grant v Australian Knitting Mills*); and a solicitor owes
a duty to exercise due care for their clients.

Where a case falls within an established category, you can simply point to the case law recognising that
category — for example, "this is a manufacturer/consumer relationship, an established category since
Donoghue v Stevenson" — without needing to run the full salient features analysis.

Unusual facts can still pull an apparently ordinary case away from the paradigm: *Chapman v Hearse* was a
car accident case, an established category between road users, but its unusual chain of events (a bystander
doctor killed while rendering aid) put the existence of a duty back in issue.

Exam use: always check the category first — only move to the salient features approach once you have
concluded the facts are genuinely novel.
