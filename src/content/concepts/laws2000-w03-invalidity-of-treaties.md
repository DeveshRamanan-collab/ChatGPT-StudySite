A treaty can be perfectly concluded — the right people signed it, in writing, all art 2(1)(a) elements met —
and still be invalid. VCLT grounds split into two chambers. **Relative invalidity**: error (art 48) — a
material factual mistake, like an erroneous map, important to the decision to join; fraud (art 49) — a
representative fraudulently misled into signing; corruption of a representative (art 50) — a non-due
advantage induces a representative to sign who otherwise would not have. **Absolute invalidity**: coercion of
a representative (art 51) — economic pressure, blackmail, or a threat to their life; threat or use of force
against the state itself (art 52), which voids the treaty ab initio; as does conflict with a peremptory norm —
jus cogens (art 53) — accepted by the international community as one from which no derogation is permitted,
such as the prohibitions on torture, genocide, aggression, piracy, and self-determination.

Violating a state's own internal law on who could conclude the treaty is deliberately *not* a ground for
invalidity, except narrowly. *Cameroon v Nigeria* confirms a state cannot invoke its own internal-law
violation "unless the violation was manifest and concerned a rule of its internal law of fundamental
importance" (art 46) — protecting the other party's reliance on the treaty.

Exam use: separate "was consent properly given" (arts 46–51) from "is this treaty simply not yet binding" —
invalidity is a defect infecting a concluded treaty, not a timing problem.
