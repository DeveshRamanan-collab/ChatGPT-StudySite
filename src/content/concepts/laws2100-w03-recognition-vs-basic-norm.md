In Kelsen's theory the basic norm performs the same function as the rule of recognition in Hart's: each explains legal
validity and the unity of a legal order. Hart knew Kelsen's work and discusses the basic norm in a long endnote.

1. **Fact or presupposition.** For Hart the existence of a rule of recognition is a complex, empirical question of fact:
   a real social rule that exists if officials buy into it. For Kelsen the basic norm is a "juristic hypothesis", a
   "postulated ultimate rule", a "fiction" — presupposed, not part of the legal order.
2. **Validity.** For Kelsen, a jurist/theorist must presuppose the validity of the basic norm. For Hart, the rule of
   recognition is neither valid nor invalid.
3. **Content.** Kelsen's basic norm has always the same content ("who laid down the first constitution ought to be
   obeyed"); only the first constitution differs. For Hart, rules of recognition in different jurisdictions may well
   have different content.
4. **Law and morals.** "One reason for using the expression 'rule of recognition' instead of a 'basic norm' is to avoid
   any commitment to Kelsen's view of the conflict between law and morals" (CL 293). For Kelsen a legal and a moral norm
   cannot both be valid at once, so no meaningful conflict is possible; Hart can accept such conflicts.
