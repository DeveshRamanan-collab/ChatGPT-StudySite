Two case studies apply the week's principles to real life.

**Adult pornography.** Traditionally, bans were justified by its offensiveness to the community's moral standards or its tendency to deprave and corrupt. From the liberal perspective these justifications are obviously moralistic and do not aim to prevent harm: if enjoying sexually explicit material is a private matter, it should not be prohibited by law.

Cf. **Catharine MacKinnon and Andrea Dworkin**: distinguish erotica from pornography. The issue is not preventing an allegedly immoral yet harmless action; pornography **harms women** and infringes women's **right to equality**. This moves the argument from moralism to harm.

**Voluntary euthanasia.** Four terms:

- **voluntary**: at the request of an adult person of sound mind;
- **non-voluntary**: on someone not capable of understanding the choice between life and death;
- **passive**: intentionally letting someone die;
- **active**: a positive act of killing.

Adults in full command of their mental capacities have the right to refuse life-sustaining treatment, but cannot ask to be actively killed. Is the choice of voluntary, active euthanasia harmful? Does it violate the rights of others? Devlin cites its prohibition as the criminal law protecting the sanctity of human life, a moral principle, without harm to others.

Exam use: run each principle in turn and test each argument against its counterargument.
