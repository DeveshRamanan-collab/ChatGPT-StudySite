There are two exceptions to the prohibition on the use of force:

- **Security Council authorisation (art 42)** — if measures under art 41 "would be inadequate or have proved to be
  inadequate", the Council "may take such action by air, sea, or land forces as may be necessary to maintain or
  restore international peace and security". Members confer on the Council "primary responsibility for the
  maintenance of international peace and security" (art 24); its powers over threats to the peace, breaches of the
  peace and acts of aggression are in Chapter VII (Week 10).
- **Self-defence (art 51)** — the "inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack
  occurs", "until the Security Council has taken measures necessary". Its purpose is to fend off current persistent
  attacks (or prevent further attacks) and restore the status quo ante bellum.

**Invitation** works differently. Force used in another state at its government's request does not violate art 2(4)
at all: the state has consented, so there is nothing to excuse. But in *Nicaragua* the ICJ said non-intervention
would lose its effectiveness if intervention could be justified "by a mere request for assistance made by an
opposition group". The lecturer noted Cold War UK guidelines doubting the consent of a government that has lost
control of part of its territory; today states commonly intervene at a government's request regardless.

Exam use: before arguing self-defence, ask whether the territorial state's government consented or the Security
Council authorised the force.
