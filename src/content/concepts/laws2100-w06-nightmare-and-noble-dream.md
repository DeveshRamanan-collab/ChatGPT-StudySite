Hart's lecture on American jurisprudence notes "a concentration, almost to the point of obsession, on the judicial
process" (Holmes, Gray, Llewellyn, Jaffe), explained partly by the "quite extraordinary" role of US courts, especially
the Supreme Court, and wide powers of judicial review.

**The Nightmare.** Litigants consider themselves entitled to an application of the existing law to their disputes, not
to have new law made for them; judges and legislators have different functions and powers. The Nightmare is that this
image of the judge is an **illusion**. American Legal Realism presented it not simply as a feature of difficult
adjudication (eg constitutional adjudication) but as an essential feature of law and adjudication. Are judges
law-makers?

**The Noble Dream.** Judges apply existing law; they never make new law, even when constitutional text, statutes or
precedents appear to offer no determinate guide. There is none the less an existing law somewhere which judges can and
should apply. It does not take the form of an invocation of a universal natural law. For Hart, **Dworkin is the noblest
dreamer of all**: even in the hardest case, where two interpretations fit equally well, the judge is never to make law.

The lecture links the dream to the one right answer thesis and a liberal view of law: legal positions determined ex ante.
Hart treats both as exaggerations, to be read with the distinction between easy and hard cases.
