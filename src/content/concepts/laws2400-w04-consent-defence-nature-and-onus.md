Consent is a defence available where a person freely agrees to conduct that would otherwise be tortious.
It covers battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass to land and interference with chattels, and is
underpinned by deference to individual autonomy — if a person has genuinely agreed to the interference,
the law generally will not intervene.

Courts disagree about what consent actually is. Courts in England and New Zealand treat consent as an
element of trespassory conduct, so the plaintiff bears the onus of proving its absence. Courts in Canada
and Australia have taken a different view. In *Secretary, Department of Health and Community Services v
JWB (Marion's Case)*, McHugh J reasoned in obiter that consent is a defence, so the onus falls on the
defendant to prove consent was given. Leeming JA in *White v Johnston* took the opposite obiter view,
considering consent an essential element of assault and battery. The position in Australia remains
unsettled.

The classification matters practically: as a defence, the defendant must plead and prove consent; as an
element, the plaintiff must plead and disprove it — changing who loses when the evidence on consent is
unclear.

Exam use: flag that consent's classification (defence vs element) is unsettled in Australia, cite both
obiter positions, and note that whichever party bears the onus is the party who loses if the evidence on
consent is inconclusive.
