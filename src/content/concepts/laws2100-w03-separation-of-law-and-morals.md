Green and Adams: positivism is "the thesis that the existence and content of law depends on social facts and not
on its merits". Austin: "The existence of law is one thing; its merit and demerit another" (PJD 157). Hart: "it is in no
sense a necessary truth that laws reproduce or satisfy certain demands of morality, though in fact they have often done
so" (CL 185–186).

In 'Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals' (1958), part of the Hart–Fuller debate, Bentham and Austin
"were not dry analysts fiddling with verbal distinctions while cities burn" but reformers. They never denied that many
legal rules mirror moral rules as a matter of historical fact, and Bentham never denied that moral principles might
enter a legal system through explicit legal provisions. They asserted two simple things: absent an express
constitutional or legal provision, a rule's violating morality does not show it is not law; and a rule's being morally
desirable does not show it is law (599).

The utilitarian tradition defended three doctrines — separation of law and morals, the analytical study of legal
concepts, and the imperative theory — and they are not a package.

Law as it is versus law as it ought to be is defended by Bentham, Austin and Hart; some find it intellectually
misleading and/or practically dangerous. Hart's three lines of criticism: law and morality; positivism and formalism in
adjudication; morally evil laws (the Nazi regime).

Exam use: separation denies a necessary connection, not every connection.
