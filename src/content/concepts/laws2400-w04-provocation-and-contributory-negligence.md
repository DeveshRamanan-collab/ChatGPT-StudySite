Provocation is a defence to a criminal charge, but generally not to an intentional tort. In *Fontin v
Katapodis*, the defendant argued he had been provoked into throwing glass at the plaintiff's face; the
High Court held provocation cannot reduce an award of compensatory damages, though it may still help
reduce or prevent an award of exemplary damages. Queensland and Western Australia are an exception,
where provocation may be pleaded as a tort defence.

Contributory negligence — carelessness by the plaintiff that contributed to their own injury — has also
been repeatedly rejected as a defence to intentional torts. In *Horkin v North Melbourne Football Club*,
an intoxicated plaintiff refused to leave a club, so employees ejected him with more force than
necessary. The trial judge reduced damages by 30% for contributory negligence, but the Victorian Supreme
Court restored full liability: an intentional battery and the plaintiff's carelessness differ "not
merely in degree but in kind", and the social condemnation attached to intentional wrongdoing differs
markedly from carelessness.

Whether contributory negligence should apply to conversion — say, a car left unlocked with keys in the
ignition and then stolen — remains a live policy question: the defence might encourage personal
responsibility, but a plaintiff's carelessness arguably should not excuse theft.

Exam use: for any intentional tort, state that provocation and contributory negligence are (subject to
the Qld/WA exception for provocation) not available to reduce liability, using the "degree vs kind"
language from Horkin to justify why.
