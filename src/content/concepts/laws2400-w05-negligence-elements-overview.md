Negligence is an action on the case, not actionable per se, so the plaintiff must prove three elements to
succeed: that the defendant owed them a duty of care; that the defendant breached that duty; and that the
breach caused the plaintiff compensable harm — physical, psychiatric or economic — assessed through factual
causation and the scope of liability.

Duty of care always requires reasonable foreseeability, following the "neighbour principle" from *Donoghue
v Stevenson*, but foreseeability is always required and not always sufficient on its own; in practice, most
duty questions are settled once the facts are matched to an established category of relationship.

The Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) applies to any claim for damages for harm resulting from negligence (s
5A), except civil liability excluded by s 3B — including intentional acts done with intent to injure or
kill, sexual assault, and claims under the workers' compensation or dust diseases regimes, which remain
governed by the common law or other legislation. Breach and causation have been substantially codified by
the CLA (ss 5B–5E); duty of care remains mostly a common law doctrine, modified by the CLA only in specific
contexts such as public authorities, mental harm and professional negligence.

Exam use: always state all three elements first, then work through each in turn — an answer that jumps
straight to breach or causation without first establishing duty loses easy marks.
