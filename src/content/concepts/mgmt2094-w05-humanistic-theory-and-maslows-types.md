The **humanistic theory of creativity** (Maslow; Rogers and Fromm) starts from the opposite end to the
psychoanalytical theory: creativity comes when needs are met, not from hardship.

Maslow's hierarchy of needs runs from physiological needs (air, water, food, shelter, sleep, clothing, reproduction)
to safety (personal security, employment, resources, health, property), love and belonging, esteem, and at the top
self-actualisation — the desire to become the most that one can be. Once we get to self-actualisation, we are free and
comfortable enough to express ourselves creatively. The lecture: we cannot be creative unless, at least partly, the
other needs are met.

The theory argues that environment is not a factor in creativity: a person able to meet all their needs can then
choose to be creative. Creativity is central to our growth and learning processes.

Maslow's three types of creativity:

- **Primary** — allows us to escape the daily grind / stress of life, channelled into painting, drawing, writing. It
  need not be good.
- **Secondary** — a higher level of thought; more thought out and involved than primary.
- **Integrated** — combines both: it may be spontaneous when we begin, but a lot more thought goes into what we hope
  to achieve before we begin, e.g. philosophy and scientific discoveries.

Example: the theory of evolution through natural selection is a scientific discovery — the slide's own example of
integrated creativity.

Exam use: contrast it with the psychoanalytical theory, and classify an activity by type.
