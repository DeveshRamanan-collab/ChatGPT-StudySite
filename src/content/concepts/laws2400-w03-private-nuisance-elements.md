Private nuisance is an action on the case protecting both tangible and intangible interferences with a
person's use and enjoyment of land. To succeed, a plaintiff must show title to sue, a substantial and
unreasonable interference, and that the defendant created, authorised or continued the state of affairs
giving rise to the harm.

**Title to sue.** Traditionally only a person with a proprietary interest — an owner or a tenant in
possession — can sue; a mere licensee cannot (*Oldham v Lawson (No 1)*). Courts wavered — *Khorasandjian
v Bush* and *Deasy Investments v Monreast* allowed a licensee to sue — before *Hunter v Canary Wharf Ltd*
overruled Bush and restored the proprietary-interest requirement.

**Substantial and unreasonable.** Courts balance the competing rights of neighbouring occupiers,
weighing frequency, duration, time, locality and distance, against an *objective* standard — the
ordinary person in the claimant's position, not one of abnormal sensitivity (*Munro v Southern
Dairies*). Mere loss of profit from lawful competition, or being overlooked, is not enough (*Victoria
Park Racing v Taylor*) — but the defendant's motive or malice can tip otherwise-lawful conduct into
nuisance (*Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett*).

**Created, authorised or continued.** Liability is not limited to the creator; an occupier who authorises
the activity, or who becomes aware of a nuisance and neglects to remedy it, can be liable too.

Exam use: work title to sue first, then the balancing factors, flagging motive/hostility; then identify
who — creator, authoriser or continuer — is the right defendant.
