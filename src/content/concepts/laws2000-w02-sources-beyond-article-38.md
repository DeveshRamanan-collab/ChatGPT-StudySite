Article 38 doesn't provide an exhaustive list. Four further categories are commonly identified. Unilateral
statements/acts: a state can create a binding obligation for itself through a public declaration, without
any offer, reciprocity or acceptance by another state required — in Legal Status of Eastern Greenland,
Norway's foreign minister's promise not to make difficulties for Denmark's claim became a binding
obligation, and in the Nuclear Tests Cases the ICJ held that French government statements evidencing an
intention to cease atmospheric testing were themselves a legal undertaking.

Jus cogens: peremptory norms "accepted and recognized by the international community of States as a whole"
from which "no derogation is permitted" (VCLT art 53) — the ILC's examples include genocide, crimes against
humanity, aggression, slavery, torture and self-determination. No treaty provision can validly contain one;
a rule that does is terminated.

Decisions of international organisations can bind states where their constitutive treaty says so: Article
25 UN Charter makes UN Security Council decisions binding on members (e.g. Resolution 1373 on
counter-terrorism, adopted weeks after 9/11).

Soft law: non-legally-binding declarations (e.g. UN General Assembly resolutions) do not themselves create
obligations, but can provide evidence of state practice or opinio juris, and can develop into custom or
prompt new treaties (the Universal Declaration of Human Rights led to the ICCPR and ICESCR).

Exam use: none of these four is independently as strong as a treaty or custom, but each can supply evidence
for one, or bind states through a different mechanism entirely.
