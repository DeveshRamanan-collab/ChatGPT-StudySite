Section 5B(1)(c) measures the defendant against "a reasonable person in the person's position". The reasonable
person is a legal fiction created by judges to apply the objective test, because there are too many subjective
variables. It first appeared in *Vaughan v Menlove* (1837) as "the man of ordinary prudence". Feminist scholars have
criticised the older "reasonable man" as built on male attributes by a predominantly male judiciary.

The construct is not completely devoid of particular characteristics:

- **Age counts.** *McHale v Watson* (1966) 115 CLR 199: a 12-year-old who threw a sharpened piece of scrap metal at a
  tree is judged by the standard of a normal child of comparable age. Kitto J: it is no answer to be abnormally
  slow-witted, quick-tempered, absent-minded or inexperienced — but a child may rely on a limitation of foresight or
  prudence characteristic of humanity at his stage of development.
- **Mental illness does not.** *Carrier v Bonham* [2002] 1 Qd R 474: the defendant's mental condition had no effect on
  the standard expected: unsoundness of mind is not a stage of development through which all humanity passes.
- **Inexperience does not.** A learner driver owes the same standard as a licensed driver: *Imbree v McNeilly* [2008]
  HCA 40. The standard is objective and impersonal.
- **Professional skill raises it** (s 5O).

Exam use: for a child defendant apply the comparable-age standard; for any other personal trait, the reasonable person
does not change.
