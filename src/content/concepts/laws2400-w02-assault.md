Assault is a direct and intentional act by the defendant which creates in the plaintiff a reasonable
apprehension of an imminent physical interference with their person.

The elements are: a positive act; direct interference; an intentional act (the weight of Australian
authority requires intention, though *New South Wales v McMaster* contains dicta suggesting otherwise);
reasonable apprehension in the plaintiff of imminent harmful or offensive contact; and no lawful
justification.

The intention required is the intention to cause apprehension of the battery, not an intention to actually
commit the battery — an important distinction from battery's own fault element (*Rixon v Star City Pty
Ltd*). The act may be mere words or a gesture.

Apprehension is assessed from the plaintiff's mind, but objectively: would a reasonable person in the
plaintiff's position have apprehended the threat? Fear is not necessary, and words alone can be an assault
provided they directly raise the apprehension.

Imminence does not mean immediate. A threat is imminent if there is no effective means of escape before it
can be carried out, even if the harm will only occur a little later (*Zanker v Vartzokas*, the girl trapped
in a moving van).

Exam use: battery and assault often occur in quick succession, but assault does not require touching and
can exist without a completed battery — always check apprehension and imminence separately from any actual
contact.
