The word 'jurisprudence' has more than one meaning. *Iurisprudentia* = *iuris* (of right, of law) + *prudentia*
(wisdom, foresight, knowledge). It can mean a body of judicial decisions ('the constitutional jurisprudence of the
High Court'), jurisprudence in relation to religious law (Hindu law, Jewish halakha, Christian canon law, Islamic
sharia), or medical jurisprudence (legal medicine). For our purposes, jurisprudence should be understood as legal
theory — a general theory of law.

Brian Bix uses 'jurisprudence', 'legal theory' and 'legal philosophy' interchangeably. Others (Cotterrell, 'Why
Jurisprudence Is Not Legal Philosophy') think they should be distinguished.

**General jurisprudence** deals with philosophical puzzles thrown up by all legal systems: what is law? what is a
legal system? what is legal reasoning? is there a general obligation to obey the law? Its object is not the law
of a particular jurisdiction but law in general — which is why some see it as a philosophical inquiry into the
nature of law.

**Particular jurisprudence** deals with puzzles that emerge in some legal systems or traditions but not
necessarily all: what is common about the common law? in what sense is Parliament sovereign? Where a system has
no doctrine of parliamentary sovereignty, that question does not really make sense.

The distinction is rough, and the two are not completely disconnected: we cannot think about law in general
without our knowledge of specific laws.

Exam use: say early whether your question is general or particular — it fixes what counts as evidence.
