Fuller's is a **procedural** natural law theory. In the Hart–Fuller debate (*Harvard Law Review*, 1958),
Hart restated Bentham's and Austin's "two simple things": it could not follow from the mere fact that a rule violated
standards of morality that it was not a rule of law, nor from the mere fact that a rule was morally desirable that it
was a rule of law.

Fuller replied that Hart should "concern himself more closely with a definition of law that will make meaningful the
obligation to fidelity to law" (635), and that most of the issues "can be restated in terms of the distinction between
order and good order" (644). Hitler's regime had judges who disregarded validly enacted law and officials who acted
"through the party in the streets" (652).

Law is the enterprise of subjecting human conduct to the governance of rules. Laws must be: general; public;
prospective; clear; coherent; practicable; stable; and there must be congruence between official action and the law.

This **inner** morality is the morality within law, which law must comply with to be law — not an external
morality used to evaluate law. The definition names no content: Rex fails not because his laws are unjust but because
nobody could comply. Following the principles treats subjects
as autonomous rational beings, which carries a moral dimension.

Hart's reply: accept the inner morality, but it is "compatible with very great iniquity" (CL 207).
