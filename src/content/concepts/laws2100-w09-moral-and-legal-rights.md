Authority: are human rights moral rights, legal rights, or both? Are there authoritative sources for them? For legal rights the answer is easier: many constitutions (not the Australian Constitution) include a Bill of Rights, high in the hierarchy of legal sources. Some direct how to read it: South Africa's s 39(1) says a court interpreting the Bill of Rights "must promote the values that underlie an open and democratic society based on human dignity, equality and freedom", must consider international law and may consider foreign law.

History sharpens the question. Where slavery was legal, enslaved people had no legal right not to be enslaved; did they still have a moral right? That positive law does not recognise a moral requirement does not mean the requirement does not exist, but the moral claim then needs its own justification.

Implementation runs the other way: from natural law to positive law, from moral rights to legal rights. Rights are "positivised" into domestic and international law, and constitutional protection shields them from ordinary legislation (eg Articles 1–19 of Germany's Basic Law; in Australia, no bill of rights in the Federal Constitution).

Recognition is not enough. Rights recognised in a constitution or treaty that stay there are "paper rights": law in books, not law in action.

Exam use: say whether your claim is about a moral right, a legal right, or both.
