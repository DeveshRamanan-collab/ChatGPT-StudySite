In Chapter III Hart raises three groups of objections to the model of coercive orders.

The content of laws: here Hart introduces the distinction between duty-imposing and power-conferring rules. Some
criminal law resembles coercive orders — this is forbidden; if you don't, you go to jail. But the law also gives
people powers: to make a contract, write a valid will, get legally married. There is no duty to make a will; if you
want one, there is a procedure to follow. Can power-conferring rules be explained as orders backed by threats?

The range of application: legislators make laws that apply to every member of the community, including themselves.
In Austin's theory the sovereign's commands are for the rest of the political community, not for the sovereign.

The modes of origin: customary law is not created or issued by the sovereign; customs develop in the community and
are accepted. Austin's answer is that customs are tacit orders of the sovereign, who by not interfering tacitly
accepts them.

Hart's strategy is the same throughout: he criticises Austin on Austin's own metatheoretical criteria. A descriptive
theory of positive law must fit the facts of real legal systems; if it does not, you cannot change the reality of
law — you change the theory. After each objection he states the reply Austin (or Kelsen) could make, and engages it.

Exam use: power-conferring rules are the best-known objection; name the other two.
