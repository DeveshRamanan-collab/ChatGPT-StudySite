de Langhe, Puntoni and Larrick (2017): the human mind struggles to understand nonlinear relationships — our brain wants to make simple straight lines. That works for books on a shelf, but business is full of nonlinear relationships, and even experts can default to their gut.

The fleet problem: replacing 10 MPG vehicles with 20 MPG ones saves 500 gallons per 10,000 miles; replacing 20 MPG with 50 MPG saves only 300, though +30 MPG looks bigger. Gas consumption is not a linear function of MPG.

Four types of nonlinear relationship:

- **Increasing gradually, then rising more steeply** — customer lifetime value against retention rate.
- **Decreasing gradually, then dropping quickly** — a mortgage balance over the loan's term.
- **Climbing quickly, then tapering off** — per-unit profit against volume (economies of scale).
- **Falling sharply, then gradually** — annual rate of return against payback period.

To limit linear bias: (1) increase awareness of linear bias; (2) focus on outcomes, not indicators; (3) discover the type of nonlinearity you're dealing with; (4) map nonlinearity whenever you can.

For marketers: sell benefits, not attributes — Apple's "1,000 songs in your pocket", not "5GB".

The lecturer opened Week 4 with this trap: critical-thinking tools such as the six hats bring in different perspectives to avoid it.

Exam use: name the curve, then show why the straight-line guess misleads the decision.
