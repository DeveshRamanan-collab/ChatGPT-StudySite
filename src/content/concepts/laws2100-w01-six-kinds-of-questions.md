Theories ask different questions; a claim can only be judged once you know which kind it is.

1. **Doctrinal** — eg questions about domestic law: what the law is on a specific issue. A hypothetical is a
   cluster of doctrinal questions.
2. **Normative** — descriptive vs normative claims (the is/ought distinction). Normative claims are either
   justificatory (defending the status quo) or critical (criticising it).
3. **Empirical** — explanatory/causal vs effects claims: why something happens, or what a new statute actually
   does to a community on the ground.
4. **Metatheoretical** — what are the success criteria for a given theory? Picking one theory over another is not
   like picking an ice-cream flavour: we look for criteria that make a theory superior, at least in some respect.
5. **Methodological** — how should we conduct research in this field? Settled in the natural sciences, harder in
   the social sciences, even more debatable in philosophy.
6. **Conceptual** — eg what is a contract? what is law? what is justice?

Legal philosophers tend to ask conceptual questions, but they also ask the others, and even an empirical inquiry
needs concepts. The task is to see how the questions relate.

These map onto two connected but different problems: the nature of jurisprudence (questions 4 and 5 about a
theory) and the nature of law (the object the theory explains).

Exam use: label the kind of question in your introduction, and do not answer a normative question with a
descriptive claim.
