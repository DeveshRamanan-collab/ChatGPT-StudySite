Conversion evolved from the old action of trover: wrongfully dealing with goods inconsistently with
another's possessory rights. It is a strict liability tort — good faith or mistake, eg by an auctioneer,
is no defence.

Conversion differs from trespass in two ways: conduct — conversion needs a serious "dealing" amounting to
dominion contrary to the true possessor's rights, not mere interference with physical state; and title to
sue — trespass needs actual possession at interference, while conversion allows a plaintiff with only a
right to immediate possession and some proprietary title (a merely contractual or custodial right is not
enough) (*Hocking v Director General of the National Archives of Australia*).

Conversion can arise from selling goods, taking possession without intending to keep them permanently, or
using them inconsistently with the owner's dominion (*Penfolds Wines Pty Ltd v Elliott* — using the owner's
branded bottles for one's own wine). "Serious enough" is genuinely contestable (*Bunnings Group Ltd v CHEP
Australia Ltd* — storing delivered goods on pallets was not conversion, but transporting Bunnings's own
goods on them was). An unconditional demand for return, wrongfully refused, is itself dominion.

Detinue remedies wrongful detention and, uniquely, lets a court order the goods' return (discretionary).
The plaintiff needs only a right to immediate possession, a clear demand, and a wrongful refusal — no
actual possession or ownership is required.

Exam use: plead conversion for a serious dealing already done, detinue to get the specific chattel back.
