The 'but for' test is not a complete answer. In *March v Stramare* Mason CJ noted three limitations: it cannot distinguish causes from necessary preconditions; it struggles with intervening events; and it fails with **multiple sufficient causes** — two or more events, each sufficient to cause the harm. The Negligence Review Panel made no recommendation on these, so the common law continues to apply.

Courts have taken different approaches. In *Cook v Lewis* (1961) the Supreme Court of Canada held both negligent hunters liable when the jury could not decide which gun fired the single bullet. Under **market share liability**, potentially negligent defendants each pay a portion of the compensation in line with their market share.

The textbook flowchart gives two kinds of "appropriate case" for s 5D(2):
- **Multiple sufficient causes** — "the cumulative operation of factors in the occurrence of the total harm in circumstances in which the contribution of each factor to that harm is unascertainable" (*Bonnington Castings v Wardlaw*; *March v Stramare*).
- **Material contribution to harm** — "the negligent conduct that materially increases the risk of harm in circumstances in which the state of scientific or medical knowledge makes it impossible to prove the cause" (*Fairchild*; *McGee*; *Amaca v Ellis*; *Henville v Walker*).

The lectures label these differently: *Bonnington* is a material contribution case, and increased risk (not enough — *Amaca v Ellis*) is kept apart from material contribution to harm. Use the lecture's terms.
