Battery is a direct and intentional or negligent act of the defendant which causes physical contact with
the body of the plaintiff without consent or other lawful justification.

The elements are: a voluntary and positive act by the defendant; direct interference; intentional or
negligent contact; physical interference (bodily contact); and that the touching was unlawful (no consent
or other lawful excuse).

Positive act: trespass cannot generally be committed by omission — standing passively causes no battery
(*Innes v Wylie*) — but a continuing failure to undo an earlier positive act can itself count as positive
conduct (*Fagan v Metropolitan Police Commissioner*).

Physical interference need not be the defendant's own body touching the plaintiff's: contact through a
weapon, a car or a substance such as tear gas is enough, so long as it is direct (*Binsaris v Northern
Territory*).

Unlawful touching is the qualification the bare elements list misses: the touching need not be hostile or
angry — any slight touching without a lawful excuse will suffice, because every person's body is inviolate
(*Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd*). Express or implied consent, the exigencies of everyday life (jostling in a
crowd, a tap on the shoulder to get attention) and reasonable self-defence are not unlawful touching.

Exam use: work through all five elements in order; the fault element (the defendant bears the onus to
disprove intention or negligence, except in highway cases) and the unlawful-touching qualification are the
two places students most often drop marks.
