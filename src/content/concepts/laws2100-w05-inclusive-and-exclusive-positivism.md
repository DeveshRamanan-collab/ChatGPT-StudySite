Hart's Postscript, his unfinished reply to critics, reads as a response to Dworkin: 1 the nature of legal
theory (general and descriptive, CL 239); 2 the nature of legal positivism; 3 rules; 4 principles and the rule of
recognition; 5 law and morality; 6 judicial discretion.

"According to my theory, the existence and content of the law can be identified by reference to the social sources of
law (e.g. legislation, judicial decisions, social customs) without reference to morality except where the law thus
identified has itself incorporated moral criteria for the identification of the law" (CL 269) — the social sources
thesis.

Does law necessarily set moral tests for legal validity?

- **Inclusive/soft positivism** (eg Hart): particular legal systems may make moral criteria necessary or sufficient
  for legal validity (in that system only). A constitutional equality principle can invalidate a procedurally correct
  but discriminatory statute. The connection is contingent, not necessary.
- **Exclusive/hard positivism** (eg Raz): moral criteria can be neither sufficient nor necessary conditions for legal
  validity. Once incorporated into the law, they become legal criteria.

The debate (Bix 46–49) has been criticised as intramural, but it started because the positivist camp recognised that
Dworkin's criticisms had merit.

Exam use: when a statute refers to fairness, say which kind of positivist you are and why that is still positivism.
