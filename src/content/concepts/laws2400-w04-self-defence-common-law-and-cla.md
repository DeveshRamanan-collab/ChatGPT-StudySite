Self-defence is a defence to trespass to the person, and can also protect third parties (family, or even
strangers) or property (land or chattels) against unlawful interference. For land, the defence is
generally only available to the person in possession, and a request to leave should normally be made
first.

At common law, *Zecevic v DPP* requires the defendant to prove a reasonable apprehension of physical
force to the defendant or others, and that the force used was proportionate to the threat. Proportionality
is assessed strictly: in *Fontin v Katapodis*, the defendant was struck with a wooden T-square, but the
High Court held his response — throwing glass at the plaintiff's face — was "out of all reasonable
proportion to the emergency" confronting him.

The Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) has codified and modified this defence, drawing on the criminal law:
s 52 protects a person who believed their conduct necessary for a listed purpose and whose response was
reasonable in the circumstances as perceived — but only where the conduct responded to was itself
unlawful, a requirement the common law test lacks. The two regimes run in parallel (ss 3A(1), 3B(1)(a)(iii)).
In *NSW v McMaster*, the Court of Appeal held both limbs of the statutory test were satisfied for an
officer who shot a man running towards him holding a metal rod.

Exam use: run the common law two-limb test, then check whether s 52 CLA also applies and whether its
extra "unlawful conduct" requirement is met.
