A new intervening act (*novus actus interveniens*) arises where 'but for' causation is established, but a later
event has some effect on the situation the defendant's negligence created. The court must decide whether the subsequent
event relieves the defendant of responsibility — whether it takes the injury outside the scope of liability — by asking
whether it breaks the chain of causation.

*The Oropesa* test: is the subsequent event "ultroneous, something unwarrantable, a new cause which disturbs the
sequence of events, something which can be described as either unreasonable or extraneous or extrinsic"?

To sever the connection, the intervening act must ordinarily be either:

- **voluntary human action** — conduct which is 'volitional'; or
- **a causally independent act which is a coincidence which is extremely unlikely** (*Haber v Walker*).

The flowchart sorts its examples into two groups:

- volitional conduct which **severed** the chain: *Rickards v Lothian*, *Yates v Jones*, *CES v Superclinics (Aust)
  Pty Ltd*;
- non-volitional conduct which **did not sever** the chain: *Urbanski v Patel*, *Haynes v G Harwood & Son*, *Medlin v
  State Government Insurance Commission*, *Hirst v Nominal Defendant*.

The facts of these cases are not in this week's materials: learn each group for the proposition it supports.

Exam use: identify the later event; ask whether it was volitional human action or an extremely unlikely coincidence;
cite the matching group. If the later event was itself negligence, use the negligent-acts branch instead.
