Chapter VII of *The Concept of Law* discusses varieties of rule-scepticism; the literature distinguishes two.

**Conceptual rule-scepticism** is about the nature of law. The real law should not be identified with "paper rules".
We are under a legal duty not to do X if and only if the courts say so, because doing X is likely to result in an
adverse court ruling. It challenges whether rules really exist, so Hart must defeat it to defend law as the union of
primary and secondary rules.

**Empirical rule-scepticism** is about adjudication. In deciding cases judges respond to the underlying facts rather
than to legal rules/reasons. There are so many rules that might be relevant that there is always support for both sides,
and conflicting ways of interpreting statutes and precedents. Judges cherry-pick doctrine to reach a result reached on
other grounds.

You can be an empirical rule-sceptic without embracing conceptual rule-scepticism: rules may exist, but judges do not
use them to decide cases.

The realists wanted a theory of adjudication consistent with scientific inquiry: empirical, observational and
positivistic in the scientific, not legal, sense, seeking "laws of judicial behaviour". Non-legal factors determine the
outcome:

- idiosyncratic adjudication (Frank): the judge's individual preferences;
- sociological adjudication (Llewellyn): shared background of judges as a class;
- pragmatic adjudication (Cohen): policy consequences.

Exam use: name the kind of scepticism before you rebut it; Hart's best arguments hit only one.
