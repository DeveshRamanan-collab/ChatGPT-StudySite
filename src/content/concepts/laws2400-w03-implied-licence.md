A path or driveway to the front door carries an implied licence for any member of the public on lawful
business to approach and knock, unless negated by a locked gate or a sign. *Halliday v Nevill* is the
leading statement: a police officer had an implied licence to enter an open driveway to arrest a
disqualified driver.

The licence is narrow and purpose-specific. It does not extend to administering a breath test to the
occupier (*Munnings v Barrett*) or to serving a summons (*Plenty v Dillon*) — those purposes fall outside
"lawful communication" with the occupier, so entering for them is trespass.

But a mixed purpose does not automatically defeat the licence. In *Roy v O'Neill*, police officers
attending a home to check compliance with a domestic violence order were not trespassers: the implied
licence to approach the front door included checking on the welfare of the protected occupant, and
having that *additional*, alien purpose (investigating DVO compliance) in mind did not negate it. An
entrant becomes a trespasser only where their sole purpose is outside the licence, the licence is revoked
and they remain, or they act inconsistently with it.

Exam use: identify the licensed purpose first (lawful communication/genuine business), then ask whether
the entrant's actual purpose(s) fit inside it, and whether anything (a sign, a lock, a revocation, or
later conduct) took them outside it.
