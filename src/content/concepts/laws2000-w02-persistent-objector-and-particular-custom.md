Once a rule of customary international law is established, it generally binds every state — but there are
two recognised exceptions. First, custom can be particular rather than universal: a regional custom binding
only a group of states, or even a custom existing between only two states. In Right of Passage over Indian
Territory (Portugal v India), the ICJ accepted that Portugal's centuries-long practice of accessing its
enclave through Indian territory had crystallised into a customary right of passage binding as between just
those two states, even though no treaty established one.

Second, a state that has persistently and consistently objected to a rule from the very beginning of its
formation is not bound by it: the persistent objector rule. In Fisheries (United Kingdom v Norway), the
Court found the UK's claimed 10-mile rule for bays had not achieved customary status at all — but added
that, even if it had, Norway had always opposed applying it to the Norwegian coast and would not be bound by
it regardless.

Exam use: the persistent objector rule reconciles customary law with the principle that international law
is based on consent — a state can "opt out" of a rule under formation, but only by objecting before it
crystallises and maintaining that objection consistently, which is why it is rarely successfully invoked.
