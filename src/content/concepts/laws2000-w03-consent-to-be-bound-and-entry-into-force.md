Consent to be bound (arts 11–17) and entry into force (art 24) are distinct questions. Article 11 lists
several modes of consent — signature, exchange of instruments, ratification, acceptance, approval, accession
— but Australia mainly uses signature and ratification. Article 14: ratification is required if the treaty
says so, the negotiating parties agreed it was needed, the signing representative signed subject to
ratification, or their full powers (or the negotiation) showed that was the state's intention. Dualist states
like Australia usually need domestic transformation before ratifying, so ratification (not bare signature)
is the norm here, especially for multilateral treaties.

Entry into force happens "in such manner and upon such date as [the treaty] may provide or as the
negotiating States may agree" (art 24). UNCLOS shows the gap: opened for ratification in 1982 with roughly
100 signatures, but needing 12 months after the 60th ratification (art 306) — not reached until 1994.

In that gap, arts 18 and 25 differ. Article 18 obliges a state signed subject to ratification, or consented
pending entry into force, only to refrain from defeating the treaty's purpose — not comply with its details.
Article 25 lets a treaty (or part) apply provisionally, in full, pending entry into force, if the treaty or
parties so agree — a stronger, opt-in status.

Exam use: work out which mode of consent applies, whether the treaty is in force (art 24), and if not,
whether art 18's minimal duty or art 25's fuller provisional application is engaged.
