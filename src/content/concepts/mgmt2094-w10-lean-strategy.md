Collis (2016) argues that strategy and entrepreneurship need each other: 'Strategy without
entrepreneurship is central planning. Entrepreneurship without strategy leads to chaos.' His **lean strategy process**
integrates the bottom-up approach of the lean start-up with the top-down orientation of strategic management.

- **Vision** — the only part that should be in any sense permanent: the organisation's reason for existence.
- **Deliberate strategy** — agreed by senior executives from an analysis of strengths, weaknesses, opportunities,
  threats, resources and capabilities; it sets the **objective**, the **scope** (what the venture will and will not
  do) and the **competitive advantage**.
- **Emergent strategy** — managers at all levels make daily decisions and run experiments guided by the strategy;
  feedback and findings reshape it, and the process restarts.

Strategy is both a **screen** that novel ideas must pass and a **yardstick** for evaluating experiments, and it frames
how feedback is read: Instagram's founders noticed that Burbn users posted a lot of pictures and refocused on photo
sharing. The single best piece of advice: know what not to do.

Collis also marks the lean start-up's limits: choices in a resource-constrained venture are mutually exclusive, every
choice sets a unique path, decisions are interdependent, and simple market tests aren't always useful — some
innovations need long-term investment (Tesla's batteries), and quick tests can mistake ephemeral traction for lasting
demand (Groupon).

Exam use: argue for experiments inside strategic boundaries, not experiments instead of strategy.
