Hart identifies five meanings of 'positivism' in jurisprudence:

1. laws are commands of human beings;
2. there is no necessary connection between law and morals, or law as it is and ought to be;
3. the analysis of legal concepts is worth pursuing and distinct from historical and sociological inquiries and from
   the criticism/appraisal of law;
4. a legal system is a "closed logical system" in which correct decisions can be deduced from predetermined rules by
   logic alone;
5. moral judgments cannot be established or defended by rational argument, evidence, or proof ("noncognitivism").

Bentham and Austin held (1), (2) and (3), but not (4) and (5). Kelsen held (2), (3) and (5), but not (1) and (4).
Working out where Hart stands tests your understanding of his theory.

Three senses of legal positivism:

- **A general theory of law** — the social facts, sources and separability theses: descriptive and conceptual claims
  about the nature of law.
- **A methodology** — legal theory is a morally neutral, descriptive enterprise: a meta-theoretical claim about legal
  theory. Hart: "My account is descriptive in that it is morally neutral and has no justificatory aims" (CL 240).
- **A political theory of law** — moral criteria of validity are not morally desirable, though conceptually possible,
  so law and morality should be kept separate: a prescriptive separability thesis.

Exam use: Question 1 tests the methodological sense; say so, and do not answer with the first.
