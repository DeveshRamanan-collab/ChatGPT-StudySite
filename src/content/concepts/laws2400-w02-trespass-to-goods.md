Chattels are tangible, moveable, visible goods transferable by delivery — pets, livestock, vehicles, books,
computers, phones and artwork, but not fixtures permanently attached to land. There is no property in a
dead body, though stored sperm may be protected as a chattel; intangible property such as bitcoin, domain
names and digital files sits in doubt.

Trespass to goods requires a direct interference with a chattel that is in the possession of the plaintiff
at the time of the interference. Because the action is in trespass, no proof of actual damage is generally
required.

Possession is different from ownership, and is essential — not incidental — to this tort. Possession has
two elements: an intention to exercise control and exclude others, and actual control. A person who has
physical custody as a bailee has possession, even against the true owner in most respects; the bailor
cannot generally sue the bailee in trespass, though may sue where the bailee has completely destroyed the
goods (*Penfolds Wines Pty Ltd v Elliott*). Even a finder of a chattel has possession in law sufficient to
sue everyone except the true owner (*National Crime Authority v Flack*).

Exam use: the threshold question is always who had possession — not ownership — at the moment of
interference; a plaintiff with only a contractual or ownership right but no possession cannot sue in
trespass, though they may still be able to sue in conversion.
