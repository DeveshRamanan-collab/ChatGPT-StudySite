Joel Feinberg (1926–2004): "It is always a good reason in support of a proposed criminal prohibition that it would probably be an effective way of preventing serious offense...to persons other than the actor, and that it is probably a necessary means to that end... the prevention of offensive conduct is properly the state's business."

The offence principle is deemed a **supplement** to the harm principle. Mill's state exceeds its limits if it protects someone from being offended; Feinberg answers the criticism that harm alone does not reach far enough. Because offence has a greater reach than harm, he narrows it with two tests that must both be met:

1. **The standard of universality**: for the offensiveness to justify coercion, it should be a reaction that could be expected from almost anyone chosen at random (regardless of sex, age, race, etc).
2. **The standard of reasonable avoidability**: you can only expect a response from the state if you cannot reasonably avoid the behaviour, or if avoiding it would be inconvenient for you.

Like the harm principle, the offence principle rejects paternalism: it protects persons **other than the actor**.

Exam use: when a prohibition targets offensive but harmless conduct, apply both standards in turn; failing either one, the offence principle gives no reason to coerce.
