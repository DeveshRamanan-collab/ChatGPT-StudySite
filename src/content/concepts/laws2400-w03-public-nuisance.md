Nuisance is unreasonable interference with the plaintiff's use or enjoyment of land; unlike
trespass, the interference is with use/enjoyment (not direct physical entry) and need not be direct or
physical. Public nuisance is a common law crime, but a private individual may also bring a tort
action if they have suffered special or particular damage.

Public nuisance is an unlawful act or omission which (i) endangers the lives, safety, property or comfort
of the public, or (ii) obstructs the enjoyment of a right common to all. To sue, a plaintiff needs: title
to sue (special/particular damage, different from the damage suffered by the public generally);
interference with a public right (the general public must be affected — this is ordinarily the
community's/the state's responsibility to address); and interference that is substantial and
unreasonable (not every annoyance or temporary inconvenience qualifies).

*Walsh v Ervin* shows how "special or particular damage" can be proved: a farmer ploughed and planted
crops on the highway and erected gates blocking the plaintiff's vehicle access to his land. The court
held that special damage is not limited to pecuniary loss — substantial delay and inconvenience,
different in kind from what the rest of the public experienced, was enough to give the plaintiff title to
sue.

Exam use: obstructing a highway is the classic public nuisance fact pattern — check all three elements,
and use Walsh v Ervin to argue that the plaintiff's inconvenience was "substantial" and "dissimilar" to
the general public's, even without financial loss.
