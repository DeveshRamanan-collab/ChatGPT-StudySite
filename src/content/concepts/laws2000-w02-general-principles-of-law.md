Article 38(1)(c) directs the Court to "the general principles of law recognized by civilized nations".
International courts and tribunals rely on these principles when they cannot find authority in a treaty or
in customary international law — when a dispute cannot be effectively resolved on the basis of those two
sources. Examples given are equity, good faith, res judicata, and the impartiality of judges: principles
common to domestic legal systems generally, transposed to the international level.

To count as a general principle, a rule needs a decent geographical spread of states recognising it — drawn
from civil law, common law and other legal traditions, not just one family of domestic systems — and it
needs to be capable of being usefully implemented and followed at the international level; not everything
recognised domestically transposes.

Equity is the clearest working example. It refers to what is fair and reasonable in the administration of
justice, and the ICJ often refers to equity and equitable principles when delimiting maritime boundaries
between coastal states with opposite or adjacent coasts, where a mechanical application of a treaty or
customary rule would produce an unfair result.

Exam use: general principles are a last resort, not a first port of call — reach for them only once you
have checked, and ruled out, both an applicable treaty and an applicable rule of customary international
law.
