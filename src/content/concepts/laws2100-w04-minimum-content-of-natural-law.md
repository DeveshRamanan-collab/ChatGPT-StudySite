According to Hart, rules restricting the use of violence and requiring respect for property and promises
constitute a 'minimum content' of natural law underlying both positive law and social morality.

The simple truisms "not only disclose the core of good sense in the doctrine of Natural Law"; given the setting of
natural facts and aims, which make sanctions both possible and necessary in a municipal system, "this is a natural
necessity", and the minimum forms of protection for persons, property and promises are "similarly indispensable
features of municipal law. It is in this form that we should reply to the positivist thesis that 'law may have any
content'" (CL 199).

This "empirical version" of natural law is based on Hobbes (*Leviathan*, chs 14–15) and Hume (*Treatise*, Book III,
part 2).

- It is a **natural** necessity in the world we live in, not a conceptual (necessary) connection between law and
  morality — just as coercion is not, for Hart, a necessary feature of law.
- Even the most cruel dictatorship needs some members to buy into (accept) its rules voluntarily; unless the rules
  protect at least them, it would be a suicide pact.
- The 'positivist thesis' Hart pushes against is Kelsen's: for Kelsen the content of individual laws is irrelevant;
  only the chain of validity matters.
- Finnis said Hart should have called it the minimum content of *positive* law.

Exam use: shows Hart conceding a grain of truth to natural law without conceding the NCLM.
