At a minimum, self-determination "entails the entitlement of peoples to have control over their destiny and to
be treated respectfully" (Australian Government). UN Charter art 1(2) refers to the "principle of equal rights
and self-determination of peoples" without framing it as a right; ICCPR art 1 enshrines it as a human right:
"All peoples have the right of self-determination."

In the decolonisation context it is generally accepted. The 1960 Declaration on the Granting of Independence
called alien subjugation "a denial of fundamental human rights", and in *Chagos* (2019) the ICJ affirmed the
Chagossians' right and the UK's obligation to complete the decolonisation of Mauritius.

Outside decolonisation the right has internal and external dimensions, and a right to unilaterally secede is
highly contentious. Kosovo's 2008 secession from Serbia was argued to be legitimate as a response to systematic
discrimination and oppression; opponents said it violated Serbia's sovereignty and territorial integrity. In
*Re Secession of Quebec* (1998) the Supreme Court of Canada held unilateral secession inconsistent with
international law and the Canadian Constitution: Quebec's internal self-determination was fulfilled, so there
was no ground for external self-determination.

Exam use: classify the claim (colonial or not; internal or external), then ask whether the people's internal
self-determination is being denied.
