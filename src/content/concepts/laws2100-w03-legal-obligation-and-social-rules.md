Hart's fresh start begins from what the coercive-orders theory got right: "where there is law, there human conduct
is made in some sense non-optional or obligatory" (CL 82). But "[t]here is a difference, yet to be explained, between
the assertion that someone was obliged to do something and the assertion that he had an obligation to do it" (CL 82).
Factually, the gunman and the tax officer look alike; yet a tax evader is still under an obligation.

The key idea is the social rule. Habits and rules share a convergence of behaviour, but for rules (1) deviations are met
with criticisms, (2) the rule is regarded as a good reason for making such criticism, and (3) rules have an internal
aspect — "a critical reflective attitude to certain patterns of behaviour as a common standard" (CL 57). "Habits are
not 'normative': they cannot confer rights or authority on anyone" (CL 60).

From the external point of view you only register patterns of behaviour. The internal point of view is the
participants' — those who buy into the rules — and you can understand it while only watching. It is a critical
reflective attitude, not feelings of compulsion (Hart's criticism of Scandinavian realism) nor a prediction of
consequences. Rules give reasons to criticise others and to act — an idea Raz develops as reasons for action.

Exam use: obliged versus having an obligation is the problem the theory explains.
