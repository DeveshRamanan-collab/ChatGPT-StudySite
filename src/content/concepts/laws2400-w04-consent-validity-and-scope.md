Consent may be express or implied, but to be valid it must be voluntary/free, given by a competent
person, and relate to the specific act complained of.

Voluntariness requires that the person had sufficient knowledge of what she was consenting to, and that
there was no force, fraud, threat or intimidation. Not every misrepresentation destroys consent: if the
plaintiff understands the "nature and character" of the interference, consent remains valid even where a
false inducement was used — for example, a false promise of marriage used to induce sexual relations.

Consent is also specific to the act it covers, and conduct exceeding what was consented to is not
protected. In *McNamara v Duncan*, the defendant deliberately struck the plaintiff on the head during an
Australian Rules match, fracturing his skull; the court rejected the argument that playing the game meant
consenting to that blow, since forcible contact within the rules is one thing, deliberately inflicted
injury another. In *Dean v Phung*, a dentist performed over 53 consultations of unnecessary treatment
costing $73,640; the court found the patient's consent was not validly given, because it did not extend
to treatment of that scale.

Exam use: check voluntariness first (force, fraud or threat vitiate; incidental misrepresentation about a
collateral matter does not), then ask whether the defendant's conduct stayed within the scope of what was
actually agreed to.
