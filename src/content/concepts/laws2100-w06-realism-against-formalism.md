Legal realism is an umbrella term: American (Holmes, Pound, Oliphant, Frank, Llewellyn, Cohen) and Scandinavian
(Hagerstrom, Lundstedt, Olivecrona, Ross) families share a set of claims rather than one theory.

The target was **formalism**, the received view in US law schools:

- law as a self-contained system of rules;
- judges decide by deductive/syllogistic reasoning, applying general rules to the facts ('mechanical jurisprudence');
- legal rules are like mathematical propositions that mechanically generate the result in all cases;
- judicial reasoning is independent from moral, political, economic and social considerations.

Is this what judges really do? Holmes: "The life of the law has not been logic; it has been experience" (*The Common
Law*, 1881). Law is a product of historical experience, social needs and public policy.

Main realist claims:

1. The traditional idea of law as a system of rules is a myth.
2. Law in books differs from law in action (paper rules vs real rules).
3. The law is indeterminate; general rules cannot decide concrete cases.
4. Judges (should) decide by making policy decisions.
5. Legal concepts and standards are not neutral/objective.
6. Rejection of "metaphysical nonsense".
7. Legal institutions can (and should) be studied with empirical sciences: sociological jurisprudence?

Exam use: realism is a family of claims; say which claim you are attacking or defending, and whether it is descriptive
(judges do) or normative (judges should).
