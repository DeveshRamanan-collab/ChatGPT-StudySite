Lecture VI asks how we distinguish positive laws from other kinds of commands. "Every positive law is set by
a sovereign person, or a sovereign body of persons, to a member or members of the independent political society
wherein that person or body is sovereign or supreme" (PJD 165). Three inseparably connected ideas: sovereignty,
subjection, independent political society.

A society is political iff the generality of its members is in a habit of obedience to a determinate and common
superior; political and independent iff that superior does not habitually obey another person or body. Without
habitual obedience by the bulk of its members to one and the same superior, a society is in a state of nature or
split into two or more independent political societies. No territory is needed: the key is the power relation, so the
model fits a city state, an absolute monarchy or a constitutional democracy.

Austin's positivism: "The existence of law is one thing; its merit or demerit is another" (Lecture V) — law as it is,
not law as it ought to be, a descriptive claim. And: "I shall present them abstracted from every particular system",
illustrated from "the Roman Law, and the Law of England" (Lectures, XI) — a claim to generality. The same two ideas
reappear in Hart's general and descriptive theory.

Exam use: the Lecture XI passage shows a 'general' theory illustrated from two particular systems — ask at what level
of generality a theory can work.
