The position on custom is less settled than for treaties. Two doctrines compete. Transformation: customary
international law only becomes part of Australian law when formally adopted by Parliament or the courts as part
of the common law. Incorporation: customary international law is automatically a source of Australian law,
unless there is a rule to the contrary. Australia has not clearly adopted either — both have been recognised in
High Court decisions — but either way a customary rule cannot generally be adopted where inconsistent with
domestic legislation or essential common law doctrine.

*Nulyarimma v Thompson*: Aboriginal applicants argued that genocide, as customary international law, could be
tried in Australian courts without legislation. The Federal Court held that even if genocide is part of
customary international law — and even accepting its prohibition is jus cogens — it does not form part of
Australian law without legislation declaring it a crime — criminal norms are different (Wilcox J). Merkel
J, dissenting, set criteria: general acceptance by the community of nations; reception, so not inconsistent with
statute or declared common law; once received, a true declaration of the common law, as from the date the rule
was established.

International law is a legitimate influence on the common law — it may "march in step" (Brennan J, *Mabo (No
2)*) — but not "a back door means of importing an unincorporated convention" (Mason CJ and Deane J, *Teoh*).

Exam use: a criminal customary norm needs legislation first.
