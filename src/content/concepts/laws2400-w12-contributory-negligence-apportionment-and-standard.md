Contributory negligence is a finding about the plaintiff's own lack of care. It results in a *partial
reduction* in the damages otherwise recoverable, based on what is "just and equitable having regard to [their] share
in the responsibility for the damage" — Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1965 (NSW) s 9(1) and its
equivalents in other states and territories. On its meaning, the flowchart refers to *Davies v Swan Motor Co
(Swansea) Ltd* and *Jones v Livox Quarries Ltd*.

Three of the flowchart's general principles frame the core inquiry:

- **Relevant standard of care.** The principles for deciding whether a person has been negligent also decide whether
  the person who suffered harm was contributorily negligent in failing to take precautions against the risk of that
  harm (CLA s 5R(1)): the standard of a reasonable person in the position of that person, judged on what that person
  knew or ought to have known at the time (s 5R(2)).
- **Causal requirement.** A causal link between the plaintiff's lack of care for their own safety and their injury
  must be established (*Caswell v Powell Duffryn Associated Collieries*); ordinary principles of causation apply.
- **What is just and equitable?** Examine the whole conduct of each of the negligent parties and consider the
  relative blameworthiness of each (*Pennington v Norris*).

Exam use: ask whether the plaintiff fell below the s 5R standard, whether that failure caused the injury, and then
argue the just and equitable reduction from the whole conduct of both parties.
