To decide whether a duty of care has been breached, the court measures the defendant's conduct against a
standard of care, on the balance of probabilities. There are no degrees of carelessness: the defendant is liable
whether her conduct was marginally careless or completely reckless.

The duty is only a duty to take **reasonable** care, not an absolute duty to avoid injury: the measure is
reasonableness, not elimination of risk (*Swain v Waverley Municipal Council* (2005) 220 CLR 517, 520 [5]). An
obligation to exercise reasonable care must be contrasted with an obligation to prevent harm (*Dederer*, Gummow J).

Courts apply the test of a **reasonable person**, an objective standard. A defendant may therefore be in breach even
though they did their personal best, if their personal best was not reasonable.

The classic statement is Alderson B's in *Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co* (1856): negligence is the omission to do
something which a reasonable man would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do. So
negligence can lie in an omission or in a positive act. (*Donoghue v Stevenson* later established negligence as a
separate cause of action.)

Exam use: never argue that the defendant "tried their hardest" or that "the risk was not eliminated" — compare the
conduct with what a reasonable person would have done.
