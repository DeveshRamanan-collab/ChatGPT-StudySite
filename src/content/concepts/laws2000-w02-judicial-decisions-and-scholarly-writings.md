As a matter of doctrine, judicial decisions are not, in and of themselves, binding international law.
Article 59 ICJ Statute: "The decision of the Court has no binding force except between the parties and in
respect of that particular case." Unlike the common law's doctrine of precedent, a decision the Court
reaches in one case is not binding in a later case, even between the same parties on similar facts — this
respects the consent-based nature of the Court's jurisdiction, which is not compulsory.

Despite that, judicial decisions carry substantial interpretative weight: international judges and legal
practitioners constantly refer to, and base their reasoning on, previous decisions, and the ICJ rarely
departs from its own jurisprudence. Consistency serves legitimacy (inconsistent jurisprudence would
undermine the international adjudicatory system), predictability (states would be reluctant to litigate if
outcomes were unpredictable), and the peaceful settlement of disputes — states also invoke ICJ decisions in
negotiating settlements outside formal litigation.

Scholarly writings — Article 38(1)(d)'s "teachings of the most highly qualified publicists" — and the work
of the International Law Commission serve the same subsidiary, interpretive function: they help clarify the
law where it is ambiguous but do not themselves create it. The ILC's draft articles (e.g. on state
responsibility) can, over time, become treaties.

Exam use: cite ICJ decisions and scholarly commentary as persuasive support for how a primary source should
be interpreted, never as a freestanding source of the obligation itself.
