'Immunities' are instances where a state's adjudicatory and enforcement jurisdiction cannot be exercised as
normal because another foreign state (or its representative) is involved. State immunity is grounded in
sovereign equality: states are juridically equal, so no state may exercise jurisdiction over another without
consent.

Immunity from adjudication means a state cannot be sued before a foreign court unless it consents; immunity from
enforcement means a judgment cannot be enforced upon the state (e.g. by confiscating assets). Prescriptive
jurisdiction is untouched. Immunity is a procedural barrier, decided first, and says nothing about the
substantive lawfulness of the conduct.

The source is customary international law. The UN Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and their
Property (2004) needs 30 ratifications and has 25, so it is not in force, but its articles mostly reflect custom:
art 5 (a state enjoys immunity for itself and its property), art 6 (courts refrain from exercising jurisdiction),
and no immunity where the state expressly consents (art 7), participates in proceedings (art 8) or faces a
counterclaim (art 9). Once waived, immunity cannot be reclaimed when judgment goes against the state.

"State" (art 2(1)(b)) covers organs of government, federal units and political subdivisions and agencies
entitled to exercise sovereign authority, and representatives acting in that capacity. The UK State Immunity Act
1978 treats immunity as a preliminary matter with a presumption of immunity; Australia's Foreign States
Immunities Act 1985 is similar.

Exam use: say the convention is not in force and you are applying custom.
