States: in Jurisdictional Immunities of the State (Germany v Italy) [2012] the ICJ held that custom did not
deprive Germany of immunity in Italian civil proceedings over WWII crimes by its armed forces. State immunity is
"procedural in character" and "entirely distinct from the substantive law" (para 93), and extends to acts jure
imperii by armed forces in armed conflict, even on the forum state's territory (para 77).

Officials: in Arrest Warrant [2002] a Belgian warrant for the sitting Congolese foreign minister breached his
personal immunity; state practice supports no exception for international crimes. But "immunity … does not
mean … impunity": immunity is procedural, criminal responsibility substantive. Prosecution remains possible (1)
in the person's own state, (2) after waiver, (3) by other states once they leave office, for acts before or after
office and private acts during it, and (4) before international criminal courts.

Former officials: in Pinochet (No 3) (1999) the House of Lords held that a former head of state has no immunity
for acts that, when done, were international crimes subject to a form of universal jurisdiction — torture under
the Convention against Torture, to which the UK, Chile and Spain were parties. ILC draft article 7 (2017) would
exclude immunity for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, torture and enforced disappearances, but
whether it reflects state practice is debated.
