You cannot think about "precautions against a risk of harm" (s 5B(1)) until you know what the risk of harm is. Garling J
in *Benic v NSW* [2010] NSWSC 1039 laid down steps for the breach question under the Act:

1. **Identify a risk of harm.** It is sufficient "if the risk of harm is described as a class of injury, as distinct
   from the particular injury actually suffered by the plaintiff".
2. **Consider the three elements** in s 5B(1) — foreseeability, probability and reasonableness of precautions — each
   separately addressed.

*Roads and Traffic Authority of NSW v Dederer* (2007) 234 CLR 330 shows why step 1 matters. A 14-year-old dived off a
bridge into an estuary with a shifting sandbar and suffered partial paraplegia. The Court of Appeal described the risk as a "serious spinal injury flowing from the act of diving off the bridge". Gummow J
said that characterisation "obscured the true source of potential injury": impact on jumping into potentially shallow
water and shifting sands [60]. The High Court (3:2) found no breach: the existing signs were a reasonable response.

The rule: be specific enough that the risk corresponds to the type of precautions that should have been taken. You
cannot formulate the risk so broadly that it encompasses fundamentally different scenarios requiring fundamentally
different responses.

Exam use: open every breach answer with one sentence naming the risk of harm and its source on the facts.
