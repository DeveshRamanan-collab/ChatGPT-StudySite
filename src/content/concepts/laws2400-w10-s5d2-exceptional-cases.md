Sometimes negligence cannot be shown to be a necessary condition of the harm — eg where science cannot identify the cause. Section 5D(2): "In determining in an exceptional case, in accordance with established principles, whether negligence that cannot be established as a necessary condition of the occurrence of harm should be accepted as establishing factual causation, the court is to consider (amongst other relevant things) whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed on the negligent party."

The Ipp Report recommended an exceptional case provision, based on the dust disease cases; s 5D(2) is the result. It has rarely been considered, so how it operates is uncertain. *Adeels Palace v Moubarak* held it did not apply where negligence only 'might have' prevented the harm. And s 3B(1) excludes the CLA from dust disease and tobacco claims, which go under special statutes such as the Dust Diseases Tribunal Act 1989 (NSW).

The flowchart says "an appropriate case" (NSW text: "exceptional") and adds:
1. the case is one of multiple sufficient causes or material contribution to harm;
2. there must also be a clear breach and an evidentiary gap;
3. the court considers whether and why responsibility should be imposed, and whether and why the harm should be left to lie where it fell. Verify: that last limb is not in the NSW text.

Exam use: reach for s 5D(2) only when necessary condition fails; say it is rare and uncertain; cite *Adeels Palace*; check s 3B(1) first.
