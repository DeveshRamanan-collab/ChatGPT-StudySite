The United Nations is the central institutional regime of international relations, formally established to
prevent instability and unchecked aggression in inter-state affairs, which had featured prominently in the first half
of the 20th century. Its first purpose is "to maintain international peace and security", including through "effective collective
measures for the prevention and removal of threats to the peace" (art 1(1)).

Collective security is the creation of a system where war and aggression are the concern of all states, not a private
dispute between states: war against one is war against all. It is an idea from international relations more than a
legal term: threats are dealt with collectively, through an organisation, rather than state by state. The League of
Nations was the first attempt and the UN the second; NATO is another example, but it stays bound by the Charter's
rules on force.

The UN system centralises the permissible use of force in one body: the Security Council. Its tools run from peaceful
settlement (Chapter VI, art 33), through collective security without force (Chapter VII, art 41: sanctions), to
collective security with force (Chapter VII, art 42: authorising member states, regional organisations or
peacekeeping operations).

Without a standing UN army, the Council relies on member states, and has not acted in every threat to the peace —
through lack of capacity, but also the P-5 veto.
