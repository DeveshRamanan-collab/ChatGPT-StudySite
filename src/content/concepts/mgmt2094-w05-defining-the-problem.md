Markman (2017) argues that stories of invention focus on the novel solution — Dyson's bagless vacuum,
Picasso and Braque's cubism, Xerox PARC's spatial desktop — which makes creativity seem a mystery. In fact most
creative solutions are found inside the collective memory of the people working on the problem: someone knows
something that will help; they just haven't realised it yet.

Memory works by cues: think of a birthday party and hats, cake and singing come to mind; switch to salad and you
recall lettuce, tomatoes and dressing. **In creative problem solving, the statement of the problem is the cue to
memory.** Change the description and new information is drawn out.

Dyson: thinking about vacuum bags reminds you of bags (hence the patent numbers on most bags). Describe the problem as
"separate the dirt from the air" and the bag drops out — and industrial cyclones, which spin air to throw particles to
the edges, become one answer. Generalising removes the specific components usually used to solve the problem.

There is no ideal problem statement. The most consistently creative people and groups find many descriptions: some
specific (the objects acted on), retrieving closely related knowledge; others abstract (relationships, or the goal),
retrieving more distantly related knowledge. Tapping memory is more cost-effective and repeatable than luck
(Archimedes in the bath) or huge research budgets.

Not "think outside the box", but find more descriptions of the box.

Exam use: rewrite a business problem at two levels of abstraction.
