Public authorities are bodies created by statutes to perform public functions or act in the public interest — the
State, councils, statutory authorities, the police. With no Crown immunity now, they can be sued in negligence. But they differ from private defendants because of their nature and responsibilities, because they
exercise functions under statute, and because courts weigh public policy — above all resources (a court telling the
executive where to spend limited funds raises the separation of powers) and indeterminate liability. Reasonable
foreseeability is always needed, never enough.

Until *Anns* (1978), UK authorities were not liable for failing to exercise a power. In *Sutherland Shire Council v
Heyman* (1985) the High Court considered the **policy versus operation** distinction and **general reliance**. Policy
— the high-level approach, such as letting an industry self-regulate — is pretty much off limits. Negligence in
operations, carrying policy out, is more arguable, though hurdles remain.

Police show the same line: no duty is owed to individual members of the public in the investigation and suppression
of crime (*Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire*): conflicting responsibilities, resource allocation, the risk of
defensive policing. But this is not an immunity: negligent operational conduct can attract a duty (*Zalewski v
Turcarolo*, a police shooting during an operational response). And a statutory power to intervene is not of itself a
duty to exercise it (*Stuart v Kirkland-Veenstra*).

Exam use: say why more than foreseeability is needed, then place the complaint on the policy–operations line.
