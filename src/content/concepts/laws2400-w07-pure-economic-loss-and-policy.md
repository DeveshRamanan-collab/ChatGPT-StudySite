Pure economic loss (PEL) is economic loss which does not result from any physical injury to the person or
property. Consequential economic loss — wages lost after an injury, medical expenses, home modifications — has never been a
problem: reasonable foreseeability is typically enough. PEL is different: investment losses from negligent
financial advice, profits lost when a negligently cut cable shuts a business, or property value lost through an
architect's defective advice. Change the facts so the cut also short-circuits the fridge and spoils the stock,
and there is property damage — the losses become consequential.

The distinction is critical because PEL was not recoverable until recently, and even now is recoverable only in
certain situations if additional conditions are satisfied. Three policy considerations explain the reluctance:

- **Indeterminate liability** — liability with no boundaries, such as a tip on radio or a podcast relied on by
  an undefinable audience. A large class is not the problem; an undefinable one is.
- **Relationship with the law of contract** — parties who have negotiated terms should not have extra
  obligations imposed by a duty of care.
- **Loss linked to a lawful commercial activity** — a new supermarket that takes a rival's profits causes PEL,
  but no duty arises.

CLA s 5 defines "harm" to include economic loss, so PEL is still harm for the negligence Part.

Exam use: classify the loss first — consequential loss rides on Donoghue v Stevenson foreseeability; PEL needs
the salient features.
