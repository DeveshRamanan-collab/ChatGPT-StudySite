The textbook flowchart (Tort Law Principles 3e, p 142) defines trespass to land as the direct, intentional or
negligent invasion or interference with land in the possession of the plaintiff without lawful authority,
justification or excuse (*Barker v The Queen*; *Roy v O'Neill*). Its general principles run as gates, in order:

1. **Exclusive possession** ("title to sue") at the time of the interference — by title, by consent under a lease, or
   by adverse possession. Licences typically do not confer it; an owner whose tenant stays on after the lease ends
   must retake possession to sue.
2. **Of land** — including airspace, fixtures and subsoil.
3. **Direct invasion or interference** — including exceeding the terms of a licence, or going beyond its expiry or
   revocation. Indirect interference may be another tort, such as private nuisance.
4. **Fault** — the act must be intentional or negligent. The defendant need not intend to trespass; mistake is
   generally no defence; involuntary conduct, utterly without fault, is not actionable.
5. **Without lawful authority, justification or excuse.**

No damage need be proved: trespass is actionable per se. Substantive damages cover actual losses that are the natural
and probable consequences of the trespass; alternatively, damages may be assessed on the "user fee" principle. An
injunction may, in the court's discretion, restrain a continuing or threatened trespass where damages would be
inadequate.

Exam use: walk the five gates in order and stop at the first the facts fail; an indirect interference points to
private nuisance.
