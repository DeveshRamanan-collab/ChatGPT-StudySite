Customary international law arises when conduct is (a) followed as a general practice among states —
consistent repetition of a particular behaviour over a period of time when confronted with the same facts
(the objective element) — and (b) accepted by those states as legally binding, opinio juris sive
necessitatis: a belief of legal obligation, not mere courtesy, convenience or tradition (the subjective
element).

Neither element needs to be perfect. In Nicaragua v United States of America the ICJ held that state
practice need not be "in absolutely rigorous conformity with the rule"; it is enough that conduct is "in
general" consistent with the rule, and that departures are generally treated as breaches, not as a new rule
— a state that defends its conduct by invoking an exception within the rule actually confirms rather than
weakens it. In North Sea Continental Shelf the Court required that state practice, "including that of
States whose interests are specially affected", be "extensive and virtually uniform", even over a short
period; and opinio juris requires evidence that a settled practice is felt as a legal obligation, not merely
"ceremonial and protocol" courtesy. The Asylum Case (Colombia v Peru) shows practice failing this test: "so
much uncertainty and contradiction... fluctuation and discrepancy" meant no "constant and uniform usage,
accepted as law" existed.

Exam use: always run both elements separately — a widespread practice with no belief of legal obligation is
not custom, and a stated belief with inconsistent practice is not custom either.
