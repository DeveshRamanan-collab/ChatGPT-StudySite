Rights are grouped into three generations:

- **First — civil and political rights:** e.g. the right to life, freedom from torture, freedom of expression, the
  right to a fair trial, political participation rights.
- **Second — economic, social and cultural rights:** e.g. the rights to education, health, an adequate standard of
  living and work, and cultural rights.
- **Third — collective rights:** e.g. self-determination; potentially the right to development and the right to a
  healthy environment.

**Universality:** rights apply to all humans everywhere. Cultural relativists answer that different societies have
different values and conceptions of rights; that Western individualism does not reflect all worldviews, so universal
rights represent cultural imperialism (IHRL is ethnocentric); and that rights must be understood in context.
Universalists reply that basic rights like life and freedom from torture cannot depend on culture; that cultural
relativism often masks oppression, particularly of women and minorities; and that culture is not static — it changes
and evolves.

**Indivisibility and interdependence:** all rights are interconnected and equally important; they have equal status and
cannot be positioned in a hierarchical order. Denial of one right invariably impedes the enjoyment of others — the right
to an adequate standard of living cannot be compromised at the expense of the rights to health or education — and
benefiting from one right may depend on the fulfilment of others.

Exam use: the generations are categories, not a ranking — indivisibility means no right sits above another.
