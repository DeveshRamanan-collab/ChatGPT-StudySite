Vicarious liability makes one person or entity liable for a tort committed by someone else — typically an
employer for a tort committed by an employee acting within the course of their employment. The detailed
legal tests are covered later (Topic 12), but the concept is introduced now because it recurs throughout the
semester and connects directly to the theories of tort law's role in society.

Professor Fleming's summary (quoted from Sappideen, Vines and Eldridge at [13.45]) gives the policy reasons:
an employer who engages others to advance its own economic interests should fairly bear a corresponding
liability for losses in the course of that enterprise; the employer is a more promising source of recompense
than an employee, often "a man of straw without insurance"; and the rule spreads tort losses widely, since
the employer can pass them on through liability insurance and higher prices. Vicarious liability also has an
admonitory (warning) value in accident prevention: deterrent pressure works best on larger units such as
employers, who can organise and supervise staff to reduce accidents, and because employees are rarely worth
suing, holding the employer liable gives it an incentive to discipline wrongdoing staff.

For the exam: vicarious liability is about who pays, not whether a tort was committed — the employee must
still have committed a tort before the question of the employer's liability arises.
