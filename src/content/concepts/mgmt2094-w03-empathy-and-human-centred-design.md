Twenty years ago design shifted from an objective perspective, where designers worked from their own assumptions, to
a human-centred one that puts users at the heart of the process. Empathy is one of the 'mindsets' of human-centred
design (HCD), championed by IDEO. HCD works on the premise that a deep understanding of users' **unarticulated needs**
(through empathy) inspires creativity and innovation; empathetic design observes users in their environments — closer
to anthropology than to market science.

Two kinds of empathy: **affective** (a shared, mirrored emotion: you see someone sad and feel sad) and **cognitive**
(perspective taking: stepping into their world, worldview, beliefs and fears). Temple Grandin noticed that cattle
reacted badly to being led in a straight line and designed curved corrals that cut stress, panic and injury.

Four **roadblocks to empathy**: idealism (seeing things as we expect them to be), egoism (as we want them to be),
realism (thinking we see things as they are) and circumstantialism (thinking only about what we see). True empathy sets
aside personal beliefs and seeks further evidence without judgement.

How close to get is debated. IDEO goes into the customer's world; Roberto Verganti warns against engaging the customer
too much, because most customers are not trained to see far-future possibilities (Steve Jobs: until you show them, they
don't know; Henry Ford: they would have asked for faster horses).

Exam use: name the kind of empathy and the roadblock in a scenario, and say how to get past it.
