**Jus ad bellum** is the law relating to the resort to force and war — when a state is allowed to go to war and
use force. **Jus in bello** is the law regulating behaviour during hostilities — what is permitted once you are "in
war". This unit studies jus ad bellum; jus in bello is international humanitarian law (the law of armed conflict).

Thomas Aquinas (13th century) set three conditions for a just war:

- **Auctoritas** — the right authority (the rightful sovereign);
- **Causa justa** — a just purpose (not the pursuit of wealth or power);
- **Recta intentio** — the right intent (the attainment of a just peace).

Later efforts to limit the right to war each failed to prevent the next war:

- the **Hague Conventions** of 1899 and 1907 — then the First World War;
- the **League of Nations** (1920–1945), the first international organisation created to limit the recourse to war;
- the **Kellogg-Briand Pact** (1928), outlawing war as a means of national policy except for self-defence — then the
  Second World War.

The **UN Charter** (1945) reflects the experience of the Second World War: its Preamble declares the peoples of the
United Nations "determined to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war".

Exam use: jus ad bellum asks *whether* force may be used at all; jus in bello asks *how* it may be used once
hostilities have begun. Art 2(4) and art 51 are rules of jus ad bellum.
