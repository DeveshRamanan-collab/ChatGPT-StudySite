After ideation the team has been in its own head; it needs to get back to customers quickly to find out whether they
are as excited about the ideas — and the way to do that is a simple prototype, tested with them. The guest lecture's
advice: think with your hands — build something, try something, then talk about it, not the reverse. Design thinking
"probably should be called design doing".

Why: fail early, when failure is cheap — like an athlete trying different routines in the years before the Olympics,
not at the Games. Building something costly and launching it means the money is spent before you learn it fails.

A prototype can be anything: scrap materials, a mock-up, a storyboard. Early on, while uncertainty is high, the
question is not "can we make it?" but "if we made it, would it matter to the customer?". As it becomes clearer that
people want it, more finished and more expensive prototypes are justified, and testing turns to feasibility and
viability. Each round: get feedback, iterate, take it back. If a test fails, the idea may be killed, or **pivoted** —
one key element (the product, the customer group, the channel) changed.

The reading agrees: create multiple drafts and working prototypes and seek feedback from end users (step 4); and the
tangibility rule says prototypes are communication media.

Exam use: match the prototype to the uncertainty, and say what the test is meant to learn.
