Human rights have a long pre-history in domestic instruments: Magna Carta (1215), the English Bill of Rights
(1689), the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789), and the US Constitution (1789) with
its Bill of Rights (1791).

The Enlightenment supplied the philosophy. John Locke, in the *Two Treatises of Government* (1689), defended the
claim that people are by nature free and equal, and argued that rights such as life, liberty and property have a foundation independent of the laws of any
particular society. Immanuel Kant, in the *Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), held that all persons
have an inherent value, or dignity: human beings must be treated "as an end" and "never merely as a means".

International protection began in the 19th and early 20th centuries but was issue-specific: the abolition of slavery
(Britain in 1807, then other European countries, and the 1926 Slavery Convention) and limited women's rights
(trafficking in women for prostitution). The Second World War and the Nazi regime followed.

The UN Charter made human rights a general concern. Promoting respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms "for
all without distinction as to race, sex, language, or religion" is a purpose of the UN (art 1(3)); the UN shall
promote their universal respect and observance (art 55(c)); and all Members pledge joint and separate action to
achieve this (art 56).

Exam use: cite arts 1(3), 55(c) and 56 for where human rights enter the UN system.
