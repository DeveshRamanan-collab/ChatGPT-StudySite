Three working definitions of public international law recur in this unit. The Australian
Attorney-General's Department's Office of International Law defines it as "rules and principles
which facilitate the conduct of states and international organisations in their relations with one
another and, in some cases, with individuals, groups and transnational companies" — a functional
definition centred on who international law is for. The Practitioner's Guide to International Law
stresses its voluntary, consent-based nature: international law is "universally applicable and
authoritative for all States", which "recognise international law as 'law' by voluntarily consenting
and adhering to agreed standards." The Permanent Court of International Justice, in S.S. Lotus, gave
the classic statement of where international law's binding force comes from: rules "emanate from
[states'] own free will as expressed in conventions or by usages generally accepted as expressing
principles of law", and, crucially, "restrictions upon the independence of States cannot therefore be
presumed" — states are free to act unless a rule actually binds them, not the reverse.

Each definition insists on a different aspect: subjects and addressees (the AG's), voluntariness (the
Practitioner's Guide), or the ultimate source of obligation (Lotus). Together they give an exam
answer its two moves: identify who the rule addresses, and identify the convention or usage that
supplies its binding force.

Exam use: when a problem asks whether something is really international law, reach for Lotus first --
it supplies a test (consent, expressed in convention or usage), not just a description.
