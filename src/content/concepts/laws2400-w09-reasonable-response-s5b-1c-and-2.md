The second breach question is whether the defendant's **response** to the risk was reasonable — the standard of
care implicit in s 5B(1)(c): a person is not negligent unless, in the circumstances, a reasonable person in the person's
position would have taken those precautions.

Section 5B(2) codifies the "calculus of negligence": the court is to consider (amongst other relevant things) —

- (a) **probability** that the harm would occur if care were not taken — judged prospectively; low in *RTA v Dederer*,
  where warning signs were a reasonable response;
- (b) likely **seriousness** of the harm — the standard rises with seriousness and known vulnerability (*Paris v Stepney
  Borough Council*: goggles for a one-eyed worker); intoxication does not affect it (s 49(1));
- (c) the **burden** of precautions — cost and practical matters such as availability and convenience (*Woods v
  Multi-Sport Holdings*: no indoor-cricket helmets);
- (d) the **social utility** of the activity (*E v Australian Red Cross Society*: testing would have cost 5% of blood
  donations).

The factors are weighed together, not ticked off (*Romeo*; *Refrigerated Roadways*). Section 5C adds: the burden includes similar risks; that
a risk could have been avoided another way, or later action, does not of itself establish liability. Obvious and
inherent risks (ss 5F–5I, 5L) also shape the response.

Exam use: name the precaution the plaintiff says should have been taken, weigh (a)–(d) on the facts with a case for
each, then conclude on (c).
