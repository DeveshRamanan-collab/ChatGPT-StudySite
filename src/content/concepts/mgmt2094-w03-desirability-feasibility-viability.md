The sweet spot for innovation is where an idea is all three at once:

- **Desirable** — people (customers, consumers or users) want it.
- **Feasible** — we can make it, technically and organisationally.
- **Viable** — we can make money from it; for government or a not-for-profit, it has a big impact for citizens.

The same three lenses sit inside Tim Brown's definition in the reading: matching people's needs with what is
technologically feasible and what a viable business can convert into consumer value and market opportunity.

The guest lecture's warning is about order. Too often a team starts with feasibility ("how cool is the thing we can
make?"), writes a business case on made-up numbers — there is no data yet for an innovation that lies in the future —
and launches, so the first time the customer sees the idea is at launch, after possibly millions have been spent.
Design thinking starts with desirability, because the number one reason ideas fail is that no one wants them; then it
tests feasibility and viability, with more finished prototypes and tools such as the business model canvas.

The lecture's contrasting pair (see the example card): one product began with what its inventor could make, was kept
from customers until launch and flopped; another began with customers' frustrations and ran thousands of experiments
to make a solution feasible and viable.

Exam use: test the idea against all three lenses and say which one is unproven.
