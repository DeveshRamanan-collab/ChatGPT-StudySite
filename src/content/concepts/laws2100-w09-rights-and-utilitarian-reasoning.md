Human rights have priority over the "general interest". Do they work as "trumps" or "shields"? (The lecture attributes trumps to Dworkin and shields to Schauer.) Either way, rights disrupt a certain kind of reasoning: a true commitment to human rights is incompatible with a certain type of utilitarian reasoning.

**Act-utilitarianism** judges each action by its consequences under the principle of utility (the greatest happiness for the greatest number): utilitarianism is a form of consequentialism. **Deontological** reasoning is, crudely, the opposite: it looks to the principles behind an action, not its consequences.

The lecture's test cases:

- Ticking bomb: an act-utilitarian may justify torturing a suspect to save hundreds of lives; someone truly committed to human rights says it is not acceptable.
- Minority religion: a majority disturbed by a minority's religion could, in purely utilitarian terms, justify banning it; defending the minority needs reasons that value its rights.

Waldron's chapter (the Troubles in Northern Ireland and the interrogation "five techniques") distinguishes act, rule and indirect utilitarianism. Indirect utilitarianism is where he sees compatibility: for utilitarian reasons, we create structures in which officials are asked to refrain from utilitarian reasoning and to respect rights.

Exam use: state the utilitarian case at full strength, then show where rights cut it off.
