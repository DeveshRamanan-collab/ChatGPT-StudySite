Several defences may be pleaded to a nuisance claim: a prescriptive right to continue the interference
(the interference must have continued for more than 20 years); the plaintiff's own conduct or consent
(the most commonly pleaded defence — though it is no defence that the plaintiff "came to the nuisance",
eg by moving in next to an existing activity); statutory authorisation (available only if the authorised
activity could not have been carried out without committing the nuisance); and contributory negligence.

Three remedies are available: damages, injunction and abatement (self-help). Damages may cover damage to
the land, financial loss (loss of trade, decline in property value) and loss of subjective enjoyment —
but the type of harm claimed must have been reasonably foreseeable. An injunction is normally granted
where damages would not be an adequate remedy, with the likelihood of recurrence a key factor. Abatement
lets the plaintiff act without going to court, but they must be cautious and use only reasonable methods
to abate the nuisance.

Exam use: after establishing the elements of (public or private) nuisance, check each defence in turn —
"came to the nuisance" is a common trap answer that does *not* work — before recommending a remedy:
prefer an injunction over damages where the interference is ongoing or likely to recur, and mention
abatement only as a cautious, last-resort self-help option.
