The ICJ has no so-called compulsory jurisdiction. Two questions come first.

**Access.** Only sovereign states may be parties in cases before the Court; non-state entities cannot bring a case
(art 34). International organisations, including the UN, cannot be parties but can ask for advisory opinions (arts
65–68).

**Consent.** Like any international judicial or arbitral body, the Court's jurisdiction rests on the consent of
states — to the specific case, or to a category of disputes the case falls within. Art 36 gives four ways:

1. **Special agreement (*compromis*)** — the parties agree to submit a particular dispute to the Court.
2. **Treaty clause** — becoming party to a treaty specifying that disputes can be brought before the Court,
   contingent on the parties being unable to resolve them by other means (e.g. art IX of the Genocide Convention).
3. **Optional clause (art 36(2))** — a declaration accepting jurisdiction over future legal disputes with another
   state accepting the same obligation.
4. ***Forum prorogatum*** — a state that had not consented when the application was filed against it consents
   afterwards.

The Court rules on its own jurisdiction (*compétence de la compétence*, art 36(6)). And a dispute must exist at the
date proceedings are instituted.
