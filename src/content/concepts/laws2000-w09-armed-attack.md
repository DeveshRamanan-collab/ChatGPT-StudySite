Self-defence is only permitted in response to an "armed attack" (art 51). The meaning of armed attack is not
synonymous with "use of force" under art 2(4): an armed attack is one of the gravest forms of the use of force. Every
armed attack is a use of force, but not every use of force is an armed attack.

There is no clear benchmark. The threshold generally depends on **scale and effects** — casualties, damage to
property and so on.

In *Nicaragua* (1986) the ICJ found that the United States had been involved in an "unlawful use of force",
including by mining Nicaragua's territorial waters and attacking naval and port facilities. But it distinguished "the
most grave forms of the use of force (those constituting an armed attack) from other less grave forms". Certain
"frontier incidents", and the provision of arms, funds or logistical support to armed groups, remain below the gravity
threshold: significant government involvement ("effective control" over the operations) is required.

Attacks on territory, and also on a state's military vessels, can constitute an armed attack — but not attacks on
private ships (*Oil Platforms*). The lecturer noted the Court is not always consistent, and that it has left open
whether a series of smaller attacks could amount to an armed attack by their cumulative effect.

Exam use: find the use of force first, then argue its scale and effects up to (or below) the armed attack threshold
before turning to necessity and proportionality.
