Three models describe how innovation happens.

**Serendipity** is unexpected discovery — it can look like luck. But to recognise the significance of a discovery you
first need previous knowledge in the area: "chance favours the prepared mind" (Louis Pasteur). The lecturer's example is
the discovery of penicillin.

**Linear models** link the key components in a chain: science and technology lead to technological developments, which
are linked to the needs of the market, and that gives innovation. The lecture's example (Fig 6.3) runs idea generation
→ concept feasibility → capability development → ramp up → launch, through four gates: a project charter; a business
plan, after financial and technical feasibility; a launch and proposal, after prototype development; and a sanity
check, after test marketing, with feedback and corrective measures before commercialisation. It need not be followed
exactly to the letter.

**Simultaneous coupling** couples all the knowledge within those key components at once — science, technology and
market opportunity combine — so the point where innovation begins is not known.

The distinction is the starting point: serendipity starts with chance meeting a prepared mind; the linear model starts
with science and moves in order; simultaneous coupling has no single start.

Exam use: say which model fits the scenario and why — a planned, gate-by-gate project is linear; an accidental find
recognised by an expert is serendipity.
