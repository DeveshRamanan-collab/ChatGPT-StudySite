Does an armed attack have to be attributable to a state? Art 51 does not say who must carry out the attack.

- **Nicaragua (1986)** — an armed attack can be carried out by armed bands, groups, irregulars or mercenaries sent by
  or on behalf of a state; but supplying arms, funds or logistics is not enough, and the effective control threshold
  for attributing their acts is high.
- **September 2001** — Al Qaida's attacks in the United States raised the question of extraterritorial non-state
  actors. The Security Council affirmed the right to self-defence (Resolution 1373 (2001)), though, the lecturer
  noted, without calling the attacks armed attacks.
- **Violating another state's territory** — there is increasing state practice of invoking self-defence against
  non-state actors (Western states against Islamic State; Turkey against Kurdish organisations in Syria). But the
  ICJ treats attribution to the host state as important (*Wall* Advisory Opinion 2004; *Armed Activities* 2005).
- **"Unwilling or unable"** — some argue action can be taken if the host state is unwilling or unable to deal with
  the terrorist threat within its borders, but this doctrine remains controversial (e.g. Turkey v PKK in Iraq).
- **Invitation** — action is less contentious if it is invited by the affected (host) state.

Exam use: name the group and where it operates from, ask whether its attack can be attributed to the host state, and
if not, put "unwilling or unable" as a contested argument — or look for the host state's consent.
