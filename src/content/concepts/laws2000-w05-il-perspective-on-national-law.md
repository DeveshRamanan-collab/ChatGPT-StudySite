From an international law perspective, a state cannot justify a breach of international law based on its
internal law. Article 27 VCLT: "A party may not invoke the provisions of its internal law as justification for
its failure to perform a treaty". Article 46 VCLT adds that a state cannot escape a treaty because its consent
was given in breach of its internal rules on competence to conclude treaties — unless the violation was
"manifest" (objectively evident to any state acting in accordance with normal practice and in good faith) and
concerned a rule "of fundamental importance".

Beyond treaties, art 3 of the ILC Draft Articles on State Responsibility (2001) — mostly recognised as
representing customary international law — provides that whether an act is internationally wrongful "is
governed by international law" and "is not affected by the characterization of the same act as lawful by
internal law". So national law is (mostly) irrelevant to international obligations and state responsibility.

Yet national law remains relevant to international law: it is the fulfilment of obligations that require
national legislation; it is factual evidence of compliance and of state practice and opinio juris for custom;
national court decisions can be subsidiary sources (art 38, ICJ Statute); and it can offer interpretation of
international law and help identify general principles.

Exam use: whenever a state pleads "our law allows it", answer with arts 27 and 46 VCLT and art 3 — domestic
legality and international legality are two different questions.
