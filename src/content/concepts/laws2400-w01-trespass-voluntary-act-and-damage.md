Two of the four common trespass elements are the **voluntary act** and **actionable per se**. The
defendant's conduct must be a "positive act" — a merely passive act will not suffice. The text (p 45) gives
examples of conduct that is not a positive act: an act during an epileptic fit, an act caused by the force of
a third party, and a mere omission to act. If the defendant did not voluntarily perform the interfering act,
trespass cannot be made out no matter how directly the harm followed.

Separately, trespass is **actionable per se**: the plaintiff does not need to prove any damage or harm
actually resulted — the interference itself is enough to found the action. The rationale is protective:
trespass exists to protect a person's bodily integrity, liberty and property from direct interference,
regardless of whether measurable loss follows. This contrasts with an action on the case, where the
plaintiff must positively establish that she suffered damage as part of the cause of action.

For the exam: always check first whether the defendant's conduct was a positive, voluntary act before
considering directness or fault, and remember that a trespass claim does not fail just because the plaintiff
cannot point to any measurable loss.
