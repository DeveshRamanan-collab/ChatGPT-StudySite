Three kinds of remedy are available for trespass to land: damages (which may be nominal, compensatory,
aggravated or exemplary), injunctions (prohibitory or mandatory), and self-help — eg physically removing
a trespasser using reasonable force.

The measure of damages follows the general compensatory rule: put the injured party in the position they
would have been in had no wrong been committed. Damages are generally assessed as the diminution in the
value of the property, though in suitable cases the higher cost of reinstatement may be ordered instead.
It is also possible to recover for consequential injury or damage flowing from the trespass — for
example, if the trespass caused the plaintiff a psychiatric injury.

Because trespass to land is actionable *per se*, a plaintiff can obtain at least nominal damages without
proving any loss at all; the choice between diminution-in-value and reinstatement, and whether
aggravated/exemplary damages are added, depends on the facts (eg deliberate or high-handed conduct
supports aggravated or exemplary damages).

Exam use: once trespass is established, address remedy separately — state the available remedies, then
select and justify the measure of damages (diminution vs reinstatement) or explain why an injunction or
self-help is the more appropriate remedy on the facts (eg an ongoing or repeated trespass suits an
injunction better than damages alone).
