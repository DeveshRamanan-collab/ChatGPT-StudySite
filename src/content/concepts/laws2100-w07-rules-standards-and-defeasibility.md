Schauer suggests "the choice between rules and standards, between specific and vague directives, may not make nearly as much of a difference as is ordinarily assumed" (p 305). The conventional wisdom: a rule maker manages discretion by choosing specific rules (little discretion) or vague standards such as 'drive carefully' (more). Schauer places both on a continuous spectrum, a matter of degree, and advances the convergence hypothesis: over time interpreters push rules towards standards (the avoidance of rules, eg by finding exceptions) and standards towards rules (the concretization of standards). So regulatory specificity is less a tool for managing discretion "than has historically been thought" (319).

Hart rejects Llewellyn's dilemma (the formalist's heaven or no rules): a promise is not kept when someone is dangerously ill, yet the rule survives. Rules may have "exceptions incapable of exhaustive statement". "A rule that ends with the word 'unless…' is still a rule." (CL 139)

A rule is **defeasible** if capable of being defeated: a conclusion justified when certain criteria are met can be defeated if further criteria are met. In *Church of the Holy Trinity v United States* (1892) a clear statute banned importing aliens under contract to perform labour "of any kind", yet the Supreme Court held an English priest was not a foreign labourer: an implicit exception. *Riggs v Palmer* can be read the same way (unless the beneficiary murdered the testator).

Open questions: is defeasibility due to language, rules or law? How does it bear on certainty and indeterminacy?
