Psychiatric harm was traditionally labelled "nervous shock" and denied recovery; it is now compensable,
but a pure psychiatric-harm plaintiff must show reasonable foreseeability that a person in their position
would suffer a recognised psychiatric illness. *Tame v New South Wales; Annetts v Australian Stations Pty
Ltd* rejected three "control mechanisms" the lower courts had treated as preconditions — normal fortitude,
"sudden shock", and "direct perception" of the event or its immediate aftermath — holding none is "a
pre-condition to liability for negligently inflicted psychiatric harm" (Gummow and Kirby JJ). Reasonable
foreseeability of a recognised psychiatric illness is the only real test.

The two cases, decided together, show the test applied both ways. In *Tame*, a police officer's corrected
clerical error triggered an extreme and idiosyncratic reaction; the High Court held her psychiatric injury
was not reasonably foreseeable, so no duty was owed. In *Annetts*, an employer's broken promise to
supervise a 16-year-old jackaroo, followed by his death in the desert, was reasonably foreseeable to cause
his parents psychiatric harm — a pre-existing relationship (their inquiries and the employer's assurance)
made the class of possible plaintiffs determinate, so a duty was owed without sudden shock or direct
perception.

Feminist scholars critique this area: claimants for psychiatric harm have often been women, privileging
physical injury over emotional harm may disproportionately affect them, and the "normal fortitude"
standard is itself contested.

Exam use: in a NSW problem, note *Tame*'s test but go straight to CLA s 32, which has since modified it.
