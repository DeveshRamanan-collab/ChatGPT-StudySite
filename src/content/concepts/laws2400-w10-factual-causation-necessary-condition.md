Factual causation asks whether the defendant's act or omission in fact caused the harm. It is proved on the evidence, on the balance of probabilities (s 5E).

- **Common law — 'but for'.** Would the plaintiff have suffered the damage but for the defendant's negligence? *No* → satisfied; *Yes* → not satisfied. In *Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital* [1969] 1 QB 428 the duty doctor was negligent, but even timely treatment would not have saved the deceased.
- **CLA s 5D(1)(a) — 'necessary condition'.** Was the negligence a necessary condition of the occurrence of the harm? *Yes* → satisfied; *No* → not satisfied.

But for = necessary condition: two ways of saying the same thing, and courts often still use 'but for' language under s 5D(1)(a). The High Court called s 5D(1)(a) 'a statutory statement of the "but for" test of causation' (*Strong v Woolworths Ltd* (2012) 246 CLR 182). The flowchart's note says the same (*Adeels Palace*; *Wallace v Kam*; *Cox v New South Wales*), though it names 'Section 5C(1)'; in NSW the provision is s 5D(1)(a).

Where it matters what the injured person would have done had there been no negligence, s 5D(3) decides it **subjectively**, in the light of all relevant circumstances; their statement after the harm about what they would have done is **inadmissible** unless it is against their interest.

Exam use: name the test (with s 5D(1)(a) if the CLA applies), ask its question exactly, and read the answer the right way round.
