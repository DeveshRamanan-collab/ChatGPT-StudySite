The lecturer calls the six hats a type of brainstorm. Classic brainstorming aloud can go wrong: once an idea is said out loud, it influences everyone else's. One person talks and everyone else listens.

**Brainwriting** (Mattimore 2012): each participant writes an idea on a sheet and passes it on; the next person builds on it or uses it as a stimulus to trigger a new idea. Papers pass for four or five rounds — 12 participants and five rounds give 60 ideas. Each sheet returns to its owner, who circles one or two favourites for the group. It generates a great number of ideas very quickly, gets ideas out early, solves the introvert/extrovert problem (everyone "speaks" with their pen — creative democracy) and models idea building and piggybacking.

**Brainwalking** — "move the people instead of the papers". Flip-chart sheets on the walls become "ideation stations": people write an idea, rotate to the neighbour's sheet and add one, then return and circle favourites. Over brainwriting it adds energy (people get up and move), public ideas (dozens on the walls within minutes), shared purpose and group identity, mini-teams of two (half the ideas, but more quality, fun and energy) and bigger ideas written in marker.

Mattimore invented it for ideation sessions for Trident and Dentyne gums.

The lecturer's frame: these tools help you **diverge** once you have a problem; later you converge.

Exam use: match the technique to the group problem it fixes.
