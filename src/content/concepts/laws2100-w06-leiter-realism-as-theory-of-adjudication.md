In 'Legal Realism and Legal Positivism Reconsidered', Brian Leiter challenges two widespread views: (1) realism and
positivism are incompatible; (2) realism is "a jurisprudential joke", "a philosophical mess". Both trace to Hart's
chapter VII, which wrongly construed realism as a **theory of law** in competition with positivism.

For Leiter realism is a **descriptive theory of adjudication**:

- judges react primarily to the underlying facts of the case;
- decisions fall into discernible patterns (situation types), and these allow prediction;
- law is rationally indeterminate, so rules cannot explain decisions;
- the question is what the real (non-legal) reasons are: empirical rule scepticism.

Positivism, by contrast, is a general theory of law (the Social Thesis and the Separability thesis). Hart has good
arguments against conceptual rule scepticism, but none against empirical rule scepticism.

"The Realists […] cannot be conceptual rule skeptics, because their arguments for the indeterminacy of law presuppose a
nonskeptical account of the criteria of legality, one that has the most obvious affinities with that developed by hard or
exclusive Positivists." (293)

Three features of the relationship:

1. Both think law is indeterminate (different source and scope).
2. Realist indeterminacy arguments presuppose a positivistic account of the criteria of legality.
3. They disagree over how often legal rules matter in decisions: an empirical question about whether rules in books play
   a causal role.

Exam use: a way to think critically about Hart's reading of realism.
