Duty in sport may be owed to fellow players, spectators, coaches or administrators, but participants
accept the risks inherent in their sport. *Rootes v Shelton*: "participants may be held to have accepted
risks which are inherent in that sport or pastime … but this does not eliminate all duty of care of the
one participant to the other" (Barwick CJ) — whether a duty arises depends on circumstances. There, a
skier's collision with a boat was a breach of the observer's separate duty to warn, not an inherent risk
of skiing.

*Agar v Hyde* shows the same reasoning can defeat a claim: rugby players injured in a scrum argued the
game's governing body owed a duty to change the rules to cut "unnecessary risk", but the High Court found
none — rugby "is based on activities … which, by the standards of most members of the community, are
obviously dangerous" (Gleeson CJ); the answer is fact-specific.

The CLA gives dangerous recreational activities their own regime. Section 5L removes liability for harm
from an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity, whether or not the plaintiff knew of it. Section
5M removes the duty of care over a recreational risk once it has been the subject of a risk warning,
spoken or written, general or specific, whether or not understood.

Exam use: separate the sport's inherent risk (no duty) from a distinct failure to warn (duty, breach in
issue), then check whether ss 5L or 5M remove liability regardless.
