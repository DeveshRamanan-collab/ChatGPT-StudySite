Remoteness of harm is concerned with the extent of the defendant's liability for losses. The leading case is
*Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v Morts Dock and Engineering Co Ltd (The Wagon Mound (No 1))*: the extent of liability is
determined by a reasonable foreseeability test.

It is a different test from duty and breach, because the court asks what damage is reasonably foreseeable as a
consequence of the negligence. But 'reasonable foreseeability' means the same thing: a risk is reasonably foreseeable when it is a 'real risk' — one which would occur to the mind of a
reasonable man in the position of the defendant's servant and which he would not brush aside as far-fetched (*The
Wagon Mound (No 2)*).

- **Kind or type of harm** — the question is framed by the kind of harm: *Tremain v Pike*, *Nader v Urban Transit
  Authority of NSW*, *Commonwealth v McLean*, *Metrolink Victoria Pty Ltd v Inglis*.
- **Extent and manner need not be foreseeable** — the extent of the harm and the precise manner of its occurrence need
  not be reasonably foreseeable: *Hughes v Lord Advocate*, *Jolley v Sutton LBC*, *Mount Isa Mines v Pusey*, *Gittani
  Stone v Pavkovic*, *Waller v James*.

For psychiatric harm, *Tame* (week 6) asks whether the class of injury — psychiatric illness — was foreseeable, not
the particular disorder.

Exam use: name the kind of harm; ask whether it was a real, not far-fetched, risk; answer arguments about extent or
manner with the extent-and-manner cases.
