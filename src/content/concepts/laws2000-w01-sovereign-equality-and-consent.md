International law rests on state consent: no state can compel another state to sign up for a treaty
if that state doesn't want to, and there is no treaty and no rule of international law binding on a
state unless that state has consented to be bound by it. Consent can be express (ratifying a treaty)
or implicit (following a customary practice in the belief that it is legally binding — customary
international law). Because consent is individual, states end up with different rights and
obligations: some ratify a given treaty, others do not.

Consent sits alongside a separate principle, sovereign equality: each state, however large or small,
counts equally. In the UN General Assembly, China (population 1.4 billion) and Malta (population half
a million) each have one vote; decisions in most international organisations are taken by a majority
in which every member's vote carries the same weight (Art 2, UN Charter). Sovereign equality is the
international-law equivalent of the idea that all humans are born equal and remain free, and it
underpins the whole UN framework, alongside self-determination.

Consent and sovereign equality together explain why international law cannot simply impose
obligations the way a domestic parliament can: states are the makers of international law and its
subjects at once.

Exam use: when a problem turns on whether a state is bound, ask what it actually consented to --
never assume a rule binds a state just because most other states accept it.
