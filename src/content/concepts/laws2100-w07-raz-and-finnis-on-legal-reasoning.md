Raz, a legal positivist: "I have rejected the strong thesis of the autonomy of legal reasoning. Legal reasoning is an instance of moral reasoning." Legal doctrines are justified only if they are morally justified. Courts ought to follow the principles of their system, "which are moral principles, though they may be mistaken moral principles", so legal reasoning is sometimes morally incorrect.

Finnis, a natural law thinker: "Legal reasoning, indeed, is technical reasoning, at least in large part not moral reasoning." Academic theory exaggerates how far reason can settle greater good or lesser evil and minimises the need for authoritative sources. "A natural law theory in the classical tradition makes no pretence that natural reason can determine the one right answer" when the sources are unclear.

You might expect the positivist to call legal reasoning technical and the natural law thinker to call it moral. The reverse is true. Two lessons:

- Specific claims defended by specific arguments are separable (though not disconnected) from an author's general view of the nature of law.
- The real problem is whether legal reasoning is different in kind from other practical reasoning, reasoning geared towards action (in law, a decision).

Finnis's view fits his criticism of Dworkin's one right answer thesis (incommensurability): legal indeterminacy is part of the law, and a natural law theory still explains it.

Exam use: zoom in on the claim, zoom out to the theory, and say how far one follows from the other.
