Creative problem solving runs in three steps:

1. **Identify an interesting problem** — find problems, identify needs.
2. **Divergent thinking** — find solutions.
3. **Convergent thinking** — find « the » solution, or a good solution.

The first two steps make up the **prospective phase**; convergent thinking is the **analytical phase**. The slide draws
each step as a funnel: problem identification narrows to a point, divergent thinking opens out into many solutions,
convergent thinking closes them down again.

Each step asks for something different. Identifying meaningful problems — asking the right questions — is a key
characteristic of creative people. Divergent thinking breaks the "linear thinking" habit of settling for the first
idea (the quota method: always ask for at least three). Convergent thinking identifies benefits and risks, keeps the
best and eliminates wrong answers: evaluation and selection, positive and critical thinking.

The steps are also drawn as a cycle — problem identification, divergent thinking, convergent thinking, and round
again. Great advances are usually the result of a long process that is a sum of intuitions and successive
progresses. The lecture's examples start at step one: "Why is the speed of light the same everywhere?" led to the
discovery of relativity by Einstein; "How to make bus stop shelters viable?" led to the worldwide success of Decaux.

Exam use: when a scenario describes an activity, name its step and phase first (generating options = divergent,
prospective; scoring them = convergent, analytical), then say what that step needs.
