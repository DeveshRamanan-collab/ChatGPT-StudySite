Art 39 lists three findings, any one of which opens Chapter VII: a threat to the peace, a breach of the peace,
or an act of aggression. Without one, the Council can act only under Chapter VI, which needs no determination.

In practice the three are used very unequally. The lecture explained that the Council has never, or only very rarely,
found an act of aggression in its resolutions. It has found a breach of the peace on a very few occasions, each an
armed invasion of territory — Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990 is the example (compare Res 678). The categories
overlap: an invasion can be both a breach and aggression.

By far the most common finding is a threat to the peace, and its reach has grown since 1945. It began with invasion or
aggression between states and now covers non-traditional threats: proliferation of nuclear,
chemical or biological weapons, terrorism, internal armed conflicts, serious human rights or IHL violations, and the
2014 Ebola outbreak in Africa. The determination is therefore flexible, and it is the Council's to make.

During the Cold War, tension between the US and Russia, sometimes playing out through proxies, made Chapter VII
determinations hard to agree; the Council has been much more active since. The P-5 veto
(art 27(3)) remains a practical limit.

Exam use: in a problem, argue for a threat to the peace — the finding the Council actually uses — and support it with
the closest example from practice.
