Law predominantly refers to classes of persons, acts, things and circumstances. "If it were not possible to communicate
general standards of conduct … nothing that we now recognize as law could exist." (CL 124)

Two devices communicate standards: **legislation** (explicit general language: "Every man and boy must take off his hat
entering a church") and **precedent** (example: "Look – this is the right way to behave"). The contrast between their
uncertainties is "far less firm than this naïve contrast suggests" (CL 126).

There is a limit, inherent in language, to the guidance general language can provide. Plain cases: "If anything is a
vehicle a motor-car is one". Unclear cases: "bicycles, airplanes, roller skates?" (CL 126). The lecture calls these the
**core** and the **penumbra**. Where there are reasons for and against, a choice between open alternatives must be made:
judicial discretion.

Natural languages are irreducibly **open-textured**: "uncertainty at the borderline is the price to be paid for the use
of general classifying terms" (CL 128). We are human beings, not gods: relative ignorance of **fact** and of **aim**.

Every system compromises between two social needs: certain rules individuals can apply to themselves, and issues left
open for an informed, official choice in the concrete case (CL 130).

"Formalism and rule-scepticism are the Scylla and Charybdis of juristic theory; they are great exaggerations, salutary
where they correct each other, and the truth lies between them" (CL 147).

Exam use: Hart concedes indeterminacy at the penumbra only; not every case is hard.
