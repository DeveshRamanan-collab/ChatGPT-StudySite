Three constitutional provisions matter: s 51(xxix), the external affairs power, lets federal Parliament
legislate with respect to "external affairs"; s 61 vests the treaty-making power in the executive; and s 75(i)
gives the High Court original jurisdiction in "matters arising under a treaty". The Constitution does not say
what legal effect international law has domestically.

The external affairs power "is intended to enable Australia to carry out its functions as an international
person" (Murphy J, *New South Wales v Commonwealth*). Valid uses include legislation giving effect to treaty or
customary obligations, affecting relations with international persons, or on a matter of "international
concern" (*Koowarta*). In the *Tasmanian Dam Case*, being party to the World Heritage Convention let the
Commonwealth legislate to stop a dam that would normally be a Tasmanian matter — though a mere "device" would
probably not be legitimate.

Treaties do not have direct effect in Australia (apart from a very limited class such as certain peace and
boundary treaties). In *Dietrich v The Queen*, ratification of the ICCPR "has no direct legal effect upon
domestic law" until specific legislation implements it. A treaty can be transformed by: (1) new legislation;
(2) amending existing legislation (the Criminal Code for the Genocide Convention); (3) scheduling it to an Act
(not full incorporation); (4) relying on existing legislation already giving effect to it; or (5) giving it the
force of law (*Diplomatic Privileges and Immunities Act 1967* s 7).

Exam use: ratification alone creates no justiciable rights.
