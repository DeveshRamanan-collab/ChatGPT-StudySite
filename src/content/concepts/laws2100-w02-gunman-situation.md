"The gunman orders his victim to hand over his purse and threatens to shoot if he refuses" (CL 6). If the
victim complies, we say he was obliged to do so. To some it has seemed that here we have "the essence of law, or at
least 'the key to the science of jurisprudence'". Hart separates being obliged from having an obligation: the clerk
is obliged to hand over the money, but has no obligation to.

Hart avoids Austin's word 'command': "To command is characteristically to exercise authority over men, not power to
inflict harm" (CL 20). He uses 'orders backed by threats' or 'coercive orders' for orders which, like the gunman's,
are supported only by threats (CL 19).

To get a plausible model for law, Hart adds four elements: generality (general type of conduct and general class of
persons), enduring character, a general habit of obedience, and supremacy and independence (CL 24). The result:
"the laws of any country will be the general orders backed by threats which are issued either by the sovereign or
subordinates in obedience to the sovereign" (CL 25). Legal obligation is "this situation writ large" (CL 82).

The model is charitable interpretation at work: Hart ignores Austin's inconsistencies and develops his hints so the
doctrine is "stated in its strongest form" (CL 18), and only then attacks it in Chapters III and IV.

Exam use: state the view at its strongest before criticising it.
