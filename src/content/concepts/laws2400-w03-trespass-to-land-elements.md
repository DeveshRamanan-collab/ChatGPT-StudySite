Trespass to land is direct interference with land in the plaintiff's possession. The elements are: a
direct and positive act interfering with the land; done intentionally or negligently; while the land is
in the plaintiff's possession; without the plaintiff's consent (express or implied) or other lawful
justification (eg exercise of a lawful duty).

Trespass to land is actionable *per se* — the plaintiff need not prove damage. Except in highway cases,
the plaintiff does not have to prove fault either: the onus is on the defendant to disprove fault.
Mistake is no defence as long as the defendant's act was itself an intentional or negligent trespassory
act — a defendant who honestly but mistakenly believes the land is theirs still trespasses.

Trespassory conduct is broader than simply walking onto land: it includes driving onto the land,
remaining after permission to remain has been withdrawn, throwing an object or an animal onto the land,
and encroachment by scaffolding, cranes or other structures. Everyday examples the lecture gives: parking
a car on someone else's land, spraying graffiti on a building, gatecrashing a private party, and dumping
old furniture on someone else's property.

Exam use: run through the four elements in order on any land-interference fact pattern before considering
whether the interference was direct enough to be trespass rather than nuisance, and before turning to
consent or an implied licence as a possible answer to the fourth element.
