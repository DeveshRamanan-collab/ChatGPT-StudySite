Courts have drawn a distinction between negligent words and negligent actions. Lord Reid in *Hedley Byrne v
Heller*: "the law must treat negligent words differently from negligent acts" — careful people express definite
opinions on social or informal occasions without the care they would take if asked professionally, and "there
can be no duty of care on such occasions".

Deceit covered fraudulent misstatement, but *Derry v Peek* (1889) held that negligent
misstatement will not suffice for deceit: it must be intentional or reckless. *Hedley Byrne* [1964] became
critical because it created liability for negligent misstatement at common law: a duty of care for negligent
words where a special relationship exists.

- Lord Reid: the inquirer trusts the speaker to take care, reasonably relies, and the speaker knows or ought to
  know of the reliance.
- Lord Morris: a person with special skill who undertakes to apply it for another who relies on it.
- The bank's reference was given "without responsibility", so no duty arose on the facts.

*Mutual Life & Citizens' Assurance Co v Evatt* (1968): Barwick CJ — information or advice on a serious matter;
the speaker realises (or should) they are being trusted; it is reasonable for the recipient to act on it; a duty
of reasonable care follows.

Exam use: open a misstatement answer with Hedley Byrne's special relationship, then use the later cases to test
reliance, knowledge and assumption of responsibility on your facts. Deceit is an intentional tort outside this
unit.
