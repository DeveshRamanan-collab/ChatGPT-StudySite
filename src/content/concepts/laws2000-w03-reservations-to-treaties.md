A reservation is "a unilateral statement, however phrased or named, made by a State, when signing,
ratifying, accepting, approving or acceding to a treaty, whereby it purports to exclude or to modify the
legal effect of certain provisions of the treaty in their application to that State" (art 2(1)(d)) — distinct
from an interpretative declaration, which only explains a state's view without conditioning consent. They
matter mainly for multilateral treaties, where 160-plus states agreeing on every provision is unrealistic.

Article 19 permits a reservation unless the treaty bans reservations altogether, bans that one, or it is
incompatible with the treaty's object and purpose (no reserving against a core emissions-reduction duty in
an ozone treaty, say). Article 20(4): states may accept a reservation (both become parties on the modified
terms) or object (the treaty does not enter into force between the two); it takes effect once one state
accepts it. Some treaties need unanimous acceptance (art 20(2)) or the organisation's own (art 20(3)).

What happens to an *impermissible* reservation is unresolved: three solutions — the state does not become a
party at all; the reservation is severed and the state fully bound, including the reserved provision; or the
state is presumed bound without the reservation's benefit, unless it expressly conditioned consent on
acceptance. Either way the invalid reservation is void from the start.

Exam use: run art 19's gates first; only a passing reservation reaches art 20's accept/object machinery; a
failing one goes to the three unresolved solutions.
