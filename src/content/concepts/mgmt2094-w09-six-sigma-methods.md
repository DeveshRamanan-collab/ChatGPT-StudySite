Six Sigma, as the guest lecturer described it, is a quality-improvement methodology: it is about building and
improving processes, the efficiency and effectiveness of organisations, and discipline. He traced it to Motorola's aim
of near-flawless output (about 3.4 defects per million opportunities), popularised by GE from the mid-1990s.

Business leaders drive the **three principal methods** used in implementing Six Sigma:

- **The Improvement Process (DMAIC)** improves *existing* process performance (in the lecture: define, measure,
  analyse, improve, control).
- **Design for Six Sigma (DFSS)** generates *new* processes, products, services and/or plants.
- **Process Management** is the system that enables leverage and sustains the gains achieved by DFSS and the Sigma
  Improvement Process.
- **Leaders** drive and align the efforts strategically; leadership sits at the centre of the triangle.

Dow Corning used DFSS as its new-product development process. The guest lecturer said GE had trademarked its own design
sequence (DMADV), so consultants such as PwC used another, DMEDI, which Dow Corning followed.

Exam use: first ask whether the problem is improving something that exists (DMAIC) or creating something new (DFSS);
then who sustains the gains (Process Management) and who aligns the effort (leaders).
