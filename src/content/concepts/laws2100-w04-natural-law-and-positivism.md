Natural law theories dispute the (positivist) claim that law and morality are conceptually separable. For
legal positivists, the existence of law is simply a matter of social facts/practices. For natural law theorists, law
is a matter of social facts + conformity to certain moral standards (form and/or content): procedural (Fuller) or
substantive (Finnis).

Hart takes legal positivism to mean "the simple contention that it is in no sense a necessary truth that laws
reproduce or satisfy certain demands of morality, though in fact they have often done so" (CL 185–186). The utilitarian
tradition's three doctrines (separation, analytical study, imperative theory) are not a package: Hart rejects the
third.

Hart reads natural law as the contention that there is a connection between law and morality which is in
some sense 'necessary' and deserves to be taken as central (CL 155) — Augustine's "robber-bands enlarged". Two
versions:

- **Thomist**: principles of true morality or justice are discoverable by human reason without the aid of revelation,
  AND man-made laws conflicting with them are not valid law (*lex iniusta non est lex*).
- **Moral obligation**: for a legal system to exist there must be a widely diffused, though not necessarily
  universal, recognition of a moral obligation to obey the law.

Finnis: natural law theorists did not conceive their theories in opposition to positivism; law is both a sheer
social fact and a set of often sound reasons for action.
