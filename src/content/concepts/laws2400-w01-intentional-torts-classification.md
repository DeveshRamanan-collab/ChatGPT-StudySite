Historically, two families of action developed for wrongful conduct: **trespass**, for a direct interference
with the person, property or goods, and **action on the case**, for indirect and consequential damage. What
is now called "intentional torts" covers the trespass family: trespass to the person (assault, battery and
false imprisonment), trespass to land, and trespass to chattels/goods. Alongside them, related actions on the
case include intentional interference with the person (*Wilkinson v Downton*), malicious prosecution,
conversion and detinue; and beyond intentional torts altogether there are other actions on the case such as
negligence, nuisance, misfeasance in public office and deceit.

The exact form of action was historically crucial because it determined which court to sue in and which
pleading rules applied. In Australia the distinction between trespass and case still matters today, because
different elements must be proved for each category — as *Williams v Milotin* (1957) 97 CLR 465 shows, the
same facts (being hit by a car) can found either trespass to the person or negligence, each with its own
elements and its own limitation period. All forms of trespass share four common elements — voluntary act,
directness, actionable per se, and fault — considered in turn.

For the exam: before analysing elements, first classify the wrong as trespass or action on the case, since
that choice decides which checklist of elements applies and which limitation period runs.
