In England, no duty is generally imposed for PEL caused by an act; Australian courts may find one by
applying the salient factors approach, incrementally.

*Perre v Apand* (1999): Apand sold potato seed infected with bacterial wilt to Sparnon's farm, 3 km from the
Perres' farm. WA prohibited potatoes grown within 20 km of the disease, so the Perres — with no disease, no
physical damage and no contract with Apand — could not export to WA. The Perres lost below, but the High Court held **a duty was owed**.

McHugh J's sequence:

1. Was the loss to the Perres or the group reasonably foreseeable?
2. Would a duty impose indeterminate liability? (No — the 20 km rule defined the class.)
3. Would it unreasonably burden Apand's autonomy?
4. Were the Perres vulnerable to loss from Apand's conduct? (Yes — no control over their neighbours' seed.)
5. Did Apand know its conduct could harm people such as the Perres?

Salient factors from the various judgments: foreseeability, indeterminacy, coherence of law, membership of an
ascertained class, knowledge of the risk and its magnitude, control, and vulnerability.

*Caltex Oil v Dredge 'Willemstad'* (1976): even before the approach was settled, the High Court found a duty for
PEL. A dredge negligently damaged an oil pipeline; Caltex claimed only the cost of trucking oil around the bay,
not the minor property damage — and was owed a duty.
