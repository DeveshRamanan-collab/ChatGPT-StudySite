International law's history is usually traced from ancient cultures with mutual expectations about how
members should behave: the Greek polis (city-state) and "nomos" — restricting natural freedoms for
expediency and self-interest — and Roman law's jus naturale and jus civile. Thucydides' line that "the
strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must" still echoes, inverted, in sovereign
equality.

Most textbooks place international law's emergence as a system of law in Europe's late Middle Ages
(15th-16th centuries), under the Holy Roman Empire and the Catholic Church, and the theory of natural
law (jus naturale) — universal values governing relations between people. Hugo Grotius (1583-1645)
secularised natural law and distinguished it from jus gentium, human-made law variable in time and
place, whose validity rested on states' consent rather than natural reason. Colonialism's early
confrontations with the New World tested this: Francisco de Vitoria (1483-1546) concluded native
populations were part of the human race, so natural law applied to them, rejecting "discovery" as a
justification for conquest. Francisco Suarez and Emmerich de Vattel (Law of Nations) followed, Vattel
underlining state equality irrespective of power.

The Peace of Westphalia (1648) — the treaties of Munster and Osnabruck, ending the Thirty Years' War
— is the "beginning" of the international state system: organising territory and people into states,
and the birth of "sovereignty" itself.

Exam use: Westphalia is the hinge — everything before it is about competing sources of authority,
everything after is about sovereign states.
