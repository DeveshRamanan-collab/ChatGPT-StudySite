Individuals were not originally subjects of international law; they were the responsibility of their state of
nationality. After WWII, rights exercisable against states emerged: the non-binding Universal Declaration of
Human Rights 1948, then binding treaties such as the ECHR 1950 and ICCPR 1966. Some treaties let individuals
complain to international bodies — eight Torres Strait Islanders won a climate claim against Australia before
the Human Rights Committee under the ICCPR Optional Protocol. Individuals also bear obligations under
international criminal law: Slobodan Milošević was indicted by the ICTY in 1999, the first head of state
indicted before an international tribunal.

Groups: peoples hold rights such as self-determination and indigenous rights (the 2007 UN Declaration on the
Rights of Indigenous Peoples is not binding; ratified ILO conventions are). Minority rights (culture, language,
religion) are usually claimed individually. Non-state armed actors are bound by international humanitarian law,
including common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions.

Multinational corporations have rights under international economic law (investment protection) but, to date,
no legally binding obligations; the 2011 Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights are not binding.

Exam use: for any non-state actor, separate rights from obligations and binding from non-binding sources.
