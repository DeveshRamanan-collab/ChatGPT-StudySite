Scholars and judges propose theoretical justifications for tort law, descriptive (what it does) or normative
(what it should do). The **compensatory
function** sees tort law as putting an injured claimant back, so far as money can, into the position she
would have been in had the wrong not occurred — though this is hard to apply in practice (miscalculating
future earnings, high legal costs, lump-sum problems). The **deterrence function** argues liability makes
defendants (and others) avoid the conduct in future; law-and-economics scholars such as Calabresi and Posner
extend this systemically, arguing tort law allocates the costs of accidents to produce the most efficient
outcome for society — though insurance may blunt this deterrent effect.

**Corrective justice** (Weinrib) sees tort as allocating responsibility for harm: whoever wrongly harms
another's rights must correct it by paying compensation, resting on free human agency — but this individual
focus can ignore the many other people and systems involved in an incident (e.g. a medical negligence case).
**Distributive justice** treats tort as a system for spreading risk and loss through society, for example
no-fault schemes based on causation rather than fault, or losses passed to a public authority and then
taxpayers. **Feminist theory** asks whether tort law reflects women's experiences, challenging the
"reasonable person" standard, the valuation of women's unpaid work in damages, and which harms the law even
recognises. *Pabai v Commonwealth of Australia (No 2)* [2025] FCA 796 illustrates tort law's contemporary
reach as a response to societal challenges such as climate change.
