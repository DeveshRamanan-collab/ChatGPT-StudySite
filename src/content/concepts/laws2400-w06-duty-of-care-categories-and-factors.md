Not every harm caused by carelessness is legally recognised in the same way. Before asking whether a duty
of care exists in a given fact pattern, courts (and exam answers) run through three groups of factors.

**How was the harm caused?** Was it by an act or an omission — omissions attract duty far less readily.
Should "words" be treated differently from "acts"? A careless statement raises different concerns
(indeterminate liability, reliance) from a careless act.

**Who is the defendant?** A private individual, or a public authority exercising statutory powers with
limited resources? Is there a special relationship recognised as attracting a duty more readily — occupier
and entrant, employer and employee, manufacturer and consumer?

**What is the nature of the harm?** Physical injury is the paradigm case; psychiatric harm and pure
economic loss are both recognised, but only within extra limits the ordinary neighbour principle does not
reach on its own.

Exam use: before applying any specific rule (obvious risk, recreational activity, mental harm), classify
the problem against these three questions first — the answer tells you which body of rules (and which CLA
Part) is actually in play, and misclassifying the harm is the most common way to answer the wrong question
entirely.
