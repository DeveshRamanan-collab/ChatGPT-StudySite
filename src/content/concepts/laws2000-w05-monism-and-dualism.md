Two theories explain the relationship between international and national law from the domestic side.

Monism sees national and international law as part of one single normative legal order. International law has
direct effect (incorporation) and holds the same legal authority as national law, so courts can apply it
directly. Inconsistency between the two must be resolved, frequently in favour of international law. It is
often associated with the civil law tradition. Germany is a very clear example: art 25 of the Basic Law makes
the general rules of international law "an integral part of federal law" that "take precedence over the laws
and directly create rights and duties".

Dualism sees international and national law as distinct legal orders. The only law that has force at the
domestic level is national law, so international law must be transformed into domestic law to have effect
(transformation). Inconsistencies do not arise, because the systems have different subjects and purposes, and
national law usually has priority. It is often associated with the common law tradition.

These are ideal types: in reality, blends of the two characterise most domestic legal orders. The Dutch
Constitution mixes them — Parliament must approve treaties (art 91), but treaty provisions binding on all
persons become binding once published (art 93) and override conflicting statutory regulations (art 94).

Exam use (tutorial question 1): Australia is primarily a dualist system — treaties must be transformed, and
clear Australian law prevails over international law.
