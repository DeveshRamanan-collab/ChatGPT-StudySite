The use of public force limits individual freedom, so in a liberal democracy it needs a justification. Five ideas are commonly used:

1. **Prevention of harm to others** – the harm principle (John Stuart Mill).
2. **Prevention of offence** – the offence principle (Joel Feinberg).
3. **Prevention of harm to self** – legal paternalism.
4. **Prevention of immoral conduct**, even when there is no harm or offence – legal moralism.
5. **General welfare/public interest** – the welfare principle (eg compulsory taxation for public projects that some citizens may not benefit from).

Two reminders run through the week:

- The distinction between **popular** morality (what a society conventionally regards as right) and **critical** morality (what is objectively right).
- From a liberal point of view, the prevention of immorality in the absence of harm is not a legitimate reason to limit our freedom.

The lecture adds a useful contrast: the harm and offence principles are negative in nature, setting limits on public force; paternalism and moralism look at positive action the state might take to help people live a good life. Practical limits on enforcement are facts about the world; the week's question is the **moral** limits of positive law.

Exam use: before arguing about any prohibition, name which of the five principles is doing the justifying work.
