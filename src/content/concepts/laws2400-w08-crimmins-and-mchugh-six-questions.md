*Crimmins v Stevedoring Industry Finance Committee* (1999): a casual waterside worker, directed where to work by the
Australian Stevedoring Industry Authority, developed mesothelioma from unloading asbestos cargoes. The Authority was
the regulator, not his employer. A duty was owed: it had an overarching supervisory and regulatory role regarding
safety. McHugh J: it directed him to places of risk it
knew or ought to have known of, and he was specially vulnerable through his casual employment and his obligation to
obey its directions.

McHugh J's six questions for an affirmative duty of a statutory authority in a novel case:

1. Was injury from its act or omission (including a failure to exercise statutory powers) reasonably foreseeable?
2. Did its statutory obligations give it power to protect a specific class including the plaintiff, rather than
   the public at large?
3. Was the plaintiff vulnerable — unable reasonably to safeguard herself?
4. Did it know, or ought it to have known, of the risk to that class if it did not exercise its powers?
5. Would a duty impose liability for "core policy making" or "quasi-legislative" functions?
6. Are there other supervening policy reasons to deny a duty?

Not every answer must be "yes": a "yes" to 5 or 6 points against a duty. Hayne J, dissenting, found no duty in
deciding whether to use the quasi-legislative power to order respirators.
