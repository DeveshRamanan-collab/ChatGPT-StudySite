Hart answered Devlin in 'Immorality and Treason' (1959) and *Law, Liberty and Morality* (1963); Devlin replied in *The Enforcement of Morals* (1965).

Hart starts not with the harm principle but with Devlin's idea of morality: "The most remarkable feature of Sir Patrick's lecture is his view of the nature of morality – the morality which the criminal law may enforce". Moral judgments sit between rationality and feelings. Is morality really a compound of intolerance, indignation and disgust, "a real feeling of reprobation"? And does a society need a rich shared morality to exist? (The lecture: Hart's minimum content of natural law concedes only a minimal one.)

Morality is not a seamless web, and there are multiple criteria for restricting liberty. Hart asks two questions:

1. Is this behaviour, which offends moral feelings, **harmful** (independently of its repercussion on the general moral code)?
2. Is it true that, if we do not criminalise it, we will **jeopardise the whole fabric** of morality and society?

So he rejects Devlin's comparison between suppressing sexual immorality and suppressing treason.

**The deeper criticism**: it must be possible to rationally assess a claim that immoral behaviour should be punished, with reason, sympathetic understanding and critical intelligence. Is general morality based on ignorance, superstition or misunderstanding? Is the practice falsely thought dangerous to society? Are the misery and evil consequences of punishment understood?

Exam use: pair Devlin's three questions with Hart's two.
