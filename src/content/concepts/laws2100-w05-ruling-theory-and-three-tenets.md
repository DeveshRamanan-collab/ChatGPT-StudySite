*Taking Rights Seriously* (1977) defends a liberal theory of law against another liberal theory Dworkin calls
"the ruling theory of law". It has two parts, derived from the philosophy of Jeremy Bentham: a theory about what law
is (legal positivism) and a theory about what law ought to be (utilitarianism). Dworkin criticises both parts + the
assumption that they are independent of one another. For Dworkin, a general theory of law must be normative as well as
conceptual.

"Legal positivism […] is the theory that individuals have legal rights only insofar as these have been created by
explicit political decisions or explicit social practice." (TRS xii) Its most powerful version is Hart's, and that is
the version criticised.

In *The Model of Rules I* (1967), the key tenets of legal positivism are:

1. Legal rules can be identified in light of their **pedigree** (the rule of recognition).
2. There are cases in which judges must exercise **discretion**.
3. **Legal obligation** if and only if the case falls under a valid legal rule.

Dworkin's strategy is Hart's against Austin: find a feature of our law — principles, and later theoretical
disagreement — that the descriptive theory cannot explain.

Like Finnis, Dworkin rejects the separation of the 'is' question from the 'ought' question, but with different
arguments.
