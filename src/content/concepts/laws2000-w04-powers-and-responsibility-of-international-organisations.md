Principle of attribution (speciality, conferred powers): IOs have no general competence, only the powers their
members give them. In the *Nuclear Weapons* Advisory Opinion (1996, [25]) the ICJ said IOs "do not, unlike
States, possess a general competence" and are governed by "the principle of speciality" — the WHO could not
address the legality of nuclear weapons.

Powers are express (UN Charter art 42: the Security Council can authorise force) or implied: the UN "must be
deemed to have those powers which, though not expressly provided in the Charter, are conferred upon it by
necessary implication as being essential to the performance of its duties" (*Reparation*, 182). Implied powers
can be disputed, as when the Security Council created the ICTY and ICTR.

Responsibility follows distinct personality: the organisation, not its members, answers for its wrongful acts
(the International Tin Council). ARIO art 3: every internationally wrongful act of an IO entails its
responsibility; art 4: conduct attributable to it that breaches its obligation; art 6: conduct of its organs or
agents in performing their functions. Art 7: a state organ placed at an IO's disposal acts for the IO "if the
organization exercises effective control over that conduct" — so a contributing state that kept control of its
peacekeepers can be responsible. The draft articles are not binding and are criticised for modelling one
state-responsibility framework for very different IOs.

Exam use: check the constitutive instrument for the power, then attribute the conduct.
