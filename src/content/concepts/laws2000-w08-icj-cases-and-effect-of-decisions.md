The ICJ is the principal judicial organ of the UN, based in The Hague. It has 15 regular judges elected by the
Security Council and the General Assembly for 9-year terms; disputing parties may in principle appoint an ad hoc
judge. Precedent does not exist in international law, yet its "case law" is cited as if it were common law.

Two types of case:

- **Contentious cases** — legal disputes between states, submitted by them. Only states may be parties (art 34 ICJ
  Statute); the Court does not decide individual criminal responsibility. Binding between the parties only.
- **Advisory proceedings** — legal questions referred by UN organs and specialised agencies (art 96 UN Charter; art
  65 ICJ Statute). Non-binding, but weighty — e.g. the *Chagos* opinion requested by the General Assembly.

Proceedings are written and oral (art 43(1) ICJ Statute), with incidental proceedings such as provisional measures.

**Effect of judgments.** Art 60: "The judgment is final and without appeal"; if its meaning or scope is disputed,
the Court construes it at a party's request. It binds only the parties and cannot oblige third parties (arts 59,
61). Art 94 UN Charter obliges members to comply; if a party does not, the other may go to the Security Council,
which can make recommendations or decide on measures to give effect to the judgment (art 94(2)).

Exam use: say whether the proceeding is contentious or advisory before saying whether it binds anyone — and whom.
