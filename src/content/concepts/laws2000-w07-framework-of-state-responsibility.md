Rules of state responsibility indicate the circumstances in which a state's responsibility for the violation
of an international obligation is triggered, and the consequences this entails. They are secondary rules:
primary rules (found in treaty law or customary international law) determine the substantive obligations,
such as non-use of force; state responsibility determines the legal consequences of breaching them. The
general regime concerns states only and is not a specialised regime like WTO law.

The law was developed by the International Law Commission over more than 40 years and codified in the
Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts (ARSIWA), adopted 10 August 2001.
ARSIWA is not binding — it is not a treaty — but many articles appear to codify customary international law.

"Every internationally wrongful act of a State entails its international responsibility"
(art 1). An internationally wrongful act exists when conduct consisting of an act or omission (1) is
attributable to a state and (2) constitutes a breach of an international obligation owed by that state
(art 2). The term covers both crime-like and tort-like wrongs; international law does not distinguish.

Five steps for a problem: (1) primary legal obligation? (2) prima facie breach? (3) attributable to a
state? (4) any grounds precluding wrongfulness? (5) consequences — cessation, reparation — and who may
invoke responsibility.

Exam use: open every answer by naming the primary rule, then walk the five steps in order; never skip
attribution just because the breach is obvious.
