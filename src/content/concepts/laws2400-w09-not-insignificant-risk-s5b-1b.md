Section 5B(1)(b) adds a second requirement, joined to (a) by "and": the risk was not insignificant. It reflects
the **probability** of the risk materialising, and may not be satisfied if there was a very low probability of injury.

The Act deliberately changed the common law. *Wyong*'s threshold was "not far-fetched or fanciful"; the Act modified
the probability of harm to "not insignificant". The Ipp Report explains the double negative: "not insignificant"
indicates a risk "of a higher probability than is indicated by the phrase 'not far fetched and fanciful', but not so high
as might be indicated by a phrase such as 'a substantial risk'". So a risk that is merely not far-fetched — foreseeable
under (a) — is not automatically not insignificant.

How significant is a risk? *Rail Corporation NSW v Donald* [2018] NSWCA 82: it depends on factors such as
**obviousness**, **likelihood of occurrence** and **seriousness of consequence**, weighed together.

The textbook flowchart says the provision's meaning is not yet completely clear, and offers as arguable markers a "real
risk" (*Bolton v Stone*) that is not far-fetched or fanciful (*Wyong*). The slides place those words at the common law
threshold, so use them only as a starting point.

Paragraphs (a) and (b) together answer the first breach question: the degree of foreseeable risk.

Exam use: once (a) is established, give (b) its own sentence — place the risk on the scale and weigh obviousness,
likelihood and seriousness before moving to (c).
