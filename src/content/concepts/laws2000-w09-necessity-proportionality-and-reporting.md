Conditions on the exercise of self-defence: it is only permitted in response to an "armed attack"; it must be
"necessary and proportionate"; and it must be reported to the UN Security Council. Necessity and proportionality are
not explicitly stated in art 51; the ICJ confirmed them in *Nicaragua* as part of customary international law.

**Necessity** — the state must have no other means of halting the armed attack than recourse to force: self-defence
is a measure of last resort. Indicator: the **temporal link**. In *Nicaragua* [237] the measures began to produce
their effects several months after the alleged attack, so "it cannot be held that these activities were undertaken in
the light of necessity".

**Proportionality** — two approaches: (1) defensive action must be commensurate with the armed attack; or (2) it is
measured against the broader effects of the attack, balanced against its purpose of halting or preventing further
attack. It depends on the facts. Indicators: **destructive scope** — the Court "cannot close its eyes to the scale of
the whole operation" (*Oil Platforms* [77]); **geographical scope** — taking airports and towns hundreds of kilometres
from Uganda's border was not proportionate (*Armed Activities* [147]).

**Reporting** — measures "shall be immediately reported to the Security Council" (art 51), and the right lasts only
"until the Security Council has taken measures necessary".

Exam use: take each test in turn and support it with the Court's indicators, even where the facts point both ways.
