Duty of care's starting point is Lord Atkin's neighbour principle in *Donoghue v Stevenson*: you must take
reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your
"neighbour" — anyone so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought reasonably to have them in
contemplation. A manufacturer who sells a product intending it to reach the consumer in the form it left
them, with no reasonable possibility of intermediate examination, owes that consumer a duty to take
reasonable care (*Grant v Australian Knitting Mills*).

From the 1960s, English courts extended negligence into pure economic loss (*Hedley Byrne v Heller*) and
public authority liability (*Home Office v Dorset Yacht*), and proposed general tests for duty in *Anns v
Merton* (proximity, then policy) and *Caparo Industries v Dickman* (foreseeability, proximity, fairness).
Neither test was adopted in Australia: courts rejected Anns (*Sutherland Shire Council v Heyman*), and
Caparo has never been incorporated into Australian law. Australian courts instead treated proximity as a
"touchstone" limiting foreseeability through the 1980s, before the High Court moved, from the late 1990s, to
the contextual "salient factors" approach.

Exam use: the neighbour principle is always your starting point, but never your ending point — say so
explicitly, then move to established categories or salient factors.
