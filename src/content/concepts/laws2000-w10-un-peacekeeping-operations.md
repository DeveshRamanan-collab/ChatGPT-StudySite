UN peacekeeping or peace operations are not explicitly mentioned in the UN Charter. They were primarily created
to keep the peace, not to enforce it. Unlike an authorisation to one state or a group of states, these are UN
operations.

Traditional principles:

1. consent of the parties;
2. impartiality;
3. non-use of force except in self-defence.

Peacekeeping is gradually evolving — e.g. the UN as transitional administration in Kosovo and Timor-Leste. There is
also a changing attitude to the use of force: some missions are authorised to use force to carry out their mandate,
such as the missions in the DRC (including targeted offensive operations) and Mali.

Deployments of UN troops, military experts and police peaked in the mid-1990s and passed 100,000 by 2015, most of
them in sub-Saharan Africa. Current missions include MONUSCO (DR Congo), MINUSCA (Central African Republic), UNMISS
(South Sudan), UNIFIL (Lebanon), UNFICYP (Cyprus) and UNMIK (Kosovo).

In the absence of a standing UN army, the Security Council may decide on (1) a UN-run peacekeeping or peace
operation, or (2) authorise the use of force.

Exam use: distinguish a consent-based UN peacekeeping operation from a Chapter VII authorisation to states to use
force.
