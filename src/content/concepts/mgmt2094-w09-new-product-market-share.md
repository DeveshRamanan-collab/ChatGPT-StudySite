Dow Corning's problem was not invention but **commercialisation**: carrying ideas through testing and scale-up to
products that sell.

- It was a heavy R&D investor, spending **10% of revenue** on R&D, and its analytical capabilities were among the best
  in the world (the University of Michigan and Michigan State University used its scanning electron microscope).
- Before 1992 R&D was decentralised across a number of locations; after 1992 management created one central R&D
  location in Midland, Michigan.
- Design and commercialisation of a new product took on average **4 years**.

One of its Key Performance Indicators was **New Product Market Share (NPS)**: the percentage of revenue that comes
from newly commercialised products. In the 1970s and 80s NPS was around **25%**; by **1995** it had declined to
**13%**. The guest lecturer said the causes were argued over — some blamed the centralisation of R&D, others the
limited permutations left on the siloxane backbone — but the number had gone down, and leadership determined that the
way products were commercialised needed to improve.

Exam use: the case shows that R&D spending and world-class science do not guarantee innovation. What matters is the
process that carries ideas to market, and Dow Corning measured it by the share of revenue new products earned.
