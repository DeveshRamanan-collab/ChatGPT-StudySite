In *On Liberty* (1859) Mill explores the struggle between authority and liberty: "the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others." Our own good, physical or moral, is not enough. Over their own body and mind, the individual is sovereign; the doctrine applies only to human beings "in the maturity of their faculties".

**Harm** is narrow: not merely affecting another's interests, but infringing interests we are under a duty not to infringe (a specific duty to the public, or perceptible hurt to a specific individual). Recall the link between interests, entitlements and rights.

So the state may stop A assaulting B or stealing B's property, but exceeds its limits if it stops A destroying her own property or protects someone from being offended. Conduct that harms only the actor, or others with their consent, should be free, however "foolish, perverse, or wrong".

**Justification:** personal autonomy and the presumptive case for liberty (freedom is of intrinsic value). Mill adds a utilitarian argument: maximum happiness in the long run, because experiments in living benefit society and exercising freedom is "one of the principal ingredients of human happiness".

**Problems:** harm is conceived too narrowly; no one's affairs affect only themselves; it ignores indirect harms and offence that could become harm.

Exam use: state the principle, then test whether the conduct harms others in Mill's narrow sense.
