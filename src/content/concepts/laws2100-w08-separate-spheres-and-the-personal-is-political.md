'Separate spheres' ideology assigns a public sphere to men and a private sphere to women (and children). It was entrenched in law until relatively recently. In *Bradwell v Illinois* (1873) Justice Joseph P. Bradley wrote that "the civil law, as well as nature herself, has always recognized a wide difference in the respective spheres and destinies of man and woman", and that the family, "founded in the divine ordinance", indicates "the domestic sphere as that which properly belongs to the domain and functions of womanhood".

The law has incorporated the public–private distinction. Liberal feminism answered with equal treatment in public life; its limits include public vs private discrimination.

Feminists challenge the distinction: **'the personal is the political'**.

- Private inequality is the source of public disadvantage.
- The politics of power permeates the private sphere.
- The problems faced by women in the private sphere are a matter of systemic injustice.
- The family is a political institution.

The lecture: the distinction is not negative per se (we value our privacy). The two spheres can be distinguished without being separate; claims about justice were traditionally confined to the public sphere.

Practical achievements followed: the defence of provocation tightened in various jurisdictions; the marital rape exception removed; the NSW sexual consent reforms of 2021 (consent is a free and voluntary agreement that should not be presumed, and involves ongoing and mutual communication).

Exam use: distinguish "the spheres can be distinguished" from "the spheres are separate".
