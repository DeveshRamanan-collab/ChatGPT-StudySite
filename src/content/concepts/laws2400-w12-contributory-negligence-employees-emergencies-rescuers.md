The flowchart picks out situations that bear on whether careless-looking conduct is contributory
negligence.

- **Momentary thoughtlessness by employees.** In the employment context, inattention bred of familiarity and
  repetition, the urgency of the task, the plaintiff's preoccupation with the matter in hand, and other prevailing
  conditions may be taken into account (*McLean v Tedman*; *Staveley Iron and Chemical Co v Jones*).
- **Agony of the moment.** Action that may be considered to demonstrate a lack of reasonable care for the
  plaintiff's own safety may not amount to contributory negligence where the plaintiff's actions can be justified in
  a situation of perceived emergency (*Avram v Gusakoski*; *Caterson v Commissioner for Railways*).
- **Rescuers.** Law in most jurisdictions now offers protection to those who act in good faith and without
  recklessness (eg Civil Law (Wrongs) Act 2002 (ACT) s 5; CLA s 56). In NSW, s 56 defines a good samaritan as one
  who, in good faith and without expectation of payment or other reward, assists a person apparently injured or at
  risk of being injured; s 57 protects a good samaritan from personal civil liability in that emergency.

The materials do not say how a rescuer's own carelessness is weighed: the NSW sections protect the good samaritan
from liability, and their wording differs from the flowchart's "without recklessness".

Exam use: when the plaintiff's carelessness happened at work, in a perceived emergency or during a rescue, name the
circumstance and its authority before arguing any reduction.
