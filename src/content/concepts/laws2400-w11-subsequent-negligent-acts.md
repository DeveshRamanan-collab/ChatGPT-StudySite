Where the defendant's negligence is followed by someone else's negligence — a third party's, or even the
plaintiff's own — the flowchart takes a separate branch.

- Whether a subsequent negligent act severs the causal connection is a matter of 'fact and degree' (*Mahony v J
  Kruschich (Demolitions) Pty Ltd*).
- Negligence of a third party or even the plaintiff themselves does not necessarily sever the chain of causation
  (*Mahony*; *Burns v Pearce*).
- This is especially so where the defendant's wrongful conduct has generated the very risk of injury resulting from the
  negligence of the plaintiff or a third party, and that injury occurs in the ordinary course of things (*March v E &
  MH Stramare Pty Ltd*). See, eg, *Club Italia (Geelong) Inc v Ritchie*, *Jausnik v Nominal Defendant (No 5)* and
  *Gittani Stone Pty Ltd v Pavkovic*.

So later negligence is weighed, not treated as an automatic break. The plaintiff's case is strongest where the later
carelessness is the very thing the defendant's negligence made likely, and the injury follows in the ordinary course
of things.

Exam use: name the later negligent actor; say the question is one of fact and degree (*Mahony*); then ask whether the
defendant's conduct generated the very risk that materialised (*March v Stramare*). A deliberate, volitional
intervention is analysed under the voluntary-act test instead.
