Before *Donoghue v Stevenson*, the owner of "dangerous things" had a duty to take reasonable precautions
to keep them from third parties, but no equivalent duty existed for non-dangerous things. After *Donoghue*
and *Grant v Australian Knitting Mills*, the ordinary principles of negligence became a basis for
liability for defective products generally, sitting alongside (not replacing) liability in contract, under
sale-of-goods law, and under the Australian Consumer Law.

*Graham Barclay Oysters v Ryan* shows foreseeability of harm does not automatically mean a duty has been
breached. Consumers contracted Hepatitis A from oysters contaminated by faecal matter after a storm; the
grower's "depuration" process reduced, but could not eliminate, the viral load. The High Court held the
NSW government owed no duty of care, and although the grower did owe one, it was not breached: even though
the risk was reasonably foreseeable and its magnitude significant, "the degree of probability of its
occurrence" did not justify the "difficult, expensive and inconvenient alleviating action" (eliminating
the risk entirely) the consumers argued for.

Exam use: in a product-liability problem, do not stop at foreseeability — go on to ask whether the
precautions the plaintiff says should have been taken were actually reasonable given their cost,
difficulty and inconvenience against the probability and magnitude of the risk.
