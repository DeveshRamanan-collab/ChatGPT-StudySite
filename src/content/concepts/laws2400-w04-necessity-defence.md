Necessity may excuse what would otherwise be a tort in three broad situations: public necessity
(throwing luggage from a sinking ship to save lives), protecting one's own property (trespassing on a
neighbour's land to rescue a pet), and preserving the life or health of someone unable to consent
(treating an unconscious patient). It requires that the action was necessary to prevent serious harm to
person or property, and that it was reasonable and proportionate to the risk.

The doctrine is narrow. In *Southwark London Borough Council v Williams*, homeless squatters pleaded
necessity against a possession order; Lord Denning rejected it, warning that admitting homelessness would
mean "no one's house would be safe" and that the doctrine "must be carefully circumscribed". By contrast,
in *Proudman v Allen*, a defendant who turned a driverless car's wheel to avoid a collision, sending it
into the sea, successfully relied on necessity — available even to strangers protecting another's
property. It has also succeeded in trespass to the person, pleaded alongside self-defence in *NSW v
McMaster*.

In the medical context, *In re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation)* requires that it not be practicable to
communicate with the person, and that the action taken be what a reasonable person would take in the
assisted person's best interests — distinguishing temporary incapacity (do no more than necessary until
consciousness returns) from permanent incapacity (act in the patient's best interests without waiting).

Exam use: identify which necessity situation applies, then test necessity and proportionality against
Southwark's narrow approach.
