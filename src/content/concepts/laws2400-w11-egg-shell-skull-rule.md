The egg-shell skull rule: "The defendant must take the plaintiff with all his weaknesses, beliefs and reactions
as well as his capacities and attributes, physical, social and economic" (*Nader v Urban Transit Authority of New South
Wales*; see also *Kavanagh v Akhtar*).

It sits on the remoteness branch of the flowchart, beside the rule that the extent of the harm need not be reasonably
foreseeable. Once harm of a reasonably foreseeable kind is caused, the plaintiff's own weaknesses, beliefs and reactions
— and their capacities and attributes, including social and economic ones — do not cut down the defendant's liability
for the harm that actually follows. A plaintiff whose condition makes the injury worse, or whose earning capacity makes
the loss larger, is taken as found.

The casebook links it to week 6. In *Tame v New South Wales*, Gummow and Kirby JJ noted Windeyer J's point in *Mount
Isa Mines v Pusey* that a "normal fortitude" condition of liability did not stand well with the egg-shell skull rule,
and said that, if liability is established, the egg-shell skull rule applies to the assessment of damages. It does not
itself create liability: for NSW mental harm, CLA s 32's normal-fortitude duty test must still be met.

Exam use: where the harm is unusually severe because of who the plaintiff is, cite *Nader* and say the defendant takes
the plaintiff as found.
