Acquisition of territory is a state gaining sovereign title over land (continental territory or an island).
There are five modes.

1. Cession: transfer from one state to another, often by purchase treaty (Alaska from Russia, 1867). The
   acquirer gets no more rights than the ceding state had, and must respect third states' and indigenous
   rights.
2. Accretion: new land formed gradually by nature (a 1986 volcanic island in Japan's territorial sea). An
   island is "a naturally formed area of land, surrounded by water, which is above water at high tide" (UNCLOS
   art 121(1)); artificial islands and low-tide elevations cannot ground sovereignty (South China Sea
   arbitration, 2016).
3. Occupation of terra nullius: effective control plus intention to claim title over land never under any
   state's sovereignty. Barely applicable now, but central to colonial expansion.
4. Prescription: title over another state's territory through peaceful control, acting as sovereign, on implied
   consent — sustained protest prevents it.
5. Conquest: prohibited since the UN Charter 1945 (art 2(4)); occupation by force now triggers the belligerent
   occupation regime, temporary and without transfer of sovereignty (Kuwait 1990, Crimea 2014).

Exam use: identify whether the land was previously sovereign (prescription, cession, conquest) or not (terra
nullius, accretion), then check consent and use of force.
