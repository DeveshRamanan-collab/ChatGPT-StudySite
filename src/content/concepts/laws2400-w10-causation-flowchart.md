Negligence needs **damage** — not damages — and damage is the gist of the action: no damage, no action. The damage must be causally connected to the breach and not too remote, and the plaintiff must prove both on the balance of probabilities (CLA s 5E). Causation is a factual question: did the act or omission in fact cause the harm? Remoteness is a legal question involving policy: should the defendant be held legally responsible?

The textbook flowchart (Tort Law Principles, pp 433–435) has two branches, and both must be satisfied: **factual causation** and **scope of liability** — CLA s 5D(1)(a) and (b). The terminology changed under the CLA, but the common law principles still apply.

**Factual causation** runs down two columns: the common law 'but for' test, and the 'necessary condition' test in s 5D(1)(a). Either way there must be a connection between the defendant's breach of duty and the plaintiff's injury (*Adeels Palace v Moubarak*; *Strong v Woolworths*). Where the test yields unsatisfactory results — **multiple sufficient causes** or **material contribution to harm** — the flowchart turns to s 5D(2).

**Scope of liability** has two parts joined by AND: new intervening acts, and remoteness (Topic 11).

Exam use: confirm damage, then work in order — factual causation (name the test, ask its question, read the answer), s 5D(2) only if necessary condition cannot be established, then scope of liability.
