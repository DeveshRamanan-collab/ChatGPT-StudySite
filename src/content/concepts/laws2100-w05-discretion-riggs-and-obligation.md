"Discretion, like the hole in a doughnut, does not exist except as an area left open by a surrounding belt of
restriction." (TRS 31)

- **Weak discretion**: the standards cannot be applied mechanically but demand judgment; or an official has final
  authority and cannot be reviewed. Dworkin accepts both.
- **Strong discretion**: on some issue the official "is simply not bound by standards set by the authority in
  question" (TRS 32). Positivists, Dworkin argues, are forced to accept it when no rule applies.

*Riggs v Palmer*: Elmer murdered his grandfather to secure his inheritance under a valid will. Literally construed, the
statutes gave him the property; but laws "may be controlled in their operation and effect by general, fundamental
maxims of the common law" — no one shall profit by his own wrong. The minority: the laws are silent, and refusing him
would add a punishment.

Do such principles control judges, as a sergeant's orders control him? If they are law, then the first tenet (a
master rule) goes, discretion is only weak, and the third tenet falls: an obligation "might be imposed by a
constellation of principles as well as by an established rule" (TRS 44). The positivist alternative: in a hard case
there is no obligation until the judge creates a new rule for the future. Raz and the realists say judges reach beyond
the law.

Exam use: say whether the judge's principle is legal or extra-legal, and what follows for obligation.
