Justification: some of the strongest moral arguments for human rights come from natural law, from deontological principles and from the idea of human dignity.

Jeremy Bentham: "Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense – nonsense upon stilts." (*Anarchical Fallacies*) The lecture: for Bentham, a utilitarian with a positivist outlook, the only rights that exist are legal rights; natural rights are wishful thinking.

The slide's sceptical questions, with the lecture's gloss:

- **Is human nature just a myth?** Rights talk is often rhetoric; if every entitlement is a right, the category loses its point. A call for better definition and discipline.
- **Are rights selfish?** They favour the individual over the general interest; what about duties?
- **Are rights an instrument of oppression?** Rights can be tied to a particular power structure.
- **Are rights a threat to the value of community?** Individual autonomy against the community.
- **Are rights ethnocentric?** Perhaps a Western creation; yet meaningful comparison across legal systems suggests common ground.

Behind these lies the nature of morality. **Moral relativism**: are moral disagreements rationally resolvable? (Distinguish metaethical from normative relativism.) **Moral objectivism/realism**: are moral statements apt for truth and falsity? Relativism makes moral claims relative to a culture or society; objectivism holds there are moral facts.

Exam use: the case for rights is only as strong as the account of morality behind it.
