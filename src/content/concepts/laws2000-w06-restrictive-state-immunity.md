State immunity was originally absolute. Today most states apply a restrictive approach. Acts jure imperii —
of a sovereign nature, such as foreign and military affairs, legislation, police power and the administration of
justice — are immune. Acts jure gestionis — of a non-sovereign, commercial nature — are not. In the Empire of
Iran Case (1963) the German Federal Constitutional Court found absolute immunity was no longer custom; in Trendex
v Central Bank of Nigeria (1977) the English Court of Appeal refused immunity to a state organ that failed to
honour a letter of credit. About one-third of states, including China, reject the distinction.

Art 10 of the 2004 Convention: a state that engages in a commercial transaction with a foreign natural or
juridical person cannot invoke immunity in proceedings arising out of it — except for transactions between
states or where the parties expressly agree. Art 2(1)(c) defines commercial transactions (goods, services,
loans, other commercial dealings, not employment contracts); profit is irrelevant.
Arts 11 and 13–17 add employment, property, intellectual property, companies, ships and arbitration.

Art 2(2): look primarily at the nature of the transaction; consider purpose only if the parties agreed. Buying
embassy office supplies is commercial whatever its purpose. In Playa Largo (1983) Cuba's diversion of a sugar
cargo for foreign-policy reasons was still commercial.

Art 12 removes immunity for compensation for death, injury or damage to tangible property caused by an act
attributable to the state, occurring in the forum state with its author present.
