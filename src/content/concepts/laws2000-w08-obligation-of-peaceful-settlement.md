Art 2(3) UN Charter: "All Members shall settle their international disputes by peaceful means in such a manner that
international peace and security, and justice, are not endangered." It works hand in hand with art 2(4), the
obligation to refrain from the threat or use of force — first settle peacefully, then never use force.

The obligation is reiterated beyond the Charter — the OAS Charter (art 2(c), 1948), the Friendly Relations
Declaration (1970), the Manila Declaration (1982), the Declaration on the Prevention and Removal of Disputes (1988)
and the Constitutive Act of the African Union (art 4(e), 2000) — and it is binding under customary international law
(*Nicaragua*, 1986, para 290).

It is an **obligation of conduct, not an obligation of result**: states are not legally obliged to reach any
particular settlement, which is why some disputes last for decades (Canada and Denmark disputed Hans Island for
about 50 years before agreeing to share it). If a dispute cannot be resolved, states remain obliged to keep trying
peacefully — and never to use force. The choice of means rests on consent.

Art 33(1) lists the means — negotiation, enquiry, mediation, conciliation, arbitration, judicial settlement, resort
to regional agencies or arrangements — and ends with "other peaceful means of their own choice", so the list is
open-ended.

Exam use: cite art 2(3) and custom for the duty, but never argue that a state is in breach merely because a dispute
remains unresolved.
