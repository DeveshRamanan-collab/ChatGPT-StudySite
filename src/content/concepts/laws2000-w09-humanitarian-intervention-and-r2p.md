Humanitarian intervention — force to protect people in another state from gross and systematic human rights
violations — is not addressed in the UN Charter and appears to conflict with art 2(4). The debate came to the fore in
the 1990s, after failures to protect in Rwanda (1994) and Srebrenica (1995).

**Kosovo (1999).** NATO intervened without Security Council authorisation. The UK argued it was legal "as an
exceptional measure on grounds of overwhelming humanitarian necessity". The Independent International Commission on
Kosovo called it "illegal, yet legitimate"; many non-participating states contested it.

**R2P.** The ICISS (initiated by Canada; report December 2001) shifted the language from a right to intervene to a
responsibility to protect. Sovereignty implies responsibility for protecting one's own people from four crimes —
genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity. When a state is "unwilling or unable", the
international responsibility is to prevent, to react (military force as a last resort) and to rebuild. Three pillars
(2005 World Summit Outcome): the state's primary duty to its population, citizens or not; international assistance;
response through UN mechanisms.

Libya (2011) drew criticism of forcible regime change. Critiques: R2P does not solve Security Council paralysis; it is
applied selectively; it raises unrealistic expectations; it is open to abuse; it risks a return to just war. ILA
(2018): humanitarian intervention remains a "legal grey area".

Exam use: argue that unilateral humanitarian force has no Charter basis and that R2P works through the Security
Council.
