Australia's treaty-making process has six steps. (1) The lead minister seeks a mandate to negotiate from
the Foreign Minister. (2) The text is negotiated and finalised, consulting other Commonwealth, state and
territory bodies. (3) A National Interest Analysis (NIA) is prepared and Executive Council (ExCo) approves
signature. (4) Australia's representative signs — a full-powers instrument is needed unless the signatory is
the PM or Foreign Minister. (5) The treaty is tabled for the Joint Standing Committee on Treaties (JSCOT),
which reports within 15–20 joint sitting days on whether binding treaty action should be taken. (6) Once any
implementing legislation passes, entry into force follows — usually an exchange of diplomatic notes.

AUKUS illustrates every step with real documents. The 2021 ENNPIA's NIA explains it is needed because US and
UK domestic law restricts disclosing naval nuclear propulsion information without it, and needs no changes
to Australian law; it can be terminated on six months' notice. The later 2024 Agreement for Cooperation
Related to Naval Nuclear Propulsion went through full JSCOT scrutiny (Report 224, November 2024, 260
submissions, four hearings) before JSCOT recommended binding treaty action. Professor Donald Rothwell
criticised its asymmetric withdrawal terms — only the UK and US can be an "Originating Party" able to
withdraw over their own defence assessment, leaving Australia's own exit route comparatively narrow.

Exam use: walk the six steps in order — nothing binds Australia internationally until ExCo approval and
signature, and nothing is domestically secure until JSCOT reports.
