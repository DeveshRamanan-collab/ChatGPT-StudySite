Diplomats and consuls enjoy a treaty regime: the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations 1961 (VCDR) and the Vienna
Convention on Consular Relations 1963 (VCCR). In Tehran Hostages (1980) the ICJ called diplomatic law a
"self-contained regime" and held Iran's inaction when militants held US diplomats for 444 days a breach.

Under VCDR art 31 a diplomatic agent is immune from the receiving state's criminal jurisdiction, and from its
civil and administrative jurisdiction except for private immovable property, private succession matters, and
professional or commercial activity outside official functions. The agent's person is inviolable — no arrest
(art 29) — as are the private residence, papers and correspondence (art 30); the agent must respect local law and
not interfere in internal affairs (art 41). Family members, administrative and technical staff and service staff
(official acts only) have narrower protection, generally not if nationals or permanent residents of the
receiving state.

Consuls have restricted immunities: limited inviolability (arrest for a 'grave crime', VCCR art 41) and immunity
ratione materiae (art 43).

Mission premises are inviolable and may not be entered without the consent of the head of mission (VCDR art 22),
and the receiving state must protect them; premises, property, transport and archives are immune from search and
execution (arts 22, 24). For abuse the receiving state may declare a diplomat persona non grata (art 9), sever
relations, act unfriendly, do nothing, or seek waiver from the sending state.
