Because international law has no legislator and no compulsory courts, its sources look nothing like a
domestic legal system's. Article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute is the first place of reference for identifying
them, though it was not drafted to give a clear reference to what the sources of international law are — it
lists what the Court applies when deciding disputes, not a designed, exhaustive catalogue.

Article 38(1) distinguishes two groups. Treaties, customary international law and general principles of law
are primary sources: the real, true sources of binding obligations by states. Judicial decisions and the
teachings of the most highly qualified publicists are secondary sources: they don't create law, they help
interpret the international legal principles found in the primary sources.

Exam use: "there is a clear hierarchy between the sources" is a claim to analyse critically, not accept. The
ordering in Article 38(1)(a)-(d) tracks the primary/secondary, law-creating/law-interpreting distinction —
it does not rank treaty above custom or custom above general principles. A treaty and a customary rule can
bind the same states on the same question simultaneously, and general principles only step in as
gap-fillers when neither is available. Article 38 is also not exhaustive: unilateral statements, jus cogens,
decisions of international organisations and soft law all sit outside it but still shape state behaviour, so
an answer that stops at Article 38(1) has not identified every possible source.
