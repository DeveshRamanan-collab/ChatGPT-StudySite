*Lex iniusta non est lex*: an unjust law is not a law. The key question is how injustice affects the
obligation to obey the law.

For Finnis, 'the obligation to obey the law' may mean:

1. empirical liability to be subjected to sanction in event of non-compliance;
2. legal obligation in the intra-systemic sense (legal obligation 'in the legal sense');
3. legal obligation in the moral sense;
4. moral obligation that does not derive from the legality of the stipulation-of-obligation.

→ Unjust laws are not laws in their central case/focal meaning.

**Strong natural law thesis**: moral soundness/validity is a necessary element of legal validity; unjust "laws" are
not legally valid. For some theorists (eg Gustav Radbruch) only extremely unjust "laws" are not legally valid. This is
a claim about each and every unjust law.

**Weak natural law thesis**: unjust "laws" are not laws in the true sense of the word (Finnis). They are morally and
legally defective — but that is not to say they are legally invalid.

The lecture's analogy: an unfaithful friend is not a friend, a fallacious argument is not an argument. We still use
the word — a fallacious argument is an argument — but it is not properly one, because we measure it against the
central case. The more unjust a system is, the more peripheral it is to the central case of law.

Exam use: always say which thesis a writer holds; attacking the strong thesis does not touch Finnis.
