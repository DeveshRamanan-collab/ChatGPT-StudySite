Article 2(1)(a) VCLT defines a treaty as "an international agreement concluded between States in written
form and governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or in two or more related
instruments and whatever its particular designation". Four elements: between states (VCLT only covers
state-to-state agreements, art 1); in written form (it can be one document, a series of resolutions, or a
treaty plus a protocol); governed by international law (disputes are resolved by international law, not any
party's domestic law); and it does not matter what label the parties use — treaty, convention, agreement and
protocol are all treaties in substance.

Courts look past labels to substance. In *Aegean Sea Continental Shelf*, a press communiqué was capable of
being a binding international agreement. In *Qatar v Bahrain*, written minutes recording commitments the
parties had consented to were held to be a treaty. In *Somalia v Kenya*, a signed, dated MoU registered with
the UN was held to be a treaty at international law — even though Australia's own DFAT practice
deliberately treats an MoU as "less than a treaty" and not a treaty at all.

Exam use: run the four elements against the facts first, and do not assume a document is not a treaty just
because it is informal, short, or called something else — but remember Australia's own practice can give a
document (like an MoU) a status different from its status at international law generally.
