CLS offers a radical critique of law, seen as a coherent and neutral system of rules; of legal institutions; and of liberal
legal values (the rule of law, the idea of rights, the legal process). For CLS, law is a contradictory and illegitimate
system which maintains oppressive social relations. Influences: legal realism, postmodernism and Marxism.

**With realism**: law is indeterminate, so it does not guide or constrain judicial reasoning; legal reasoning is a myth.
**Beyond realism**: more interested in the politics and ideological nature of law (law mirrors political visions), and a
more radical indeterminacy. From postmodernism, words do not have stable meaning.

**Postmodernism** (Lyotard, Derrida, Foucault, Baudrillard, Rorty): anti-foundationalist; rejects epistemic certainty,
the stability of meaning and "grand narratives" of the Enlightenment project; human reasoning is always socially
conditioned; language constitutes reality; deconstruction.

**Marx's historical materialism**: forces of production → relations of production; the superstructure helps the
dominant class maintain them; capitalism needs law that legitimises private ownership, yet law presents itself as neutral.

Key claims:

- radical legal indeterminacy; no coherent sense out of the legal materials;
- law is contradictory because it stems from political conflict;
- yet decisions may be predicted because judges share a commitment to the status quo;
- law as ideology: it promotes the interests of the powerful and legitimises injustice;
- scepticism about neutral legal concepts and doctrines.

Exam use: the is/ought separation makes no sense to CLS; test each claim against Hart's or Dworkin's reply.
