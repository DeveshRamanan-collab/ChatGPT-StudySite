  Halvia and Mesken are neighbouring states, both UN members and both parties to the ICCPR. Both have made declarations under art 36(2) of the ICJ Statute accepting the Court's jurisdiction over legal disputes with any state accepting the same obligation.

  Eighteen months ago, after a border war, Halvian forces occupied the Drava Valley, part of Mesken's territory. (The lawfulness of the original use of force is not in issue.) Halvia administers the valley through a military governor. Since then:

  (a) Halvian soldiers have detained about 300 Meskeni civilians at Camp Orl, in the valley, without charge or access to lawyers; former detainees describe beatings during interrogation;
  (b) the Valley Guard, a local militia armed and paid by Halvia but commanded by its own leaders, has burned the homes of Meskeni families who refuse to leave the valley, while Halvian patrols watched without intervening; and
  (c) Halvia has built a fortified fence through the valley, cutting 4,000 Meskeni farmers off from their fields and from the valley's only hospital. Halvia has not proclaimed any emergency or derogation.

  Two former detainees brought individual complaints to the Human Rights Committee, whose competence Halvia has accepted. The Committee adopted views finding that Halvia had violated ICCPR arts 7, 9 and 10. Halvia rejected the views as "not binding". It also says that the ICCPR does not apply outside Halvia's territory, that it is not responsible for what the Valley Guard does, and that the fence is a temporary security measure made necessary by cross-border raids.

  Mesken, which has protested each of these matters in diplomatic notes, has filed an application against Halvia in the ICJ alleging breaches of the ICCPR. It asks the Court (i) to indicate provisional measures ordering the release of the Camp Orl detainees and the opening of gates in the fence for access to the hospital, and (ii) at the merits stage, to order the dismantling of the fence and compensation.

  Advise Mesken on: (a) the Court's jurisdiction and the request for provisional measures; (b) whether Halvia's conduct in the valley breaches the ICCPR and engages its responsibility; (c) the weight of the Committee's views; and (d) the remedies Mesken can obtain.
model: |-
  **Issue**

  The issues are: (a) whether the ICJ has jurisdiction and will indicate the provisional measures sought; (b) whether the ICCPR applies in the occupied valley, and whether Halvia is responsible for the detentions, the militia's burnings and the fence; (c) the weight of the Human Rights Committee's views; and (d) the remedies available.

  **Rule**

  The ICJ's jurisdiction rests on consent, which both states have given by optional clause declarations, and a dispute must exist when proceedings begin. Under art 41 of its Statute the Court may indicate provisional measures where it has prima facie jurisdiction, the rights claimed are plausible, the measures are linked to them, and there is a real and imminent risk of irreparable harm; such measures bind (*LaGrand*).

  States must respect the rights of people within their territory and subject to their jurisdiction, irrespective of nationality (ICCPR art 2(1)). Jurisdiction may be exercised outside a state's territory, and the drafters "did not intend to allow States to escape from their obligations when they exercise jurisdiction outside their national territory" (*Wall*, para 109). In *Armed Activities* Uganda, as occupying Power in Ituri, was responsible both for its military's acts and "for any lack of vigilance in preventing violations" by other actors in the occupied territory, "including rebel groups acting on their own account" (para 179); human rights instruments apply to acts done in the exercise of jurisdiction abroad, particularly in occupied territories (para 216). Positive obligations require protection against private persons as well as state agents (General Comment 31, para 8).

  Conduct of state organs is attributable to the state (art 4 ARSIWA); a group's conduct is attributable only on the state's instructions or under its effective control (art 8; *Nicaragua*; *Genocide*, 2007), or if it acts in "complete dependence" on the state. A breach may be an act or an omission (art 2). Necessity precludes wrongfulness only where the act is the only way to safeguard an essential interest against a grave and imminent peril (art 25). A responsible state must cease a continuing wrong and offer assurances of non-repetition (art 30), and make full reparation (art 31) by restitution, compensation or satisfaction. Treaty body views are not binding, but the ICJ gives the Committee's interpretation "great weight" (*Diallo*, para 66).

  **Application**

  *(a) Jurisdiction and provisional measures.* Both states have accepted the Court's jurisdiction under art 36(2) for legal disputes with states accepting the same obligation, so prima facie jurisdiction exists. A dispute existed when Mesken filed: Mesken protested each matter in diplomatic notes, and Halvia's denial that the ICCPR applies, or that it is responsible, shows positively opposed views of which it was aware. The rights claimed are plausible — arts 7, 9 and 10 for the detainees, and freedom of movement (art 12) and life (art 6) for those cut off from the only hospital — and each measure is linked to them. There is urgency: detainees held without lawyers and beaten in interrogation, and civilians without access to a hospital, face a real and imminent risk of irreparable harm. The Court is likely to indicate measures of this kind, and they will bind Halvia (*LaGrand*).

  *(b) Does the Covenant apply in the valley?* Yes. Halvia occupies and administers the valley through a military governor, so the people there are subject to its jurisdiction, though they are outside its territory and are not its nationals (art 2(1)). *Wall* (para 109) and *Armed Activities* (para 216) apply the Covenant to acts done in the exercise of jurisdiction abroad, particularly in occupied territory. Halvia's first argument fails.

  *Camp Orl.* The soldiers are state organs, so the detentions and beatings are attributable to Halvia (art 4). Detention without charge or access to lawyers breaches arts 9 and 10, and beatings in interrogation engage art 7, which can never be derogated from. Halvia has proclaimed no emergency, so it cannot rely on art 4 for any of these measures.

  *The Valley Guard.* Halvia arms and pays the militia, but its own leaders command it, so it is neither a de facto organ acting in complete dependence on Halvia nor under Halvia's effective control in the burnings; financing and arming are not enough (*Nicaragua*; art 8). The burnings are not attributable to Halvia as such. But that does not end Halvia's responsibility. As occupying Power it is responsible for any lack of vigilance in preventing violations by other actors in the occupied territory, including groups acting on their own account (*Armed Activities*, para 179), and it must protect individuals against private actors (General Comment 31, para 8). Halvian patrols watched the burnings without intervening: an omission by state organs, attributable to Halvia, breaching its own obligations (art 2). Halvia's second argument fails.

  *The fence.* Cutting 4,000 farmers off from their fields and from the only hospital restricts freedom of movement (art 12) and endangers lives. With no derogation, the restriction must be provided by law, serve a legitimate aim and be necessary in a democratic society. Security against raids may be a legitimate aim, but cutting civilians off from their livelihoods and the only hospital is unlikely to be proportionate. Halvia's plea that the fence is "necessary" fares no better as a ground precluding wrongfulness: necessity requires that the act be the only way to safeguard an essential interest against a grave and imminent peril, and in the *Wall* opinion construction of a barrier in occupied territory was held not to be necessary.

  *(c) The Committee's views.* The views do not bind, but the Covenant does, and the Committee is the body established to supervise it, so the ICJ would ascribe "great weight" to its findings (*Diallo*, para 66).

  *(d) Remedies.* The breaches are continuing, so Halvia must cease them — release or lawfully process the detainees, protect residents from the Valley Guard, and stop obstructing access — and offer assurances of non-repetition given its denials (art 30). It must make full reparation (art 31). Restitution comes first: as in the *Wall* opinion, where Israel had to stop construction, dismantle what had been built and compensate affected persons, Mesken can seek the fence's dismantling. Compensation covers financially assessable damage, such as lost harvests and homes burned while Halvian patrols failed to act. A declaration of wrongfulness provides satisfaction. Mesken is directly injured, as the violations occur on its territory and against its nationals.

  **Conclusion**

  The Court has prima facie jurisdiction under the two optional clause declarations, and it is likely to indicate binding provisional measures for the detainees and for hospital access (*LaGrand*). The ICCPR applies in the occupied valley (*Wall*; *Armed Activities*). Halvia is responsible for the detentions and beatings by its soldiers (arts 7, 9, 10) and, although the Valley Guard's burnings are not attributable to it, for its own failure to prevent them (*Armed Activities*, para 179; General Comment 31). The fence is a disproportionate restriction of movement that necessity does not excuse. The Committee's views are not binding but carry great weight (*Diallo*). Mesken can seek cessation, assurances, the dismantling of the fence and compensation (arts 30–31).
