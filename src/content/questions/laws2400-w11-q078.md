model: |-
  The issue is whether the lightning strike breaks the chain of causation between Dee's negligence and Eli's back injury, taking that injury outside the scope of Dee's liability under CLA s 5D(1)(b).

  'But for' causation may be satisfied: Eli was in bed only because of the broken leg. But the novus actus branch starts from cases where 'but for' causation is established and later events affect the situation the defendant created. The question is whether the later event severs the connection. The Oropesa asks whether it is a new cause which disturbs the sequence of events, extraneous or extrinsic. To sever the connection, the intervening event must ordinarily be voluntary human action, or a causally independent act which is a coincidence which is extremely unlikely (Haber v Walker).

  The lightning strike is not human action at all, so the first limb does not apply. But it fits the second: it is causally independent of the accident — nothing Dee did made lightning more likely — and a freak strike felling a healthy tree is an extremely unlikely coincidence. It is an extraneous, extrinsic cause.

  Conclusion: the strike is likely to sever the chain, so the back injury probably falls outside the scope of Dee's liability, although Dee remains liable for the broken leg.
