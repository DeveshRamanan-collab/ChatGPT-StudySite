  **Thesis**

  The statement is half right. Formal equality was feminism's greatest legal achievement in that it dismantled the 'separate spheres' in law and gave women a legal claim to participate in public life. But calling it a theoretical mistake goes too far. The mistake was not the demand for equal treatment, on which later feminisms and critical race theory continue to rely, but two assumptions that came with it: that male experience is the benchmark against which equal treatment is measured, and that equality is a matter for the public sphere only. Both can be corrected without abandoning formal equality. The better verdict is that formal equality was necessary but not sufficient.

  **Argument**

  *The achievement.* Justice Bradley's opinion in *Bradwell v Illinois* shows what liberal feminism was up against. "The civil law, as well as nature herself", he wrote, recognised "a wide difference in the respective spheres and destinies of man and woman", and the family indicated "the domestic sphere as that which properly belongs to the domain and functions of womanhood". The passage maps the respective spheres onto the distinction between public and private, and ties them to the family. Liberal feminism replied that individuals should be assessed on their merits, not on their membership of a group, and that the solution to women's oppression was to remove all formal barriers to their equal participation in social, economic and political life. Most of the legal gains of the 1960s and 1970s were won on this basis, including the Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth), which prohibits direct discrimination on the basis of sex in employment, housing and the provision of goods and services. Against reasoning that treated women's exclusion as natural and divinely ordained, the demand for equal treatment was the necessary first move.

  *The limits.* The criticisms are real. Formal equality did not translate into substantive equality. It failed to address how discrimination on grounds unique to women, such as pregnancy, could count as discrimination at all. It reached direct discrimination (treatment) more easily than indirect discrimination (impact), and public more easily than private discrimination. Not all women could take advantage of their equal rights. Above all, it implicitly assumed male experiences and perspectives as the norm. The lecture connects this with the realists' distinction between paper rules and real rules: a statute that guarantees rights to everyone does not mean that every group can take advantage of them. Law in books is not law in action.

  *The parallel in critical race theory.* Critical race theory makes the same argument about race. It rejects the idea that race is an irrelevant category and that racial equality involves only extending equal treatment, because racism can coexist with formal "objectivity" and "neutrality"; ideals such as meritocracy, equal opportunity and colourblindness can obscure structural racism. Moreton-Robinson shows that the problem recurs inside feminism: white Australian feminists' demands left out Indigenous women's claims to maternity allowances, voting rights and labour rights. Equal treatment measured against a benchmark — male, or white — reproduces the benchmark.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the second limb of the statement is that the error lies in formal equality itself. Difference feminism says that formal equality forces women to assimilate to patriarchal structures, acting and behaving like men; Gilligan's ethics of care stands beside the ethics of justice that the law already reflects. Radical feminism goes further. For MacKinnon, women do not so much speak with a different voice as have no voice at all, because women as a class are dominated by men as a class. On this view, equal treatment between individuals cannot reach a domination that operates between classes, and the separate spheres it attacked in public survived in private. 'The personal is the political': private inequality is the source of public disadvantage, and the family is a political institution. The examples Naffine gives of law that is not impartial — sexual assault law, the defence of provocation, the marital rape exception, the historical reluctance to intervene in domestic violence — are all cases where formally equal law left private power untouched.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument shows that formal equality is insufficient, not that it was a mistake. First, each alternative carries costs. Difference feminism can reinforce the stereotypes — the "natural and proper timidity and delicacy" of Justice Bradley's opinion — that were the main cause of women's subordination. Radical feminism risks seeing women only as victims, which makes it hard to move from analysis to action. Postmodern feminism's rejection of essentialism meets Naffine's questions: at what point do we know enough about each woman's circumstances, and how can women's situation improve if feminists will not speak for women? Some feminists fear that it leads only to political paralysis. Change needs a critical mass, and that was the strength of the liberal approach.

  Secondly, the later critiques depend on equality. The complaint that formal equality assumes a male norm is a complaint that the law does not yet treat women as equals; Moreton-Robinson's complaint is that some women's rights were left out. The practical achievements the lecture lists — the provocation defence tightened, the marital rape exception removed, the NSW consent reforms of 2021 — extend equal protection into the private sphere; they do not reject it. That is why the better target is the separation of the spheres rather than equality itself. As the lecture puts it, the two spheres can be distinguished without being separate; what feminism rejects is the tradition of confining claims about justice to the public sphere.

  So the statement should be qualified. Formal equality was a great legal achievement and remains a premise of every later critique. Its theoretical failure was to take a benchmark and a boundary for granted — the male norm and the public–private divide — and the later feminisms, together with critical race theory, are best read as correcting those assumptions rather than refuting equality.
