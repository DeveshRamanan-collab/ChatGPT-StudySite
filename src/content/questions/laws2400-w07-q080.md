model: |-
  The issue is whether an auditor owes a lender, relying on audited accounts, a duty of care to avoid pure economic
  loss. Northgate's loss is PEL — money lost with no physical injury or property damage. Reasonable foreseeability is
  present: it is foreseeable that someone relying on audited accounts might suffer loss. But, as Dawson J said in
  Esanda Finance Corp v PMH, mere foreseeability does not, where the only harm is PEL, give rise to a duty; the law
  requires a special relationship. The facts closely resemble Esanda, where a financier relied on audited accounts and
  no duty was found. Weighing the salient features: there was no request by Northgate; Lowe & Co did not know of
  Northgate or its reliance and had no intention to induce it to lend; there was no assumption of responsibility
  toward lenders; and the purpose of an audit is to inform the company and its shareholders, not lenders. McHugh J's
  policy reasons also tell against a duty — indeterminate liability (accounts can be read by anyone), higher auditing
  costs, more complex litigation and difficulty proving reliance. Lowe & Co's special skill does not outweigh these
  features. Conclusion: following Esanda, Lowe & Co very likely owed Northgate no duty of care.
