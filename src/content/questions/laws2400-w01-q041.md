  Tort law differs from criminal law in at least two ways. First, the nature of the wrong: tort deals with
  private wrongs between individuals, while criminal law deals with public wrongs (offences) against the
  state. Second, the main purpose and principle of justice differ: tort aims at compensation and redress or
  correction of the victim's loss, while criminal law aims at punishment through retribution or deterrence.
  These differences also show up procedurally — a tort action is initiated by the individual and proved on the
  balance of probabilities, whereas a criminal prosecution is initiated by the State and proved beyond
  reasonable doubt. The line is not always sharp, however: the lecture gives assault as an example of conduct
  that can amount to both a tort and a crime, and notes that compensation may sometimes be obtained for
  injuries arising from a crime.

  Tort law differs from contract law in the source and scope of duties. Contractual duties arise from the
  parties' own agreement (and the law), and bind only those parties (privity is generally required); tort
  duties arise from the general law itself, such as the neighbour principle, and are owed more broadly, not
  only to parties who have agreed to anything. This is why product liability, for example, can generate claims
  in both contract (between the parties to a sale) and tort (owed more generally to anyone foreseeably
  affected).
