model: "The position for custom is less settled than for treaties. Under the doctrine of transformation, customary international law only becomes part of Australian law when formally adopted by Parliament or the courts as part of the common law. Under the doctrine of incorporation, it is automatically a source of Australian law unless there is a rule to the contrary. Australia has not clearly adopted either approach, and both have been recognised in High Court decisions. Under either, a customary rule cannot generally be adopted where inconsistent with domestic legislation or essential common law doctrine. In Nulyarimma v Thompson, Aboriginal applicants argued that genocide, as customary international law, could be tried in Australian courts although the Genocide Convention had not been transformed. The Federal Court held that even if genocide is part of customary international law, and even accepting its prohibition is jus cogens, it is not part of Australian law without legislation declaring it a crime; Wilcox J contrasted civil rules like foreign sovereign immunity with criminalising norms.
