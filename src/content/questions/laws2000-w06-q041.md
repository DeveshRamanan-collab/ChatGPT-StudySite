  A state-owned shipping company of State P contracts to carry grain to a buyer in State Q. After a change of
  government in State Q, State P orders the ship home for foreign-policy reasons. The buyer sues in the courts of

model: |
  The issue is whether State P's conduct is a sovereign act (jure imperii), attracting immunity, or a commercial act
  (jure gestionis), which does not. State immunity was once absolute, but custom now follows the restrictive
  approach: the Empire of Iran Case (1963) held absolute immunity was no longer customary law, and Trendex (1977)
  denied immunity to a state organ in a commercial transaction. Art 10 of the 2004 Convention, reflecting custom
  though not in force, removes immunity in proceedings arising out of a commercial transaction with a foreign
  natural or juridical person, unless the transaction is between states or the parties agreed otherwise.

  A contract of carriage is a contract for the supply of services under art 2(1)(c). Under art 2(2) we look
  primarily at its nature; purpose counts only if the parties so agreed. In Playa Largo (1983) Cuba's diversion of
  a sugar cargo after the coup in Chile was held commercial despite its foreign-policy objective. The same applies
  here: the recall breaches a commercial contract, and the political motive does not change its nature. State P is
  likely not immune, although R's courts may note that about one-third of states reject the distinction.
