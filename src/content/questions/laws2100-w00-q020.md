  **Thesis**

  The statement is partly right. The harm principle cannot justify prohibiting voluntary active euthanasia for an adult of sound mind who asks for it: the person who dies is the only person harmed, and they consent. Nor can Feinberg's offence principle fill the gap. But it does not follow that the prohibition is legal moralism in disguise. A defence that does not appeal to morality as such is available, paternalism, and the slides themselves list prohibiting people from consenting to their own violent killing as an example of the soft paternalism all states employ. The real question is not whether the prohibition is moralism but whether paternalism of this kind is compatible with liberalism. I argue that a liberal defence of the present law stands or falls on the distinction between voluntary and non-voluntary choice, not on the distinction between passive and active euthanasia that the law actually draws.

  **Argument**

  *Harm.* Mill allows power to be exercised over a member of a civilised community against their will only "to prevent harm to others". Harm is narrow: not merely affecting another's interests, but infringing interests we are under a duty not to infringe. Behaviour that harms only the actor, or harms others with their consent, should not be punished, however "foolish, perverse, or wrong". Voluntary euthanasia, as the materials define it, is carried out at the request of an adult person of sound mind. The person killed consents, and no third party's protected interests are infringed. On Mill's terms the state exceeds its proper limits here, just as it would if it stopped A from destroying her own property. The slide's two questions, whether the choice of voluntary active euthanasia is harmful and whether it violates the rights of others, both seem to get the answer no.

  *Offence.* Feinberg's offence principle does not help the prohibition either. It protects persons other than the actor from serious offence, and only where two tests are met: the reaction must be one expected from almost anyone chosen at random, and the conduct must not be reasonably avoidable. A medical decision made at a patient's request is easily avoided by anyone who disapproves of it, and disapproval of it is far from universal.

  *Paternalism.* Legal paternalism justifies coercion to protect people from self-inflicted harm, or to guide them toward their own good whether they like it or not. Both the harm and the offence principles reject it. Yet all states employ some soft paternalism: they prohibit consent to one's own disablement or violent killing, and the law of contract forbids selling oneself into slavery. The prohibition of active euthanasia fits that pattern. The rationale for paternalism weighs five factors: the probability that harm to oneself will result, the seriousness of the harm, the probability that the goal will in fact be reached, the value of the goal, and whether there is a less risky way of reaching it. Death is certain and as serious as harm gets. But the goal, an end to a life the person no longer wants to live, is also certain to be reached, and its value is precisely what the person claims the right to judge.

  *The line between refusing and requesting.* The law's distinction follows the distinction between passive and active euthanasia. A competent adult may refuse life-sustaining treatment: that is control over whether others must act on one's body, the kind of right the will theory describes (week 9). A request to be killed asks another person to perform a positive act of killing. The lecture said that these distinctions track different situations that may make a moral difference. The question is whether the harm principle recognises the difference. It is hard to see how: in both cases the person dies by their own choice, and in neither is anyone else harmed.

  *Moralism.* Devlin needed no disguise. He cited the prohibition of voluntary euthanasia as proof that the criminal law punishes harmless conduct in order to enforce a moral principle, the sanctity of human life. If no liberal argument can explain the line, the statement is right that what remains is Devlin's reason.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest objection is that the harm principle, taken seriously, has limits of its own that support the prohibition. Mill said the doctrine "is meant to apply only to human beings in the maturity of their faculties". The materials separate voluntary euthanasia from non-voluntary euthanasia, performed on someone not capable of understanding the choice between life and death. A rule permitting active killing on request would be applied to people at the end of life, some of whom may not be capable of that understanding; the prohibition protects those outside Mill's principle. Second, the critics' objections to the harm principle bite here: no person's affairs have effects only on that person, and the principle ignores indirect harms. A death affects others. Third, the passive–active distinction marks a difference in who acts. Refusing treatment asks others to refrain; requesting death asks another person to kill, and that person's conduct is conduct towards someone else.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument is strongest where it relies on the voluntary–non-voluntary distinction and weakest where it relies on the passive–active one. Mill's maturity qualifier is a genuinely liberal reason for caution: the harm principle protects choices, and it cannot protect a choice that is not really made. But that reason supports rules ensuring that a request is voluntary, not a blanket prohibition of every request by an adult of sound mind. The indirect-harm objection proves too much: if a person's death harms those close to them, so does a refusal of treatment, which the law permits. And the point about who acts does not make the doctor's act harmful in Mill's sense, since the person on whom it is performed consents to it.

  That leaves paternalism, and Hart's two questions sharpen the choice. Is the conduct harmful, independently of its repercussion on the general moral code? To the person who dies, yes, but by their own choice. Is it true that failing to criminalise it would jeopardise the whole fabric of morality and society? Nothing in the materials suggests so. A paternalist who defends the prohibition must therefore claim that the state may override a competent adult's judgement about the value of their own life. The slides call prohibitions on consenting to one's own violent killing soft paternalism, but overriding that judgement is the state intervening for the person's own good, which is how the slides describe hard paternalism. Whatever label it carries, it sits uneasily with Mill's view that over their own body and mind the individual is sovereign.

  So the statement is partly right. The ban cannot be defended by the harm principle, and Devlin's sanctity-of-life rationale is moralism. But the best defence is paternalist, not moralist, and its liberal credentials depend on the voluntary–non-voluntary distinction rather than on the line the law actually draws between refusing and requesting.
