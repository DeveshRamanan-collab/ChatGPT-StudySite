model: |
  Conceptual analysis is a necessary method for general jurisprudence but not, by itself, an adequate one. It has
  been a key method of analytical philosophy, and Hart imported it into jurisprudence. Conceptual theories generally
  define terms by necessary and sufficient conditions and so set the boundaries of categories; as Bix says, drawing
  them can establish common ground for investigation and discussion. Without that common ground, disputes collapse
  into semantic confusion, like the football/soccer example. The counterargument is that the placement of the
  boundaries is often contested, and Dickson reports sceptics who doubt conceptual analysis as a method at all. The
  better evaluation is Twining's: conceptual analysis "has an important part to play in reviving general
  jurisprudence", but what is needed is "a much broader conception" than Hart's or Raz's, one that also serves
  cross-cultural discourse, empirical legal studies and the social sciences. So analysis is adequate only if it is
  widened: contestation is a reason to do it more carefully and more broadly, not to abandon it.
