  Using the right to self-defence as an example, explain the three ways a treaty rule and a rule of customary

model: |
  A rule of international law can be contained in a treaty and be a rule of custom simultaneously; the two are
  not mutually exclusive. This week's material, drawing on Nicaragua v United States of America and North Sea
  Continental Shelf, identifies three relationships. First, a treaty may codify a pre-existing rule of custom,
  simply writing down in treaty form an obligation that already independently bound states through practice
  and opinio juris. Second, a treaty may crystallise a developing rule of custom: by prompting an intense
  practice among states around the treaty's terms, the treaty helps a still-forming customary rule complete
  its formation. Third, a treaty may give rise to an entirely new rule of customary international law, where a
  genuinely new issue is addressed by the treaty and the practice generated around it develops into custom in
  its own right, independently of the treaty.

  The right to self-defence illustrates why this matters. It exists as a rule of customary international law,
  and it is also expressly recognised in Article 51 of the UN Charter as a right of individual or collective
  self-defence. Because the customary and treaty versions coexist rather than one displacing the other, a
  state that is not a party to the UN Charter would still have a right of self-defence, since it can rely on
  the customary rule independently. The two versions need not even be identical: the rule found in the treaty
  may carry a certain specification, while the rule that has evolved through custom may carry a different
  one, so the source relied upon can affect the exact content of the right being asserted, even though both
  trace back to the same underlying idea.
