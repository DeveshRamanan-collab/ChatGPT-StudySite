  Rostam Vell was President of Kaldera from 2016 until he lost an election in 2024. Human rights investigators have documented that between 2019 and 2022, on orders issued by Vell as President, Kalderan security forces systematically tortured hundreds of political prisoners in state detention centres. Kaldera and Australia have both been parties to the Convention against Torture since the 1990s. Kaldera has not prosecuted anyone.

  In March 2026 Vell arrived in Sydney on a private visit, accompanied by Ines Moro, Kaldera's current Minister for Foreign Affairs. Evidence suggests that, as Kaldera's Minister of the Interior in 2021, Moro personally directed the enforced disappearance of several opposition journalists. The Commonwealth Director of Public Prosecutions considers that the conduct of both could amount to crimes against humanity within Division 268 of the Criminal Code Act 1995 (Cth).

  A group of torture survivors, some of whom are now Australian citizens, wants three things. First, that Vell and Moro be arrested and prosecuted in Australia. Second, and failing that, that the Federal Court declare that Vell has committed "the customary international crime of torture", even if no Australian statute applies. Third, they have sued the State of Kaldera in the Supreme Court of New South Wales for damages for their torture in Kalderan detention centres. Kaldera has not appeared in the civil proceedings but has sent a diplomatic note asserting immunity. Kaldera has also formally demanded Vell's and Moro's immediate departure, insisting that both are protected by immunity "as a matter of customary international law".

  **Issue**

  The issues are: (1) whether Australia can prosecute Vell and Moro, which depends on Australian jurisdiction and on their immunities; (2) whether the Federal Court can declare a "customary international crime of torture" without legislation; and (3) whether Kaldera is immune from the survivors' civil claim.

  **Rule**

  Universal jurisdiction allows any state to act over offences so serious that no direct link is required, including crimes against humanity and torture. Without a treaty it is controversial, and states usually require the accused's presence. Division 268 of the Criminal Code provides for universal jurisdiction over genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity, and usually requires the person's presence in Australia.

  Immunity is procedural, and criminal responsibility is substantive: "immunity ... does not mean ... impunity". Personal immunity attaches to the office of head of state, head of government and foreign minister; it is complete, covering private and official acts, but lasts only while in office (*Arrest Warrant*; *Djibouti v France*). In *Arrest Warrant*, a Belgian warrant for the sitting Congolese foreign minister breached his immunity, and state practice supported no exception for international crimes. Prosecution remains possible in the person's own state, after waiver, by other states once they leave office for acts before or after office and private acts during it, and before international criminal courts. Functional immunity attaches to official acts and continues after office. But in *Pinochet (No 3)* the House of Lords held that a former head of state has no immunity for acts that, when done, were international crimes subject to a form of universal jurisdiction — torture under the Convention against Torture, to which the relevant states were parties. ILC draft article 7 (2017) would exclude immunity for torture, crimes against humanity and enforced disappearances, among others, but whether it reflects state practice is debated.

  In Australian law, a customary criminal norm needs legislation: in *Nulyarimma v Thompson* genocide, though customary and jus cogens, was not an offence in Australian law without legislation (Wilcox J).

  State immunity is grounded in sovereign equality and custom; the 2004 Convention is not in force but largely reflects custom, and the *Foreign States Immunities Act 1985* (Cth) is similar. A state is immune from the jurisdiction of another state's courts (arts 5–6) unless it consents or participates (arts 7–9) or an exception applies. Article 12 removes immunity for death or injury caused in the forum state with the author present. In *Germany v Italy* the ICJ held that state immunity is "procedural in character" and "entirely distinct from the substantive law" (para 93), and custom did not remove Germany's immunity in civil proceedings over serious crimes.

  **Application**

  *(1) Prosecution — jurisdiction.* The alleged crimes occurred in Kaldera, by Kalderan officials, against Kalderan detainees at the time. There is no territorial or active nationality link, and passive personality is doubtful since the victims became Australian citizens only later. The relevant ground is universality. Division 268 expressly provides for universal jurisdiction over crimes against humanity, and both Vell and Moro are physically present in Australia, satisfying the usual presence requirement. Australia therefore has prescriptive jurisdiction under its own legislation.

  *Vell's immunity.* Vell left office in 2024, so his personal immunity has ended. What remains is functional immunity for official acts, and the torture was ordered by him as President, using state security forces. Ordinarily functional immunity would cover such acts. But *Pinochet (No 3)* holds that a former head of state has no immunity for acts that, when done, were international crimes subject to a form of universal jurisdiction — torture under the Convention against Torture, to which the states concerned were parties. Here, Kaldera and Australia were both parties to that Convention throughout 2019–2022. The *Pinochet* reasoning applies closely, and Vell's functional immunity is unlikely to bar prosecution. ILC draft article 7 supports the same result, though its status as custom is debated. Kaldera's demand for his departure is not a legal bar: it could waive immunity but has not done so, and the *Pinochet* principle does not depend on waiver.

  *Moro's immunity.* Moro is Kaldera's sitting foreign minister. She has personal immunity, which is complete while she holds office and covers acts from before she took office, including her conduct as Interior Minister in 2021 (*Arrest Warrant*; *Djibouti v France*). *Arrest Warrant* confirms that state practice recognises no exception to a serving foreign minister's personal immunity even for international crimes, and even the issue of a warrant can breach it. She cannot be arrested or prosecuted in Australia while she remains foreign minister. That is not impunity: she may be prosecuted in Kaldera, after waiver, by Australia once she leaves office (for acts before her term), or before an international criminal court with jurisdiction.

  *(2) A declaration of a customary crime.* The survivors' fallback fails. *Nulyarimma* holds that a customary criminal norm — even one with jus cogens status, such as genocide — does not form part of Australian law without legislation declaring it a crime. Criminal norms are different. A court cannot declare Vell guilty of a "customary international crime of torture" without a statute. The proper avenue is a prosecution under Division 268, which is available.

  *(3) The civil claim against Kaldera.* State immunity is a preliminary question. Kaldera has not submitted to the jurisdiction, participated in the proceedings (art 8) or expressly consented (art 7); its diplomatic note asserting immunity is not participation. No commercial transaction is involved, so the FSIA's commercial exception does not apply. Article 12's territorial-tort exception requires that the injury occur in the forum state with the author present, but the torture occurred in Kaldera. The survivors may argue that immunity should give way for torture, a jus cogens violation. *Germany v Italy* rejects that argument: state immunity is procedural and distinct from the substantive wrong, and custom did not deprive Germany of immunity in civil proceedings concerning serious crimes. The *Pinochet* exception concerns individual criminal responsibility, not the civil immunity of the state itself. The NSW proceedings will therefore be barred by Kaldera's immunity.

  **Conclusion**

  Australia can prosecute Vell under Division 268's universal jurisdiction, since he is present, and his functional immunity for official acts of torture is unlikely to bar prosecution, following *Pinochet (No 3)*, given both states' participation in the Convention against Torture. Moro, as a serving foreign minister, has complete personal immunity and cannot be arrested or prosecuted while in office (*Arrest Warrant*), though she may be prosecuted after she leaves office. A declaration of a "customary crime" is unavailable without legislation (*Nulyarimma*). The civil claim against Kaldera is barred by state immunity: no exception applies, and *Germany v Italy* confirms that even serious violations do not displace a state's procedural immunity.
