  **Issue**

  Three issues arise: (a) whether Velmora's sinking of the Tamsin, or its support for the Northern Front, was an "armed attack" giving Ardenia a right of self-defence; (b) whether Ardenia's March strike at Port Ovin and its July operations met the conditions of self-defence; and (c) whether the ICJ will indicate the provisional measures Velmora seeks.

  **Rule**

  Art 2(4) of the UN Charter prohibits "the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state". The prohibition is also customary international law (*Nicaragua*, para 34) and a jus cogens norm. Self-defence under art 51 is one of its two exceptions: "the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs". Three conditions apply: (1) an armed attack; (2) a necessary and proportionate response — conditions confirmed as custom in *Nicaragua*; and (3) immediate reporting to the Security Council. Lawful self-defence precludes wrongfulness (art 21 ARSIWA).

  An armed attack is one of "the most grave forms of the use of force" (*Nicaragua*); not every use of force is an armed attack. The threshold depends on scale and effects. An attack on a state's military vessel can be an armed attack (*Oil Platforms*). In *Nicaragua* the provision of arms, funds or logistical support to armed groups remained below the threshold, and the contras' acts were not attributable to the United States without "effective control" of the operations (art 8 ARSIWA).

  Necessity means force must be a last resort; one indicator is the temporal link — measures taking effect "several months after" the alleged attack were not undertaken "in the light of necessity" (*Nicaragua* [237]). Proportionality is measured against the attack itself or against the purpose of halting or preventing further attack. The Court "cannot close its eyes to the scale of the whole operation" (*Oil Platforms* [77]), and taking airports and towns "many hundreds of kilometres" from the border was not proportionate (*Armed Activities* [147]). Reporting is not a condition of lawfulness in the same way, but a failure to report may indicate that the state did not itself believe it was acting in self-defence (*Nicaragua*).

  The ICJ's jurisdiction rests on consent, including declarations under art 36(2) (the optional clause). Under art 41 the Court may indicate provisional measures if the absence of jurisdiction is not manifest, the rights claimed are plausible, the measures are linked to those rights, and there is a real and imminent risk of irreparable harm. Such measures bind (*LaGrand* [102]).

  **Application**

  *(a) Was there an armed attack?* The missile attack on the Tamsin was a use of force by Velmora's navy, a state organ (art 4 ARSIWA). Its scale and effects were serious — a warship sunk and 31 sailors killed — and the target was a military vessel in Ardenia's territorial sea. *Oil Platforms* indicates that such an attack can be an armed attack, and this was no minor "frontier incident". Velmora's unsupported claim that the corvette had strayed does not change this. The sinking was an armed attack.

  Velmora's support for the Northern Front is different. Supplying rifles, funds and training is exactly the kind of assistance *Nicaragua* placed below the gravity threshold. Nor is the Front's conduct attributable to Velmora: there is no evidence that Velmoran officers plan or direct its operations, so Velmora lacks effective control of them (art 8). Whatever the other legal consequences of that support, it gave Ardenia no right of self-defence.

  *(b) The March strike.* Ardenia acted two days after the attack, against the squadron that had fired the missiles, at a base 40 kilometres from the island. The temporal link is close. With the squadron still operational there was a real risk of further attacks, which a diplomatic protest would not have removed, so force was a last resort. The strike was confined to the units responsible and caused four deaths, against 31 Ardenian deaths. It was proportionate on either approach: commensurate with the attack, and aimed at preventing a repetition. Ardenia reported it to the Security Council the same day. The March strike was lawful self-defence, and its wrongfulness is precluded (art 21 ARSIWA).

  *The July operations.* These fail every condition. On necessity, they began four months after the armed attack, while a ceasefire was holding — the kind of delay that defeated the United States' argument in *Nicaragua* [237]. The mediation had stalled, not ended, and peaceful means remained available. On proportionality, occupying a city 350 kilometres from the island and destroying the national power station bears no relation to repelling the sinking of one corvette or preventing a repeat. Like the taking of towns "many hundreds of kilometres" from the border in *Armed Activities*, their geographic and destructive scope is excessive, and the Court would look at the scale of the whole operation (*Oil Platforms*). The Defence Minister's words reveal a punitive purpose — to "teach Velmora" a lesson and to stop its support for the Front, which itself was not an armed attack. Punishment and coercion are not the purposes of self-defence. Finally, Ardenia did not report the July operations. That is not fatal on its own, but under *Nicaragua* it suggests Ardenia did not regard them as self-defence. The July operations breach art 2(4).

  *(c) Provisional measures.* Ardenia's first objection fails: the Court need not finally decide its jurisdiction first. It is enough that the absence of jurisdiction is not manifest, and both states have made art 36(2) declarations. A dispute existed when proceedings began, since Ardenia's claim of self-defence and Velmora's claim of unlawful force are positively opposed. The rights Velmora asserts — to be free of unlawful force and occupation — are plausible on the analysis above, and withdrawal from Lorvan and an end to attacks on infrastructure are directly linked to them. A continuing occupation and attacks on power infrastructure create a real and imminent risk of irreparable harm to Velmora's population before judgment. Ardenia's second objection also fails: in *LaGrand* the Court held that provisional measures are binding, since anything else would defeat the object and purpose of art 41.

  **Conclusion**

  Velmora's sinking of the Tamsin was an armed attack; its arming and funding of the Northern Front was not, and the Front's conduct is not attributable to Velmora. Ardenia's March strike was necessary, proportionate and reported, and so lawful self-defence. Its July operations were neither necessary nor proportionate, served a punitive purpose and went unreported: they are an unlawful use of force under art 2(4). The ICJ is likely to find prima facie jurisdiction under the two optional clause declarations and to indicate provisional measures, which will bind Ardenia. Both states also remain bound by art 2(3) to keep seeking a peaceful settlement of the Kessel Island dispute.
