  Ridgeline Golf Club hosted a corporate golf day. Sam, a groundsman employed by GreenEdge Turf Pty Ltd, a contractor that maintains the course, was mowing the approach to the 7th green, which lies beyond a blind crest about 220 metres from the 6th tee.

  Priya and Mark were playing together. Standing on the 6th tee, both could see the top of Sam's mower over the crest, and Mark joked that they should "give the mower a fright". They had a $50 bet on the longer drive. Rather than waiting for Sam to move on, they agreed to tee off at the same time "to keep things moving". Neither called "fore". Both hit identical balls of the same brand and model, supplied in the club's corporate gift pack, in the same direction. One ball came over the crest and struck Sam in the left eye; the other landed nearby. Nobody saw which ball hit Sam, and there is no way to tell. Sam has permanently lost the sight in that eye.

  A sign on the 6th tee tells players to wait for a bell, rung by a member of staff on the crest, before driving. The bell had been broken for a month, and the club had not repaired it, although a repair would have cost $150. Sam's lawyers have an expert who says that a working bell "might have" made the players wait. Priya and Mark both admit they knew the mower was there.

  Sam's claim is not a dust disease or tobacco claim. Assume that Priya, Mark and the club each owed Sam a duty of care, and that the club breached its duty by failing to repair the bell. Do not consider defences.

  Advise Sam on his claims in negligence against Priya, Mark and the club, including whether Priya and Mark breached their duties, whether each defendant's negligence caused his injury, and from whom he could recover.
model: |
  **Issue**

  Four questions arise: (1) whether Priya and Mark breached their duties; (2) whether Sam can establish factual causation against either when no one can say whose ball hit him, and if not, whether the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") s 5D(2) assists; (3) whether the broken bell caused his injury; and (4) from whom he could recover.

  **Rule**

  Under s 5B, a person is not negligent unless the risk was foreseeable (not far-fetched or fanciful: Wyong Shire Council v Shirt (1980) 146 CLR 40), not insignificant, and a reasonable person would have taken precautions, considering probability, seriousness, burden and social utility.

  Under s 5D(1), causation comprises (a) factual causation — a necessary condition of the harm — and (b) scope of liability. Section 5D(1)(a) is a statutory statement of the "but for" test (Strong v Woolworths Ltd (2012) 246 CLR 182), and the plaintiff must prove causation on the balance of probabilities (s 5E). If the harm would have occurred anyway, the negligence is not a cause (Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital [1969] 1 QB 428), and that a precaution "might have" made a difference does not prove factual causation (Adeels Palace v Moubarak (2009) 239 CLR 420).

  The "but for" test fails with multiple sufficient causes (March v Stramare (1991) 171 CLR 506, Mason CJ). Courts have responded differently: in Cook v Lewis (1961) the Supreme Court of Canada held both negligent hunters liable when the jury could not decide whose gun fired the single bullet. Section 5D(2) provides that "in an exceptional case, in accordance with established principles", where negligence cannot be established as a necessary condition, the court is to consider "whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed on the negligent party". It has rarely been considered and its operation is uncertain; Adeels Palace held it did not apply where negligence only "might have" prevented the harm. The textbook flowchart describes two kinds of case: multiple sufficient causes (Bonnington Castings v Wardlaw; March v Stramare) and material contribution to harm where science makes the cause impossible to prove; it adds that there must be a clear breach and an evidentiary gap. A more than negligible contribution to cumulative harm is enough (Bonnington), but an increase in risk is not (Amaca Pty Ltd v Ellis (2010) 240 CLR 111); Australia has not adopted Fairchild v Glenhaven Funeral Services Ltd [2003] 1 AC 32, where materially increased risk sufficed. Sam's claim is not a dust disease or tobacco claim, so s 3B(1) does not exclude the CLA.

  Tortfeasors liable for the same damage are jointly and severally liable, and contribution between them is governed by the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1946 (NSW) s 5.

  **Application**

  *1. Breach by Priya and Mark.* The risk is of a person beyond the crest being struck by a ball driven blind. Each player knew Sam was there, so it was foreseeable (s 5B(1)(a)). It was not insignificant (s 5B(1)(b)): a drive towards a known worker is likely to land near him, and a golf ball can cause serious eye and head injuries. The precautions — waiting for Sam to move on, or at least calling "fore" — cost a minute (s 5B(2)(c)), and a social round and a $50 bet have little social utility to weigh against them. Each breached his or her duty. These are clear breaches, which matters for s 5D(2).

  *2. Factual causation against Priya and Mark.* Sam must show, on the balance of probabilities, that each defendant's negligence was a necessary condition of his injury. He cannot. Only one ball hit him, and on the evidence it is equally likely to have been either player's. A 50% probability does not discharge the onus in s 5E against either of them. Sam therefore needs s 5D(2).

  Sam has a strong argument that this is an exceptional case. Each breach is clear. The evidentiary gap was created by the defendants' simultaneous drives with identical balls, not by any failure of Sam's. They acted together, in the same negligent way, and the only uncertainty is which of two proven wrongdoers caused a harm one of them certainly caused. Cook v Lewis, on closely similar facts, is one common law response to multiple sufficient causes: both negligent defendants were held liable. On the s 5D(2) question — whether and why responsibility should be imposed — the answer is powerful: otherwise an innocent plaintiff recovers from neither of two negligent defendants.

  The defendants have real answers. Section 5D(2) operates only "in accordance with established principles", and Cook v Lewis is Canadian, not established Australian principle. The case fits neither flowchart category neatly: it is not a cumulative harm with unascertainable contributions, as in Bonnington, but a single ball from one of two sources; nor does scientific knowledge make proof impossible. Each may say that at most they increased the risk, and Amaca v Ellis holds increased risk is not enough; Australian law has not adopted Fairchild. Adeels Palace shows the High Court's caution about s 5D(2).

  On balance, Sam's argument is respectable but the outcome uncertain. His best case is that shared negligent conduct, with an evidentiary gap of the defendants' own making, is the exceptional case the provision contemplates. If s 5D(2) is accepted, scope of liability raises no difficulty: being struck by a ball is the very risk of driving blind at a worker (s 5D(1)(b) and (4)).

  *3. The club.* Breach is assumed; the question is whether the broken bell was a necessary condition of the injury. The bell told players when the landing area was clear, but Priya and Mark already knew Sam was there and chose to drive anyway. Sam's expert goes no further than that a bell "might have" made them wait, which is exactly what Adeels Palace held does not prove factual causation. As in Barnett, the harm would probably have occurred even if the club had taken care. Section 5D(2) does not rescue a weak causal link. Sam's claim against the club fails on factual causation.

  *4. Recovery.* If s 5D(2) is accepted against both players, they are liable for the same damage. Sam could recover the whole from either, and the one who pays could seek contribution from the other (Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1946 (NSW) s 5(1)(c) and s 5(2)); any inability of one to pay would be the other's risk, not Sam's.

  **Conclusion**

  Priya and Mark each clearly breached their duty. Sam cannot prove that either one's negligence was a necessary condition of his injury, because either ball could have hit him, so his claim against them depends on s 5D(2). He has a strong argument, supported by Cook v Lewis, the clear breaches and the evidentiary gap, that responsibility should be imposed on both; but the provision is rare and uncertain, the case fits neither flowchart category neatly, and Amaca v Ellis rejects increased risk as enough, so the outcome cannot be predicted with confidence. His claim against the club should fail: a working bell "might have" made a difference at most (Adeels Palace). If the players are liable, Sam may recover the whole from either, leaving contribution to them.
