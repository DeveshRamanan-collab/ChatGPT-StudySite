prompt: |
  Julian Assange took refuge in the Ecuadorian embassy in London in 2012 to avoid extradition. Under international
  law, could the UK enter the embassy to arrest him? What other options did the UK have in its dealings with
  Ecuador's mission?
model: |
  Diplomatic relations are governed by the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations 1961, a treaty regime the ICJ in
  Tehran Hostages (1980) called "self-contained": it sets out the receiving state's obligations and the means it has
  to counter abuse. Under art 22, the premises of the mission are inviolable, and agents of the receiving state may
  not enter them except with the consent of the head of mission. The premises, their furnishings, means of
  transport and archives are immune from search, requisition, attachment and execution (arts 22, 24), and the
  receiving state must protect the premises against intrusion (art 22(2)).

  Assange was not a diplomat, so he had no personal immunity under art 31 or inviolability under art 29. But the
  inviolability attaches to the premises, so the UK could not enter the embassy to arrest him without the
  ambassador's consent; a UK warrant does not override the treaty.

  The UK's options lay within the regime: seek the head of mission's consent, or respond to any abuse by declaring
  diplomats persona non grata (art 9), severing relations or other unfriendly acts, or doing nothing — which is, in
  effect, what happened for nearly seven years.
