  Immunity is procedural, so the seriousness of an allegation does not by itself remove it. For states, Germany v
  Italy (2012) held that custom did not deprive Germany of immunity in Italian civil proceedings over crimes by its
  forces in World War II. Immunity rules are "procedural in character" and "entirely distinct from the substantive
  law" (para 93), and immunity for acta jure imperii extends to acts of armed forces in armed conflict even on the
  forum's territory (para 77). Powerful policy arguments exist, but state practice does not yet support an
  exception.

  For serving officials, Arrest Warrant (2002) held that Belgium's warrant breached the sitting Congolese foreign
  minister's personal immunity; there is no international crimes exception. But immunity is not impunity:
  prosecution is possible at home, after waiver, by other states once the person leaves office (for acts before or
  after office and private acts during it), and before international criminal courts.

  For former officials, Pinochet (No 3) (1999) held a former head of state had no immunity for torture, which was an
  international crime with a form of universal jurisdiction under the Convention against Torture binding the UK,
  Chile and Spain. ILC draft article 7 (2017) would exclude immunity for genocide, crimes against humanity, war
  crimes, torture and enforced disappearances, but whether it reflects state practice is debated.
