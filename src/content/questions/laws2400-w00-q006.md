  **Issue**

  Two issues arise. First, whether Deng's off-ball elbow strike on Kwame is actionable as battery, given that Kwame had agreed to play in the match and had already accepted an earlier hard tackle without complaint. Second, whether Sam's additional dry-needling treatment on Kwame's shoulder is actionable as battery, given that Kwame had consented to treatment of his jaw injury but not to anything else.

  **Rule**

  Battery is a direct and intentional (or negligent) act of the defendant that causes physical contact with the body of the plaintiff without consent or other lawful justification. Any touching without a lawful excuse is unlawful, however slight, because every person's body is inviolate (Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd (2001) 53 NSWLR 98). Consent is capable of excusing what would otherwise be a battery, but to be valid it must be voluntary, given by a competent person, and — critically — must relate to the specific act complained of. Consent is specific to the act it covers, and conduct exceeding what was actually consented to is not protected.

  In McNamara v Duncan (1971) 26 ALR 584, the defendant deliberately struck the plaintiff on the head during an Australian Rules match, fracturing his skull. The court rejected the argument that playing the game meant the plaintiff had consented to that blow, holding that forcible contact within the rules of a game is one thing, but deliberately inflicted injury is another; a player consents only to the risks and contact ordinarily incidental to the sport as played, not to gratuitous violence. In Dean v Phung [2012] NSWCA 223, a dentist performed over fifty consultations of unnecessary treatment on a patient; the court found the patient's consent was not validly given, because it did not extend to treatment of that scale or kind — consent to some treatment does not extend to treatment the patient neither asked for nor was told about.

  **Application**

  *The first tackle.* Deng's earlier slide tackle, though hard, was an ordinary incident of a competitive football match — the kind of forcible but rule-consistent contact players of a physical sport are taken to accept by choosing to play, particularly as the referee did not even consider it worth a penalty. This tackle does not give Kwame any claim; it falls within the scope of what he consented to by taking the field.

  *The elbow strike.* The later elbow strike is a different matter entirely, and the facts are strikingly close to McNamara v Duncan. The strike occurred well after the ball had moved away, with no play near either player, and was motivated by Deng's anger over an earlier, unrelated foul rather than by any contest for the ball. This was not forcible contact incidental to a passage of play but a deliberate, off-the-ball blow calculated to injure Kwame in retaliation. Applying McNamara directly: just as playing Australian Rules football did not mean the plaintiff there had consented to a deliberate blow to the head, playing in this social competition did not mean Kwame consented to Deng deliberately elbowing him in the jaw once the play had moved on. Kwame's consent to physical contact was limited to contact of the kind, and in the circumstances, ordinarily incidental to the game as played — exactly the limit McNamara draws, and exactly the limit Deng's conduct crossed. Because the contact falls outside the scope of any consent Kwame gave, it is unlawful touching within Rixon v Star City's broad formulation, and all the elements of battery — a direct, intentional act causing physical contact without lawful excuse — are satisfied.

  *The dry-needling.* Kwame plainly consented to being examined and treated for his jaw injury by Sam; that consent was voluntary and given by a competent adult, and nothing suggests otherwise. But consent is specific to the act it covers. Kwame's consent extended to assessment and treatment of the jaw injury he presented with — the reason he was on the table in the first place — not to an entirely separate procedure on an unrelated part of his body that he had never mentioned, never asked about, and was never even told was going to occur. This mirrors the reasoning in Dean v Phung: just as the patient's consent to some dental treatment did not extend to the unnecessary and undisclosed additional treatment actually performed, Kwame's consent to jaw treatment does not extend to shoulder dry-needling performed simply because he was "already there." If anything, this case is more straightforward than Dean v Phung, since here Kwame was not even informed that any additional procedure was contemplated, let alone asked to agree to it — there was no attempt at disclosure at all, whereas Dean v Phung concerned treatment that was at least nominally within an ongoing course of dental care. Sam's own view that Kwame would "appreciate" the treatment is irrelevant; consent must come from the patient, not be inferred from the practitioner's assessment of what would be beneficial. Kwame's evidence that he would have refused if asked, given his dislike of needles and a past adverse reaction, only reinforces that his actual will was never sought or respected. The dry-needling was accordingly an unlawful touching outside the scope of any consent given, and battery is established against Sam.

  **Conclusion**

  Kwame has a strong claim in battery against Deng for the elbow strike: applying McNamara v Duncan, playing in the match did not extend Kwame's consent to a deliberate, off-the-ball blow inflicted well after the tackle he had already accepted, and Kwame should recover compensatory damages for his fractured jaw, together with aggravated damages given the deliberate and retaliatory nature of the conduct. Kwame also has a viable claim in battery against Sam for the dry-needling: his consent to treatment was confined to his jaw injury, and Dean v Phung confirms that treatment exceeding the scope of what was actually agreed to — here, treatment of an entirely different body part that was never disclosed or discussed — is not protected by consent, however well-intentioned Sam's motive. Kwame should be advised that he has good prospects against both Deng and Sam, and that the two claims are independent of one another and may be pursued separately.
