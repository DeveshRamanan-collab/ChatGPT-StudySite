  Parkview Shopping Centre has a mezzanine food court that overlooks the main ground-floor walkway.   On day nine, Theo, aged 13, was standing at the gap with friends. To show off, he threw a full one-litre frozen drink bottle towards a bin on the ground floor "to see if he could get it in". He missed, and the bottle struck Hana, aged 70, on the head. Theo's mother says he has always been impulsive and immature for his age.

  Dazed, Hana stumbled sideways into the path of a ride-on floor scrubber driven by Liam, a Parkview cleaner in his first week, who was driving it at full speed through the crowded walkway while looking down at its control screen.   **Issue**

  The question is whether Theo (throwing the bottle), Liam (driving the scrubber) and Parkview (leaving the gap guarded only by tape and a sign) each breached the duty assumed to be owed to Hana. The difficulty is the standard of care: Theo is a child, Liam is inexperienced, and Parkview faces a risk created partly by others' misbehaviour.

  **Rule**

  Breach is measured against an objective standard: the reasonable person in the defendant's position (Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") s 5B(1)(c)). The duty is to take reasonable care, not an absolute duty to avoid injury; the measure is reasonableness, not elimination of risk (Swain v Waverley Municipal Council (2005) 220 CLR 517). For a child, the standard is that of a normal child of comparable age (McHale v Watson (1966) 115 CLR 199); but, as Kitto J said, it is no answer that the child was abnormally slow-witted, quick-tempered, absent-minded or inexperienced — he may rely only on a limitation of foresight or prudence characteristic of his stage of development. Inexperience does not lower the standard: a learner driver owes the same standard as a licensed driver (Imbree v McNeilly [2008] HCA 40).

  Under s 5B(1), a person is not negligent in failing to take precautions against a risk of harm unless (a) the risk was foreseeable — one the person knew or ought to have known of; (b) it was not insignificant; and (c) a reasonable person in the person's position would have taken those precautions. The risk of harm is identified first, as a class of injury (Benic v NSW [2010] NSWSC 1039), specific enough to correspond to the precautions in issue (RTA v Dederer (2007) 234 CLR 330). A risk that is not far-fetched or fanciful is foreseeable (Wyong Shire Council v Shirt (1980) 146 CLR 40); "not insignificant" is a higher threshold, depending on obviousness, likelihood and seriousness (Rail Corporation NSW v Donald [2018] NSWCA 82). Under s 5B(2) the court considers probability (judged prospectively: Dederer), seriousness, the burden of precautions and social utility. Under s 5C(b), the fact that a risk could have been avoided by doing something in a different way does not of itself give rise to liability.

  **Application**

  *Theo.* Theo is judged against a normal 13-year-old, not an adult (McHale v Watson). His mother's evidence that he is impulsive and immature for his age does not lower the standard further: on Kitto J's reasoning, individual traits such as being quick-tempered or inexperienced are no answer; Theo can rely only on limitations of foresight and prudence characteristic of 13-year-olds generally.

  The risk of harm is of people on the walkway being struck by an object thrown from the mezzanine. A normal 13-year-old knows that a heavy frozen bottle thrown from a height into a crowded walkway may hit someone, so the risk was foreseeable (s 5B(1)(a)). It was not insignificant (s 5B(1)(b)): the walkway was crowded, a throw at a distant bin can easily miss, and a one-litre frozen bottle falling from a mezzanine can cause serious head injury. Under s 5B(1)(c) the precaution was simply not to throw it. Its burden was nothing, and showing off has no social utility. Theo breached his duty.

  *Liam.* The standard is that of a reasonable operator of a floor scrubber in Liam's position. His inexperience does not lower it, any more than a learner driver's does (Imbree v McNeilly), and "doing his best" is no answer. The risk of pedestrians in a crowded walkway being struck by a heavy machine driven at speed by an operator not looking ahead is plainly foreseeable and not insignificant: it is obvious, likely in a crowd, and serious, as Hana's crushed foot shows. The precautions — walking pace and eyes on the walkway — cost almost nothing. A reasonable operator would have taken them. Liam breached his duty, and Parkview is vicariously liable; that no one trained him in the written procedure is, if anything, a shortcoming of Parkview's own.

  *Parkview — the risk of harm.* Following Benic and Dederer, the risk is of people on the ground-floor walkway being struck by objects falling, or thrown, through the unguarded gap in the mezzanine edge. That framing matches the precaution in issue — a barrier across the gap — rather than Hana's particular injury.

  *Section 5B(1)(a) and (b).* Parkview knew of the risk: it put up tape and a sign, and its own security logged two objects dropped through the gap in a week. The risk was foreseeable. Parkview argues that two incidents among 20,000 daily visitors shows the risk was tiny. But probability is judged prospectively (Dederer), and the relevant population is not every visitor but the teenagers gathered at an open edge during school holidays. Two objects in one week, one landing in a pram, show a risk that was materialising, not a remote one. On the Donald factors — an obvious open gap, a demonstrated likelihood and serious consequences from objects falling from height — the risk was not insignificant.

  *Section 5B(1)(c) and (2).* Weighing the considerations together:

  - Probability: real and recurring, as the two incidents show.
  - Seriousness: objects falling from a mezzanine onto shoppers' heads risk grave injury.
  - Burden: a $900 hoarding, already offered by the builder. Unlike Woods v Multi-Sport Holdings, where suitable helmets did not exist, the precaution was available, cheap and quick.
  - Social utility: keeping the food court open during the holidays had real value to shoppers and tenants. But, unlike E v Australian Red Cross Society, where testing would have cost about 5% of all blood donations, a hoarding would not have closed the food court; it would only have closed the gap.

  *Parkview's other arguments.* It is right that it need not eliminate every risk, and cannot stop teenagers misbehaving (Swain). But the question is whether a reasonable occupier would have taken the precaution against the risk, and a barrier deals with dropped and thrown objects alike without policing anyone. The sign and tape do not help as Dederer's signs did. There, warnings were a reasonable response to a grave but improbable risk, given the expense, difficulty and inconvenience of anything more; here, the probability was demonstrated, the alternative cost $900, and a sign addressed to teenagers on the mezzanine gives no protection to shoppers below. Finally, Parkview is right that the night-time, panel-by-panel alternative is beside the point: under s 5C(b), the fact that the risk could have been avoided by doing the renovation differently does not of itself give rise to liability. Hana should rest her case on the failure to put up the hoarding, which the s 5B analysis supports.

  **Conclusion**

  All three defendants breached their duties. Theo, judged as a normal 13-year-old, fell short because a child of his age appreciates the risk of throwing a heavy bottle into a crowd; his individual impulsiveness does not lower the standard (McHale v Watson). Liam fell short of the standard of a reasonable scrubber operator, which his inexperience does not reduce (Imbree v McNeilly), and Parkview is vicariously liable for him. Parkview itself breached its duty: a reasonable occupier would have spent $900 on a hoarding rather than relying on tape and a sign.
