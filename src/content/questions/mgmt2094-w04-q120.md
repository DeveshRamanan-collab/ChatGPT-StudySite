Calling ideas out is classic brainstorming, and the lecturer explains why it fails: once an idea is said aloud it influences everyone else's, and Mattimore (2012) adds that one person talks while everyone else listens, so the extroverts dominate. Brainwriting fixes this: each manager writes an idea, passes the sheet on, and the next builds on it; with five rounds, 30 managers would produce about 150 ideas, everyone \"speaks\" with their pen, and ideas piggyback. For a long conference day I would recommend brainwalking, Mattimore's improvement: flip-chart \"ideation stations\" on the walls, one per waste problem (produce, bakery, deli), with managers rotating in pairs. Moving raises energy, public ideas reinforce progress, the walls build shared purpose, and pairs trade some quantity for quality. Mattimore invented it for Trident and Dentyne sessions when repeat participants needed something new. The trade-offs: brainwalking needs space and facilitation, and pairs halve the idea count. These tools serve the divergent stage; the chain still has to converge on a few ideas to trial."
