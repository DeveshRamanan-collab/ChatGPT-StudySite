prompt: |
  Jeremy Bentham dismissed the language of natural rights: "Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense – nonsense upon stilts" (Anarchical Fallacies). On the lecture's reading, for Bentham, a utilitarian with a positivist outlook, the only rights that exist are legal rights enforceable in court.

  Whether human rights are moral rights, legal rights or both returns us to the relationship between law and morality. Austin said that "the existence of law is one thing; its merit and demerit another" (PJD 157), and Hart restated legal positivism as the contention that "it is in no sense a necessary truth that laws reproduce or satisfy certain demands of morality, though in fact they have often done so" (CL 185–186). Natural law theorists, by contrast, have offered some of the strongest justifications of human rights: universal principles of morality and justice in light of which every human being has basic entitlements.

  History sharpens the question. Where slavery was accepted both morally and legally, enslaved people had no legal right not to be enslaved. Did they have a moral right? Today rights are "positivised" in constitutions and treaties. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) presented itself as "a common standard of achievement for all peoples and all nations"; South Africa's Constitution directs courts interpreting its Bill of Rights to promote the values of "an open and democratic society based on human dignity, equality and freedom"; Australia has no bill of rights in its federal Constitution. And rights recognised in a text that stay there are only "paper rights".

  Critically evaluate the following statement:

  "If legal positivism is true, there are no human rights, only legal rights. Bentham was simply being consistent."

  In your essay, say whether each claim you consider is about moral rights, legal rights or both, and distinguish the separation thesis from any view about the nature of morality. Draw on material from more than one week of the unit. Take a clear position, support it with reasons, and deal with the strongest counterargument to your view.
model: |
  **Thesis**

  The statement is wrong on both counts. Legal positivism, in the form Hart defends, is a theory about the existence and content of law; it says nothing about whether there are moral rights. It is consistent with the view that human rights are moral rights which legal systems may or may not recognise — indeed it is the theory that best explains how an enslaved person could have a moral right the law denied. Bentham's dismissal of natural rights does not follow from his positivism. It follows from a further view about morality, his utilitarianism and his rejection of natural law, and that view has to be defended on its own terms.

  **Argument**

  *What positivism claims.* Austin's formula separates the existence of law from its merit (PJD 157). Hart's version is that it is "in no sense a necessary truth that laws reproduce or satisfy certain demands of morality" (CL 185–186); Green and Adams state positivism as the thesis that the existence and content of law depend on social facts and not on its merits. Each is a claim about law. None says that morality is unreal, or that moral claims cannot be true. The separation thesis denies a necessary connection between law and morality; it does not deny that there is a morality, or that laws often reproduce its demands.

  *What human rights claim.* The week breaks the topic into six issues: identity, authority, content, history, justification and implementation. Human rights are said to be plural, universal and high-priority: they belong to all human beings regardless of race, sex, nationality or any other status, which is what art 2 of the Universal Declaration proclaims. If human rights are legal rights only, their universality is hostage to the legal systems that recognise them. If they are moral rights, they can exist where no legal system recognises them, and they then need a moral justification. The question of authority — moral, legal or both — is therefore the question of how law and morality relate.

  *Slavery as the test.* Where slavery was legal, enslaved people had no legal right not to be enslaved; the legal system accepted and regulated slavery. Yet one can say they still had a moral right, because the fact that positive law does not recognise a moral requirement does not mean the requirement does not exist. Positivism has no difficulty with that sentence. It is exactly what the separation thesis allows one to say: the slave laws were valid law, and they violated a moral right. Hart chose the rule of recognition over Kelsen's basic norm partly to avoid Kelsen's view of the conflict between law and morals (CL 293); on Hart's account, valid law and morality can genuinely conflict. The strong natural law thesis has more trouble. If unjust laws are not law at all, the slave codes were never law, and the history of rights is harder to tell. Hart's minimum content of natural law (CL 199) shows how much a positivist can accept without giving up separation: rules protecting persons, property and promises are a natural necessity for any viable society, but that is a fact about the human condition, not a conceptual link between law and morality.

  *Positivisation.* Implementation runs from natural law to positive law, from moral rights to legal rights. Rights are positivised into domestic and international law, and protected at the constitutional level because the interests they capture are thought too important to be left to majoritarian rule through ordinary legislation. Some legal sources direct how rights are read: South Africa's s 39(1) requires a court interpreting the Bill of Rights to "promote the values that underlie an open and democratic society based on human dignity, equality and freedom". The Universal Declaration presents itself as "a common standard of achievement", stressing teaching and education before enforcement. The language of these instruments assumes rights that exist before their legal recognition and serve as a standard for it.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the statement is that Bentham saw something the moderate positivist avoids. If law is a matter of social facts, a "right" has content only when a social fact — a rule recognised and enforced by officials — stands behind it. Talk of rights that no one can enforce borrows the authority of legal language without its institutions. The sceptical questions of the week add weight. Rights language has expanded so far that every entitlement can be cast as a right; if every card is a trump, what is the point of the category? Rights may be ethnocentric, a Western creation expressed in concepts that do not translate into other cultures or worldviews. And if moral relativism is true, so that moral claims are always relative to a culture or society, "human" rights cannot be universal. The realists' warning completes the case: rights that stay in a text are paper rights. Bentham was consistent, on this view, because only legal rights do anything.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument is serious, but it is not an argument from positivism. Each step relies on a claim about morality, not about law. That rights talk is inflated is a call for better definition and more discipline in using it, not proof that moral rights do not exist. The ethnocentrism objection has to meet the fact that we compare how different legal systems recognise rights, and a meaningful comparison needs common ground: that rights are expressed differently does not show that nothing common lies behind them. The relativist step does the real work, and it is a metaethical thesis — about whether moral disagreements are rationally resolvable and whether moral statements are apt for truth and falsity. A positivist can be a moral objectivist. Hart's acceptance of conflict between valid law and morality presupposes that the moral judgment is worth making.

  Bentham's own position confirms this. He was a utilitarian: he judged actions and laws by the principle of utility, which is itself a moral standard. His rejection of natural rights was the rejection of one moral theory in favour of another, not a consequence of the separation of law and morals. Austin, who shared both his positivism and his utilitarianism, shows that the two doctrines travel together historically; Hart shows that they come apart.

  Nor does the paper-rights point help Bentham. It shows that recognition is not enough and that implementation matters, but it applies equally to legal rights that are recognised and not enforced. The gap between law in books and law in action is a gap within legal rights; it cannot be used to show that only legal rights exist.

  So the statement should be rejected. Positivism leaves open whether there are human rights as moral rights, and its separation of law and morality is what lets us say that the slave codes were law and were wrong. Bentham was consistent with his utilitarianism, not with his positivism. Whether human rights exist independently of their positivisation is a question about the nature of morality — relativism or objectivism — and a positivist may answer it either way.
