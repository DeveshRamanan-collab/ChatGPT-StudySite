model: |
  **Issue**

  Two issues arise. First, whether Theo's relocation and use of Priya's boat, and the resulting hull and propeller damage, amounts to conversion. Second, whether cutting and using Klaus's mooring rope amounts to conversion of a different, and more clear-cut, kind. Third, whether necessity provides Theo with a defence to either or both dealings.

  **Rule**

  Conversion evolved from the old action of trover: wrongfully dealing with goods inconsistently with another's possessory rights. It is a strict liability tort — good faith or an honest motive is no defence to the tort itself, however relevant that motive may be to a separate defence. Conversion requires a serious "dealing" amounting to dominion contrary to the true possessor's rights, going beyond mere interference with the physical state of the goods. Conversion can arise from taking possession without intending to keep the goods permanently, or from using them inconsistently with the owner's dominion, such as using an owner's own branded bottles for one's own wine (Penfolds Wines Pty Ltd v Elliott (1946) 74 CLR 204). Whether a dealing is "serious enough" to amount to conversion is genuinely contestable and fact-sensitive: in Bunnings Group Ltd v CHEP Australia Ltd (2011) NSWCA 342, merely storing delivered goods on the plaintiff's pallets was not conversion, but transporting Bunnings's own goods on them was, because that use went beyond incidental interference to an assertion of dominion over the pallets themselves for the defendant's own purposes.

  Necessity may excuse what would otherwise be a tort where the defendant's action was necessary to prevent serious harm to person or property, and was reasonable and proportionate to the risk. This extends to protecting one's own property and to protecting the property of another, and is available even to a stranger acting to protect someone else's property in a genuine emergency: in Proudman v Allen [1954] SASR 336, a defendant who turned a driverless car's wheel to avoid a collision, sending it into the sea instead, successfully relied on necessity for the resulting damage, because the emergency was genuine and sudden and the response was a proportionate way of averting a worse outcome.

  **Application**

  *The boat.* Theo's use of the spare key to start Wandering Star's motor and relocate the boat is a serious dealing with Priya's property that goes well beyond incidental interference: like the defendant's use of the owner's bottles in Penfolds Wines, Theo exercised control over Priya's boat for a purpose she had not authorised, asserting a degree of dominion over it — starting its engine and physically moving it to a location of his choosing — that an ordinary bystander could not lawfully do without permission. This is a more serious dealing than the mere storage found not to be conversion in Bunnings Group v CHEP, since Theo actively operated and relocated the vessel itself, rather than merely housing it; it sits closer to the active-use side of the Bunnings distinction, where actually deploying another's property for one's own purposes (there, transporting goods on the pallets) crossed into conversion. Conversion being strict liability, Theo's good intentions in trying to save the boat do not answer the tort itself — that question is reserved for necessity, considered below. The resulting $2,400 damage to the hull and propeller shaft is consequential loss flowing from the dealing.

  *The rope.* Theo's conduct with Klaus's rope is a more straightforward case of conversion. Entering the unlocked locker to take the coil, and then cutting it in half and using both lengths so that the remainder was "cut and unusable for its original purpose," is a clear act of dominion inconsistent with Klaus's rights as owner: unlike simply borrowing an intact item, cutting and consuming the rope for Theo's own purpose destroyed its usefulness to Klaus and permanently altered it without any prospect of full restoration in its original form. This mirrors the kind of serious dealing Penfolds Wines and Bunnings both treat as conversion once a defendant moves from incidental contact to actively using up another's property for their own ends. Again, conversion being strict liability, Theo's motive does not prevent the tort arising, though it is squarely relevant to necessity.

  *Necessity.* The rising storm, the boat swinging dangerously close to a piling, and the near-certainty of the hull being holed within minutes together constitute a genuine, sudden emergency of the kind recognised in Proudman v Allen, not a foreseeable or self-created problem — Theo had no time to locate Priya or seek any other permission before the swell would have caused serious damage. Proudman confirms necessity is available even to a stranger, like Theo, acting to protect another's property from such a threat. Relocating the boat was a reasonable and proportionate response: it was directed at preventing the very harm (holing on the piling) that was imminent, and some risk of incidental contact with the piling during the manoeuvre in swell and poor light was, on the facts, effectively unavoidable in achieving that protective purpose. Necessity should therefore excuse the conversion of the boat, including the incidental $2,400 damage caused while executing an unavoidably risky manoeuvre in genuinely dangerous conditions.

  The rope stands differently. While using some additional line to secure the now-relocated boat against the worsening swell was itself a reasonably necessary step (continuing the same emergency), the proportionality of taking it specifically from Klaus's locker, rather than from any other available source, and consuming the entire coil by cutting it rather than borrowing and later returning an intact length, is more finely balanced. If no other rope was reasonably available in the time Theo had, necessity can extend to this taking too, since Proudman confirms the defence is not limited to protecting only the property directly at risk, but covers reasonably necessary incidental measures taken to avert the emergency. However, necessity requires the response to be no more than what the emergency required; cutting the rope, rather than using it intact and returning it afterwards, meant Klaus's property was destroyed rather than merely borrowed, and Theo should be advised this makes his necessity defence to the rope weaker than his defence in relation to the boat, since a less destructive alternative (using it uncut) may well have achieved the same protective purpose.

  **Conclusion**

  Theo committed conversion of both Priya's boat and Klaus's rope, each involving a serious dealing amounting to dominion over property not his own. His necessity defence is strong in relation to the boat: a genuine, sudden emergency threatened serious damage, and relocating the boat, including the incidental piling contact, was a proportionate response, so Priya's claim for the $2,400 damage should fail. The rope stands on weaker ground: while taking some rope in the emergency may be excused, cutting rather than merely using Klaus's line goes further than the emergency plainly required, and Theo should be advised he remains at risk of liability to Klaus for the $95 loss, or at least that part of it attributable to destroying rather than borrowing the coil.
