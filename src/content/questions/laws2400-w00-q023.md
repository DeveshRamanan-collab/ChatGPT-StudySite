  **Issue**

  Breach is assumed. The question is whether each breach caused the harm Tom complains of: (1) whether Dr Okafor's failure to warn caused the foot drop; (2) whether the registrar's two-day delay caused the joint damage; and (3) whether Harbourview's lack of an inspection system caused the broken wrist.

  **Rule**

  Damage is the gist of an action in negligence, and the damage must be caused by the breach. Under the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") s 5D(1), a determination that negligence caused particular harm comprises (a) that the negligence was a necessary condition of the occurrence of the harm (factual causation), and (b) that it is appropriate for the scope of the negligent person's liability to extend to the harm so caused (scope of liability). Section 5D(1) treats these as separate and distinct issues (Adeels Palace v Moubarak (2009) 239 CLR 420). Section 5D(1)(a) "is a statutory statement of the 'but for' test of causation" (Strong v Woolworths Ltd (2012) 246 CLR 182). The plaintiff always bears the onus of proving, on the balance of probabilities, any fact relevant to causation (s 5E).

  If the harm would have happened even without the negligence, the negligence is not a cause. In Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital [1969] 1 QB 428 the duty doctor was negligent in refusing to see the deceased, but even timely treatment would not have saved him. That changing the circumstances "might have" made a difference does not prove factual causation (Adeels Palace). In personal injury cases the common law of Australia does not entertain an action where the damage is characterised as the loss of a chance of a better outcome (Tabet v Gett (2010) 240 CLR 537). But where the timing of a hazard is unknown, causation can still be proved on the probabilities: in Strong, the probabilities favoured the chip having been deposited in the longer period before the fall, when a proper inspection system would have found it.

  Where it is relevant to decide what the injured person would have done if the negligent person had not been negligent, s 5D(3) provides that the matter is to be determined subjectively in the light of all relevant circumstances, and that any statement made by the person after suffering the harm about what he or she would have done is inadmissible except to the extent that it is against his or her interest.

  **Application**

  *1. The failure to warn and the foot drop.* A warning could only have prevented the foot drop if Tom, once warned, would have declined the operation. So the necessary-condition question turns on what Tom would have done, and s 5D(3) governs it. The question is subjective — what this Tom would have done, not what a reasonable patient would have done — but it is decided in the light of all relevant circumstances. Tom's evidence that he "would never have gone ahead" is a statement made after the harm, in his own favour, so it is inadmissible under s 5D(3)(b). That leaves the circumstances, and they point the other way. Before the operation Tom called the pain unbearable and said he would do "whatever it takes" to get back to work. Most tellingly, two years earlier he was warned of exactly this nerve risk, in writing, and went ahead anyway. On the balance of probabilities (s 5E), Tom would have had the operation even if warned. The failure to warn was not a necessary condition of the foot drop, so this claim fails on factual causation, even though Dr Okafor breached his duty.

  *2. The registrar's delay and the joint damage.* The question is whether, but for the two-day delay, the joint damage would have been avoided. The experts put that chance at about 40%. It follows that it is more likely than not — about 60% — that the damage would have happened even with antibiotics on time. As in Barnett, timely care would probably not have changed the outcome, and Tom cannot discharge the onus in s 5E. The delay was not a necessary condition of the joint damage.

  Tom may try to recast his loss as the loss of a 40% chance of a better outcome. Tabet v Gett answers that. There, a child's chance of a better outcome after a delayed CT scan was under 50%, and the High Court held that in personal injury cases Australian common law does not entertain an action where the damage is characterised as the loss of a chance of a better outcome. Tom's position is materially the same, so this claim also fails.

  *3. The inspection system and the broken wrist.* Harbourview will argue that no one knows when the water appeared, so Tom cannot prove that an hourly inspection would have found it. Strong v Woolworths shows how to answer that. In Strong, the time the chip was dropped was unknown, but the probabilities favoured its having been deposited in the longer period, when a proper inspection would have found it. Here, the bathroom went unchecked for nine hours. The likeliest source of soapy water is the cleaner's trolley with the leaking bucket, parked outside from about 9 am until noon — four hours or more before Tom fell. On that evidence, the probabilities favour the water having been on the floor for well over an hour, so an hourly inspection would probably have found and removed it.

  This is not Adeels Palace, where the evidence went no further than that security staff might have prevented the shooting. Tom's case is that a reasonable system probably would have prevented his fall, which is what s 5D(1)(a) and s 5E require. The absence of an inspection system was a necessary condition of the fall.

  Scope of liability is a separate, normative question (s 5D(1)(b)), and under s 5D(4) the court considers whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed. It raises no real difficulty here. A slip on a wet bathroom floor is precisely the harm a ward inspection system exists to prevent, and nothing intervened between the hazard and the fall. It is appropriate for Harbourview's liability to extend to the wrist fracture.

  **Conclusion**

  Tom's three claims show that breach and causation are separate hurdles. His claim against Dr Okafor fails: once his self-serving statement is excluded under s 5D(3), the circumstances — above all his decision to go ahead after the same warning two years earlier — show he would probably have had the operation anyway. His claim for the joint damage fails because timely antibiotics would probably not have avoided it (Barnett), and a lost 40% chance is not compensable damage in personal injury (Tabet v Gett). His claim for the broken wrist succeeds: on the probabilities, an hourly inspection would have found the water (Strong v Woolworths), and the fracture is within the scope of Harbourview's liability.
