  **Issue**

  Two questions arise. First, whether Danny's conduct in confining Aisha in the back office amounts to false imprisonment, for which BrightMart would also answer. Second, whether Constable Reyes's arrest of Aisha, and her continued detention at the station, were lawful under the Law Enforcement (Powers and Responsibilities) Act 2002 (NSW) ("LEPRA").

  **Rule**

  False imprisonment is the direct total restraint of the liberty of the plaintiff by an intentional or negligent act of the defendant without lawful justification. The elements are: a positive act; direct interference; an intentional or negligent act; and total restraint of the plaintiff's liberty without lawful justification. Total restraint does not require physical boundaries in the sense of a full prison cell, but it does require that there be no reasonable means of escape available to the plaintiff. A condition attached to a lawful entry can amount to a lawful excuse and defeat a claim: in Balmain New Ferry Co v Robertson (1906) 4 CLR 379, a passenger who had entered a wharf on the accepted condition that he pay a further penny to leave was not falsely imprisoned when the company insisted on that fee, because he had agreed to that condition as part of the terms of his entry.

  Arrest is a defence to what would otherwise be false imprisonment where it is carried out under lawful power. Section 99(1) LEPRA permits arrest without warrant only where both limbs are satisfied: the officer has a suspicion, on reasonable grounds, that the person is committing or has committed an offence; and the officer is satisfied that the arrest is reasonably necessary for a reason the Act lists. Suspicion is more than a mere possibility but less than a belief. Sections 230–231 LEPRA confine any force used in effecting an arrest, or otherwise exercising a power under the Act, to what is reasonably necessary in the circumstances.

  **Application**

  *Danny's conduct.* Gripping Aisha's arm, walking her to the back office and locking the door is plainly a positive, direct and intentional act. Total restraint is made out: a locked door with no means of opening it from the inside left Aisha with no reasonable means of escape for the full forty minutes, regardless of the room's small size or ordinary appearance. The only remaining question is whether Danny had a lawful justification. Balmain New Ferry does not assist BrightMart here: Robertson's claim failed only because he had accepted, as an express condition of entering the wharf, that he would pay to leave — he had bound himself to that arrangement in advance. Aisha never agreed, expressly or by implication, that shopping at BrightMart was conditional on submitting to physical restraint or confinement if a guard suspected her of theft; nothing about buying a jacket carries such a condition. Nor does BrightMart's own policy assist Danny — on the facts, that policy authorised only asking a customer to voluntarily wait, not physical restraint or locking a customer in a room, so Danny in fact exceeded even his employer's own authorised procedure. Because there is no accepted condition, no lawful arrest power available to a store security guard on these facts, and no other recognised defence (no consent, no necessity, no self-defence) that fits this scenario, Danny has no lawful justification for the total restraint, and false imprisonment is established against him. BrightMart is also exposed, since Danny was acting within the general scope of his role as a security guard carrying out (even if exceeding) store procedure when he detained Aisha, engaging vicarious liability principles.

  *The police arrest.* Constable Reyes's arrest must be tested against both limbs of s 99(1). The first limb — reasonable suspicion that Aisha had committed an offence — is difficult to sustain by the time of the arrest: Reyes had already examined Aisha's receipt, which matched the jacket, and himself acknowledged it "appeared genuine." Once independent, credible evidence directly rebuts the very suspicion that might otherwise justify an arrest, any residual suspicion becomes difficult to characterise as reasonable rather than a bare, unsubstantiated possibility — precisely what s 99(1) requires suspicion to exceed. Even assuming some residual suspicion survived, the second limb — that arrest was reasonably necessary — is harder still to establish. Reyes's own stated reason, taking her in "just to check things out properly," is not a considered application of a recognised statutory purpose to genuine investigative need; it reads as a decision to arrest despite exculpatory evidence rather than because a real necessity for arrest (rather than, for example, simply recording her details or continuing an on-the-spot enquiry) remained. On the facts as given, both limbs of s 99(1) are doubtful, and the arrest is therefore likely unlawful.

  Separately, ss 230–231 LEPRA confine the force used in effecting an arrest to what is reasonably necessary. Aisha had not resisted, argued or attempted to leave, and had just produced credible exculpatory evidence; handcuffing her in these circumstances, and marching her through a public car park, goes well beyond force that was reasonably necessary to safely effect an already doubtful arrest. This use of force is itself difficult to justify even if the arrest were otherwise lawful.

  **Conclusion**

  Aisha has a strong claim in false imprisonment against Danny, for which BrightMart is also answerable: all four elements are made out, and Balmain New Ferry does not provide a lawful excuse because Aisha never accepted any condition of entry authorising her restraint. Separately, Constable Reyes's arrest is likely unlawful because, by the time of the arrest, the reasonable-suspicion limb of s 99(1) LEPRA was undermined by the produced receipt and the reasonably-necessary limb was not genuinely engaged; the handcuffing also appears to exceed the force reasonably necessary under ss 230–231 given Aisha's cooperation. Aisha should be advised she has good prospects of recovering compensatory damages from BrightMart for the initial confinement, and separately from the police for the unlawful arrest and detention, with aggravated damages available given the public and humiliating manner of both incidents.
