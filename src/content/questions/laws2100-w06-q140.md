prompt: "\"For CLS, law is a contradictory and illegitimate system which maintains oppressive social relations.\" Can a positivist accept any of this?"
model: Some, but not the conclusion. CLS draws on realism (law is indeterminate, legal reasoning is a myth), postmodernism (words do not have stable meaning; rejection of grand narratives and epistemic certainty) and Marxism (the superstructure helps the dominant class; law legitimises private ownership while presenting itself as neutral). Its key claims are radical indeterminacy, contradiction because law stems from political conflict, and law as ideology that promotes the interests of the powerful. A positivist like Hart can accept a good deal. Law embodies political choices and values; Hart's theory explains morally sound and morally unsound regimes alike; and law may present itself as neutral without being so. But Hart would say law is contradictory sometimes, not always, and that radical indeterminacy is refuted by the plain cases that constantly recur. The deeper disagreement is methodological. For CLS the distinction between law as it is and law as it ought to be does not make sense; for Hart and Kelsen, describing law is not endorsing it. A positivist can accept that law may be oppressive; what they deny is that this is part of what law is.
