  Explain the three forms of fault recognised in trespass and the general rule (with its exception) on who bears
  the onus of proving fault.
model: |
  Trespass requires the defendant's direct interference to be accompanied by fault, which can take the form of
  intention, recklessness, or negligence. Intention and recklessness are subjective states of mind, assessed by
  reference to the defendant's own actual state of mind, while negligence is objective, judged by the standard
  of a reasonable person in the defendant's position. Intention is required only as to performing the
  interfering act itself, not as to its consequences: if a defendant touches someone while mistakenly believing
  it is their partner, that still counts as an intentional act, and the mistake is no defence. Recklessness
  applies where the consequences of the act are so likely that the defendant is taken to have foreseen them;
  this is rare, since most trespass falls under intentional conduct. Negligent trespass — failing to exercise
  reasonable care, resulting in interference — is also rare in practice today, because such cases are now
  mostly brought under the tort of negligence instead.

  On the onus of proof, the general rule is that the plaintiff need only prove that the direct interference
  occurred; the onus then shifts to the defendant to disprove fault, typically by showing the interference was
  an "inevitable accident". The exception is harm arising out of use of the highway: there, the plaintiff must
  prove the defendant's fault, because the public's right to use the highway means road users and adjoining
  property owners must accept some risk of harm and so should bear the onus of proving fault themselves. In an
  action on the case, by contrast, the plaintiff always bears the onus of proving fault, with no equivalent
  onus-shifting rule.
