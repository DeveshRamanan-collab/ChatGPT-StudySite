  **Issue**

  The issue is whether Castrea has committed internationally wrongful acts against Lorran in connection with (1) the storming of the embassy, (2) the three-week occupation and the publication of Lorran's correspondence, and (3) the arrest of the First Secretary; and, if so, what Lorran may demand.

  **Rule**

  Under the Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts (ARSIWA), which are not binding but largely codify custom, every internationally wrongful act entails responsibility (art 1). A wrongful act exists where conduct consisting of an act or omission is attributable to the state and breaches an international obligation (art 2).

  The primary obligations are in the VCDR, which the ICJ in *Tehran Hostages* called a "self-contained regime". Mission premises are inviolable, may not be entered without the head of mission's consent, and the receiving state must protect them (art 22); archives and documents are inviolable (art 24). A diplomatic agent's person is inviolable and he may not be arrested (art 29). Where a diplomat abuses his position, the receiving state may declare him persona non grata (art 9), sever relations or seek waiver.

  On attribution, the conduct of any state organ is the act of the state (art 4). Conduct of an organ acting in that capacity is attributed even if it exceeds authority or contravenes instructions (art 7; *Velásquez Rodríguez v Honduras*). Private conduct is not ordinarily attributed, but becomes the state's if and to the extent the state acknowledges and adopts it as its own (art 11). In *Tehran*, Iran was responsible first for its own failure to protect the US embassy, and later for the militants' conduct once its decree endorsed them.

  An injured state — one to which the obligation breached was owed — may invoke responsibility (art 42). The responsible state must cease a continuing wrongful act and offer assurances of non-repetition if circumstances require (art 30), and make full reparation (art 31), which must "wipe out all the consequences of the illegal act" (*Factory at Chorzów*), by restitution, compensation or satisfaction (arts 35–37).

  **Application**

  *Primary obligations.* Both states are VCDR parties, so the obligations in arts 22, 24 and 29 bound Castrea at the time of the conduct.

  *Phase one — the storming.* The Castrean Youth Front is a private association, and nothing suggests it was instructed or controlled by Castrea. Its storming of the embassy is not, on its own, attributable to Castrea. But Castrea's responsibility does not depend on the students' conduct. Article 22 imposes a positive duty to protect the premises. The ambassador asked three times for protection; police were present and did nothing. That is an omission by Castrean organs (art 4), and an omission can itself be a breach (art 2). This is exactly the first phase of *Tehran*, where Iran's inaction breached its protective duties.

  *Phase two — the occupation.* The Interior Minister's televised statement calling the students "true patriots" and declaring the embassy "held in trust by the Castrean people" until the businessman's release goes beyond approval: it adopts the occupation as a tool of Castrean policy. The police's conduct confirms this — supplying the students and preventing the diplomats from leaving. Under art 11, from that point the continued occupation, the detention of the two diplomats and the breaking open of the filing cabinets become Castrea's own acts, mirroring the effect of Iran's decree in *Tehran*. These breach art 22 (entry and occupation of the premises), art 24 (archives) and art 29 (detention of diplomats). Publication of the correspondence in a government newspaper is a further breach of art 24.

  *Sergeant Ilić.* A police officer is an organ of the state under art 4. Ilić arrested the First Secretary while acting as a police officer, using police powers and a police cell. His commander's express instruction not to approach Lorranian diplomats does not matter: under art 7 conduct in an official capacity is attributed even where it contravenes instructions, as the Inter-American Court held in *Velásquez Rodríguez*. The arrest breaches art 29.

  *The espionage justification.* Castrea's claim that the First Secretary was gathering intelligence does not excuse its conduct. A diplomat must respect local law and not interfere in internal affairs (art 41), but the VCDR itself provides the receiving state's remedies for abuse: declaring the diplomat persona non grata (art 9), severing relations or seeking waiver. Because diplomatic law is a self-contained regime (*Tehran*), Castrea could not respond by arresting the diplomat or occupying the mission. Nor would any alleged abuse by one diplomat explain the occupation of the whole embassy and the detention of two other diplomats.

  *Invocation.* The VCDR obligations of protection and inviolability were owed by Castrea to Lorran as the sending state, so Lorran is the injured state under art 42 and may invoke Castrea's responsibility.

  *Consequences.* To the extent any part of the wrong is continuing — for example, if Castrea retains copies of the seized archives — Castrea must cease it and, given the ministerial endorsement, offer assurances and guarantees of non-repetition (art 30). Full reparation (art 31; *Chorzów*) requires: restitution of the embassy, its property and all archives (art 35); compensation for financially assessable damage, such as repairs to the building and the hospitalised diplomat's medical costs (art 36); and satisfaction for the moral injury of the occupation, the detention and the publication — an apology, an expression of regret, or a judgment declaring the conduct wrongful (art 37).

  **Conclusion**

  Castrea is internationally responsible. It breached art 22 VCDR by omission when its police failed to protect the embassy, and it became responsible for the occupation, the detention of the diplomats and the seizure and publication of the archives once its Interior Minister and police acknowledged and adopted the students' conduct (art 11; *Tehran*). Sergeant Ilić's arrest of the First Secretary is attributable under arts 4 and 7 despite his disobedience (*Velásquez Rodríguez*) and breaches art 29. The espionage claim does not justify any of this; the proper remedy was persona non grata under art 9. As the injured state (art 42), Lorran may demand cessation and assurances of non-repetition, restitution of the premises and archives, compensation for damage and medical costs, and satisfaction by way of a formal apology.
