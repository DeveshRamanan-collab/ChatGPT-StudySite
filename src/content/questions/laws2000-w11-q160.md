model: |-
  The issue is when a state that has not joined the Rome Statute must cooperate, and what that means for the Court.
  As a treaty, the Statute binds its parties: States Parties must "cooperate fully with the Court in its investigation
  and prosecution of crimes" (art 86) and have national procedures for every form of cooperation (art 88). A non-party
  is not bound as such, but on this week's materials it comes under a duty in two ways. First, by consent: a non-party
  may accept the Court's jurisdiction over a crime by declaration lodged with the Registrar (art 12(3)), and "the
  accepting State shall cooperate with the Court without any delay or exception in accordance with Part 9". Second,
  through the Security Council: where the Council, acting under Chapter VII, refers a situation (art 13(b), as with
  Libya in 2011) and requires states to cooperate, UN members are bound because under art 25 of the Charter they "agree
  to accept and carry out the decisions of the Security Council". Consequences: outside those two routes the Court
  depends on voluntary cooperation, and for situations in non-party states its reach turns on the Security Council's
  willingness to refer and to require cooperation. Conclusion: an art 12(3) declaration or a binding Chapter VII
  decision.
