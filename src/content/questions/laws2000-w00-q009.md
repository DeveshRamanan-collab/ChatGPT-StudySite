In March 2026 an earthquake jammed the gates shut, and for three weeks no water could be released at all. After the gates were repaired, Arvena's worst drought in a century set in. Arvena reduced the release to 200 cubic metres per second, saying that its capital's reservoirs would otherwise run dry within two months. Arvena did not consult Dessary, did not impose water restrictions in its capital, and continued to supply full flows to its own export cotton farms upstream. Crop losses in Dessary are severe.

Arvenian officials say they did not know of the raid in advance.

  **Issue**

  The issues are: (1) the meaning of Article 3 and whether Article 8 affects it; (2) whether Arvena breached Article 3 during the three-week stoppage, and whether force majeure precludes wrongfulness; (3) whether the later reduction to 200 cubic metres per second is excused by necessity; (4) whether the River Guard's raid is attributable to Arvena; (5) whether Dessary's withholding of fees and airspace closure are lawful countermeasures; and (6) the consequences.

  **Rule**

  Under ARSIWA, which largely codifies custom, an internationally wrongful act requires conduct, by act or omission, that is attributable to a state and breaches its international obligation (art 2). Treaties are interpreted under arts 31–32 VCLT, which reflect custom: ordinary meaning, in context, in the light of object and purpose, with the preamble forming part of the context.

  Force majeure (art 23) precludes wrongfulness where an irresistible force or unforeseen event beyond the state's control makes performance materially impossible; it is unavailable if the state contributed to the situation or assumed the risk. The standard is "absolute and material impossibility" (*Rainbow Warrior*; *Serbian Loans*). Necessity (art 25) is framed in the negative: it may not be invoked unless the act is the only way for the state to safeguard an essential interest against a grave and imminent peril, and does not seriously impair an essential interest of the state towards which the obligation exists. In the *Wall* opinion the construction was not necessary.

  The conduct of private persons is attributable to a state only if they act on its instructions or under its direction or control (art 8). *Nicaragua* held that US participation "even if preponderant or decisive" in financing, organising, training, supplying and equipping the contras, and selecting their targets, was insufficient without "effective control" of the operations in which the violations occurred (para 115). *Tadić*'s looser "overall control" test was rejected for state responsibility in *Application of the Genocide Convention*.

  Countermeasures (arts 22, 49–53) may be taken only against a responsible state, to induce compliance; must not breach jus cogens; must be proportional; and must follow procedural steps — negotiate, notify, give a chance to cease, and end once the wrong ceases. Czechoslovakia's diversion of the Danube was disproportionate (*Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros*). A responsible state must cease a continuing wrong and make full reparation (arts 30–31).

  **Application**

  *(1) Interpretation of Article 3.* Arvena may argue that Article 8 qualifies Article 3 in emergencies. The ordinary meaning of Article 3 — a minimum flow "at all times" — is unqualified. Read in context, Article 8 requires only consultation in an emergency; it does not say the minimum flow is suspended. The preamble's aim "to guarantee Dessary a reliable and predictable flow" confirms that Article 3 is a firm obligation. Under art 31 the meaning is clear, so there is no need to resort to supplementary means under art 32. Arvena's failure to consult is itself a breach of Article 8.

  *(2) The three-week stoppage and force majeure.* Releasing no water at all breached Article 3. The act — or omission — is that of the Arvenian state, which operates the dam. An earthquake is the paradigm of an irresistible, unforeseen event, and the jammed gates made release materially impossible, so on its face art 23 is satisfied. But force majeure is unavailable where the state contributed to the situation. Arvena's own engineers warned in 2024 that the gates were corroded and needed urgent replacement, and the government deferred the work to save money. That decision contributed to the impossibility; arguably Arvena also assumed the risk of gate failure. Force majeure is therefore likely to fail, or at least Dessary has a strong argument that it does.

  *(3) The reduction to 200 and necessity.* The later reduction is a continuing breach of Article 3. Securing a capital city's drinking water is plausibly an essential interest, and reservoirs running dry within two months is arguably a grave and imminent peril. But art 25 is deliberately restrictive. The reduction must be the only way to safeguard that interest. Arvena had other means: it imposed no water restrictions in its capital and continued full flows to its export cotton farms. Diverting water away from its own commercial agriculture would have preserved supply to the capital without cutting Dessary's flow in half. The reduction is therefore not the only way, and as in the *Wall* opinion, necessity fails. Further, halving the flow that irrigates most of Dessary's farmland seriously impairs Dessary's essential interest, so the second limb also fails.

  *(4) The River Guard raid.* The River Guard is not an organ of Arvena. Its conduct is attributable only under art 8. Arvena's army funds, trains and supplies it and helps select targets — precisely the forms of support held insufficient in *Nicaragua*. There is no evidence of instructions for this specific raid, and Arvenian officials deny prior knowledge. On the effective control test, affirmed in *Application of the Genocide Convention*, the raid is probably not attributable to Arvena. Dessary could seek further evidence of control of this operation, or rely on any later acknowledgement by Arvena, but on present facts attribution fails.

  *(5) Dessary's response.* Withholding the Article 11 fees is a potential countermeasure: it is directed against Arvena, a responsible state, to induce it to comply with Article 3, and it involves no use of force or breach of jus cogens. Proportionality is arguable, since financial fees may be commensurate with crop losses. But Dessary failed the procedural requirements: it gave no prior notice and made no attempt at negotiation. As the requirements stand, the withholding is not a valid countermeasure until Dessary calls on Arvena to comply, notifies it, and offers to negotiate. The airspace closure is more problematic. It is not obviously linked to inducing compliance with the Treaty, it affects all civil aviation, and it may breach obligations to which the problem gives no details. Its lawfulness depends on those obligations, and it risks disproportion, as in *Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros*.

  *(6) Consequences.* Arvena must cease its continuing breach of Article 3 and consult under Article 8, and, given its disregard of the Treaty, offer assurances of non-repetition (art 30). It must make full reparation for Dessary's crop losses (art 31), most naturally by compensation for financially assessable damage.

  **Conclusion**

  Article 3 imposes an unqualified minimum-flow obligation; Article 8 requires consultation but does not suspend it. Arvena breached Article 3 during the stoppage, and force majeure likely fails because Arvena contributed to the gate failure by deferring repairs. The reduction to 200 cubic metres per second is not excused by necessity, since it was not the only means — Arvena kept supplying its cotton farms and imposed no restrictions — and it seriously impaired Dessary's essential interest. The River Guard raid is probably not attributable to Arvena on the *Nicaragua* effective control test. Dessary's fee withholding could become a lawful countermeasure once it notifies Arvena and offers to negotiate; the airspace closure is doubtful. Dessary should demand cessation, consultation and compensation.
