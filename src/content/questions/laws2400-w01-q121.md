model: |
  Trespass requires the defendant's conduct to be a "positive act" — a merely passive act will not suffice. The
  lecture gives three examples of conduct that fails this requirement: an act performed during an epileptic
  fit, an act caused by the force of a third party (rather than the defendant's own volition), and a mere
  omission to act. If none of the defendant's own positive, voluntary conduct caused the interference, trespass
  cannot be made out, however directly the harm followed from what happened.

  Separately, trespass is "actionable per se": the plaintiff does not need to prove that any harm or damage
  actually resulted from the interference — the direct interference itself is enough to found the claim. For
  example, a plaintiff who is deliberately touched without consent, but suffers no physical injury or financial
  loss, can still succeed in trespass to the person, because the rationale for this rule is to protect a
  person's bodily integrity, liberty and property from direct interference regardless of whether measurable
  loss can be shown. This is a key contrast with an action on the case, where the plaintiff must positively
  establish that she suffered damage as part of the cause of action itself.
