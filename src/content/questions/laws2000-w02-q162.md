model: |
  Article 38(1) lists treaties, customary international law, general principles of law, and (as a subsidiary
  means) judicial decisions and scholarly writings — but this week's material identifies four further
  categories that can create or evidence obligations outside that list.

  First, unilateral statements or acts: a state can create a binding obligation for itself through a public
  declaration, without any offer, reciprocity or acceptance from another state. In Legal Status of Eastern
  Greenland, the Permanent Court of International Justice held that the Norwegian foreign minister's statement
  that "the Norwegian Government would not make any difficulty in the settlement of this question" was
  unconditional and definitive, binding Norway not to contest Danish sovereignty over Greenland. The Nuclear
  Tests Cases confirm this applies to modern government statements too: French government declarations of an
  intention to cease atmospheric testing were themselves held to be a legal undertaking, based on the
  principle of good faith.

  Second, jus cogens: peremptory norms "accepted and recognized by the international community of States as a
  whole" from which "no derogation is permitted" (VCLT art 53). The ILC's examples include genocide, crimes
  against humanity, aggression, slavery, torture and self-determination; no treaty provision can validly
  contain one, and a treaty that does will be terminated.

  Third, decisions of international organisations: these can bind states where their constitutive treaty
  provides for it. Article 25 UN Charter makes UN Security Council decisions binding on member states — for
  example, Resolution 1373 (2001), requiring states to implement counter-terrorism measures in domestic law.

  Fourth, soft law: non-legally-binding declarations, such as UN General Assembly resolutions, do not
  themselves create obligations, but can provide evidence of state practice or opinio juris and can develop
  into custom or prompt new treaties — the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, itself non-binding, led to
  the binding ICCPR and ICESCR.
