  Harlow & Finch, a firm of chartered accountants, audits the annual accounts of Solace Wellness Pty Ltd, which runs a chain of day spas. Its audit report on last year's accounts was addressed, as usual, to Solace's shareholders. Harlow & Finch negligently failed to detect that Solace's managers had recorded $2 million of unused gift-card sales as earned income, turning a loss into an apparent profit.

  In February, Solace's chief executive gave a copy of the audited accounts to Northpoint Capital, an investment fund considering a $3 million loan to Solace. Northpoint employs a team of credit analysts and normally insists on detailed warranties from borrowers about their accounts; to close this deal quickly, it did not ask for any. Northpoint never contacted Harlow & Finch, which did not know the accounts had been given to a lender. Northpoint lent the money in reliance on the accounts.

  In March, Solace's founder, Ruby, emailed Ken, the Harlow & Finch audit partner, saying that her uncle Walter, a retired bus driver, was thinking of putting his $150,000 of savings into Solace shares, and asking whether the accounts were "sound". Walter was copied in. Ken replied to both of them: "Happy to help, Walter. Our audit gives Solace a clean bill of health — the accounts fairly show a profitable business. I've only had time to look at the summary pages again today, but I'd be comfortable investing." The email ended with Harlow & Finch's standard footer, in small grey type: "This email contains general information only. Harlow & Finch accepts no responsibility to any person for reliance on it." Walter bought the shares the next day.

  Six months later Solace collapsed. Northpoint has lost $2.4 million and Walter has lost all of his $150,000.

  Advise Northpoint Capital and Walter whether they can recover their losses from Harlow & Finch.
model: |
  **Issue**

  The issues are: (1) whether Harlow & Finch owed Northpoint a duty of care in respect of its audit; (2) whether it owed Walter a duty in respect of Ken's email, and the effect of the footer and of Ken's remark about the summary pages; (3) breach and causation under the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA"); and (4) whether either plaintiff has a claim under s 18 of the Australian Consumer Law ("ACL").

  **Rule**

  Pure economic loss is "harm" under CLA s 5, but "the mere foreseeability of harm does not, where the only harm is pure economic loss, give rise to a duty of care"; the law requires "a special relationship between the parties" (Dawson J, Esanda Finance Corp v PMH (1997) 188 CLR 241). Under Hedley Byrne v Heller [1964] AC 465 a duty for negligent words may arise where the inquirer trusts the speaker to take care and reasonably relies, and the speaker knows or ought to know of the reliance (Lord Reid), or where a person with special skill undertakes to apply it for another who relies on it (Lord Morris). A disclaimer of responsibility, which excludes the duty, must be distinguished from a warning that care has not been taken, which goes to whether reliance was reasonable.

  The salient features are weighed as a whole. Esanda (no duty from auditors to a lender), Tepko Pty Ltd v Water Board (2001) 206 CLR 1 (known reliance or an assumption of responsibility needed) and Anchorage Capital Master Offshore Ltd v Sparkes (2023) 111 NSWLR 304 (sophisticated lenders not vulnerable) mark the limits. Breach falls under s 5B and causation under s 5D. ACL s 18 prohibits misleading or deceptive conduct in trade or commerce, with no need to prove a duty or negligence, though a causal link is still needed.

  **Application**

  *Northpoint — duty.* Northpoint's claim is almost exactly Esanda. A financier lent to a company in reliance on audited accounts that the company itself handed over. Harlow & Finch received no request from Northpoint, did not know the accounts had gone to a lender, did not intend to induce anyone to lend, and assumed no responsibility to Northpoint; its report was addressed to shareholders, whom an audit exists to inform. Loss to someone relying on the accounts was foreseeable, but that is not enough. Every lender, investor or supplier who might see a company's audited accounts would form an undefinable class, and McHugh J's policy concerns — higher auditing costs, more complex litigation, indeterminacy and difficulty proving reliance — apply with full force. Vulnerability points the same way: like the lenders in Anchorage, Northpoint is a sophisticated commercial party with its own credit analysts, and it normally protects itself by warranties; it chose not to here. No duty is owed to Northpoint.

  *Walter — duty.* Walter's position is very different. Ken was asked a direct question, knew who would rely on the answer — Walter, copied in and addressed by name — and knew why: to decide whether to invest his $150,000 of savings. That is the known reliance missing in Esanda and Tepko. Ken is the audit partner commenting on his own firm's audit, so he has special skill and, by replying "Happy to help, Walter", undertook to apply it for Walter (Lord Morris). The setting was professional, not social. Walter, a retired bus driver, could not check the accounts himself and had no contract through which to protect himself: unlike the lenders in Anchorage, he was vulnerable. There is no indeterminacy, because the duty would run to one identified person. On these features a duty is strongly arguable.

  *The footer.* Harlow & Finch's best answer is the footer. In Hedley Byrne itself, a reference given "without responsibility" meant no duty arose, and the footer's words — "accepts no responsibility to any person for reliance on it" — are a disclaimer of responsibility, not merely a warning. Walter will reply that the words must be read in context. The footer is standard text in small grey type attached to every email the firm sends; it describes the email as "general information only", while the body gives specific, personal advice to a named individual about a specific investment. In Hedley Byrne the disclaimer was attached to the very reference that was relied on. A disclaimer is one factor against a duty, weighed with the circumstances as a whole; a court may find that Ken's specific undertaking outweighs a boilerplate footer. It is the main risk in Walter's claim.

  *The summary-pages remark.* Ken's statement that he had "only had time to look at the summary pages again today" is a warning that care has not been taken, not a disclaimer, so it goes to whether Walter's reliance was reasonable. It qualifies only Ken's fresh look, not the firm's audit opinion — "Our audit gives Solace a clean bill of health" — and it came with a confident recommendation: "I'd be comfortable investing". This is not the reluctant, provisional "ball-park" figure of Tepko. Walter's reliance on the audit opinion was reasonable.

  *Breach and causation.* Under s 5B, the risk that a $2 million overstatement would mislead those relying on the accounts was foreseeable and not insignificant, and a reasonable auditor would have tested how gift-card sales were recorded — the very task the firm undertook. Ken repeated the negligent audit's conclusion to Walter. Under s 5D(1)(a), Walter must show he would not have invested had he been told the accounts were unsound. Because this turns on what he would have done, s 5D(3) requires it to be decided subjectively on all the circumstances, and his after-the-event statements are inadmissible unless against his interest; but buying the shares the day after Ken's reassurance is strong circumstantial evidence. His loss on Solace's collapse is within scope (s 5D(1)(b), (4)): it is exactly the risk that accounts showing a loss-making business as profitable create.

  *ACL s 18.* Harlow & Finch, a professional firm advising in the course of its business, acts in trade or commerce, and Ken's statement that the accounts "fairly show a profitable business" was false and, on its face, misleading. Section 18 needs no duty of care, so it sidesteps the duty question the footer is aimed at, although Walter must still show a causal link; buying the next day helps. Northpoint's s 18 case is weaker: the firm said nothing to it, and its own choice not to seek warranties weakens causation.

  **Conclusion**

  Northpoint is unlikely to recover: on Esanda, auditors owe no duty to a lender relying on accounts passed on by the company, and Northpoint, a sophisticated party that chose not to protect itself, was not vulnerable (Anchorage). Walter's claim is much stronger: Ken knew of and invited his reliance, and Walter could not protect himself. The footer is the main obstacle, because Hedley Byrne shows a disclaimer can prevent a duty arising; but read against Ken's specific, personal advice it may not carry the same weight, and the summary-pages remark does not make reliance on the audit unreasonable. If a duty is found, breach and causation should follow. Walter should also plead ACL s 18, probably his easier route.
