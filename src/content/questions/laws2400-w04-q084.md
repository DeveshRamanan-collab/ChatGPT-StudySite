  Diego can raise self-defence, and his prospects look reasonably strong on the facts given. At common
  law, Zecevic v DPP requires Diego to prove (1) a reasonable apprehension of physical force to himself,
  and (2) that the force he used was proportionate to the threat. The stranger's raised fist, in an
  aggressive confrontation, would likely support a reasonable apprehension that he was about to be
  punched. As to proportionality, a single hard push in response to an imminent punch is a comparatively
  measured response — unlike the disproportionate response in Fontin v Katapodis, where the defendant
  responded to a blow with a T-square by throwing glass at the plaintiff's face. That the stranger happened
  to fall and suffer a broken wrist does not necessarily make the push itself disproportionate, since
  proportionality is assessed by reference to the response chosen, not solely its unintended consequences.
  Section 52 of the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) additionally requires that the conduct Diego was
  responding to have been unlawful — the stranger's aggressive advance with a raised fist, apparently
  about to strike Diego, would likely satisfy this, since a threatened battery is itself unlawful conduct.
  Diego would also need to show he believed his push was necessary to defend himself, and that it was a
  reasonable response in the circumstances as he perceived them — again plausible on these facts. Both
  regimes operate in parallel, so Diego does not need to elect between them; if either test is satisfied,
  he has a defence to the battery claim, and on these facts both look capable of being made out.
