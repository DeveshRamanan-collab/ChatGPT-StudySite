  **Issue**

  Five issues arise: (1) whether the March Resolution binds Belvane and overrides its treaty with Sarovia; (2) whether the coalition's proposed use of force is authorised; (3) what the General Assembly can do; (4) the principles governing a UN peacekeeping operation; and (5) what further non-forcible measures are available.

  **Rule**

  The Security Council has primary responsibility for international peace and security and acts on the members' behalf (art 24). Chapter VII begins with an art 39 determination that there is a threat to the peace, breach of the peace or act of aggression, after which the Council may make recommendations or decide on measures. To prevent aggravation it may call on the parties to comply with provisional measures, without prejudice to their rights (art 40). It may decide on measures "not involving the use of armed force" (art 41), a non-exhaustive list extending to comprehensive and targeted sanctions, ad hoc criminal tribunals and referral to the ICC (Rome Statute art 13(b)). Only if those measures "would be inadequate or have proved to be inadequate" may it take action by air, sea or land forces (art 42). Its decisions bind all members (art 25), and Charter obligations prevail over conflicting treaty obligations (art 103). Because art 43 was never implemented, the Council authorises member states, coalitions or regional organisations, typically to use "all necessary means" (Res 678, Kuwait 1990). Claims to revive or imply an authorisation, as in Iraq in 2003, were rejected by most Council and UN members and most scholars.

  The General Assembly may discuss and recommend (arts 11(2), 14), but while the Council is exercising its functions on a dispute or situation the Assembly may not make recommendations on it unless the Council requests (art 12(1); *Wall*, paras 27–28). Under Uniting for Peace (Res 377(V)), where the Council fails to act because of a lack of unanimity of the permanent members, the Assembly "shall consider the matter immediately with a view to making appropriate recommendations to Members for collective measures". The Assembly recommends; the Council decides. UN peacekeeping is not mentioned in the Charter; it rests on consent of the parties, impartiality and non-use of force except in self-defence.

  **Application**

  *(1) The armoured vehicles.* The March Resolution was adopted "acting under Chapter VII" after an express art 39 determination that the situation threatens international peace and security. Its call for a ceasefire operates as an art 40 provisional measure. Its arms embargo, the asset freezes and the travel bans are art 41 measures. Because the Council "decided" that all states shall prevent the supply of arms to Sarovia, the embargo is a decision, not a recommendation, and binds every member under art 25. Belvane's 2019 treaty obliges it to deliver armoured vehicles — military equipment within the embargo — so the two obligations conflict. Under art 103 the Charter obligation prevails. Belvane must not deliver the vehicles, and Sarovia cannot hold it to the treaty while the embargo stands.

  *(2) The coalition's argument.* Security Council authorisation under art 42 is one of only two exceptions to the prohibition on force in art 2(4). The March Resolution contains no authorisation. Its statement that the Council "will consider further measures" looks to a future decision; it does not make one. Express authorisation uses words such as Resolution 678's "all necessary means". The coalition's argument resembles the 2003 claim that Resolutions 678, 687 and 1441 revived or implied an authorisation against Iraq — a claim rejected by most other Council and UN members and by most legal scholars. The 13 votes for the vetoed draft add nothing: a vetoed draft is not a decision of the Council (art 27(3)), and the veto is part of the Charter system all members accepted. With no armed attack on the coalition states and no consent from Sarovia, the proposed force would be unlawful. Unilateral humanitarian intervention has no Charter basis — Kosovo in 1999 was called "illegal, yet legitimate" — and the responsibility to protect works through UN mechanisms, not around them.

  *(3) The General Assembly.* The Assembly may discuss Sarovia and make recommendations (art 11(2)), and may recommend measures for the peaceful adjustment of the situation (art 14). Art 12(1) is an obstacle while the Council is exercising its functions on Sarovia, and the March Resolution shows that it is. But the September veto is exactly the situation Uniting for Peace addresses: the Council has failed to exercise its primary responsibility "because of lack of unanimity of the permanent members". The Assembly may therefore consider the matter immediately and recommend collective measures to members. Its resolutions remain recommendations: they carry the political weight of a body in which every member has one vote (art 18), but they do not bind as art 25 decisions do. The Assembly recommends; only the Council decides under Chapter VII.

  *(4) Peacekeeping.* A peacekeeping operation would be a UN operation, unlike an authorisation to states, and would traditionally rest on three principles: consent of the parties, impartiality, and non-use of force except in self-defence. Consent is the difficulty here: the junta is blocking UN convoys and may refuse a mission, and with three warring parties the consent of all may be hard to obtain. Practice has evolved: missions in the DRC (including targeted offensive operations) and Mali have been authorised to use force to carry out their mandates. A mission with a mandate to protect civilians by force would, however, itself need a Council decision, and the same permanent member could veto it.

  *(5) Further non-forcible measures.* Art 41's list is non-exhaustive. The Council could extend targeted or "smart" sanctions — asset freezes and travel bans on faction commanders and on those supplying weapons, or bans on trade in particular commodities — which are preferred to comprehensive sanctions because of the harm comprehensive sanctions have done to civilian populations, as in Iraq in the 1990s. It could establish an ad hoc international criminal tribunal, as it did for the former Yugoslavia and Rwanda, or refer the situation in Sarovia to the ICC Prosecutor under art 13(b) of the Rome Statute, as it did for Darfur in 2005; a Council referral needs no territorial or nationality link. Each would bind members under art 25, but each needs a resolution that survives the veto.

  **Conclusion**

  The March Resolution is a binding Chapter VII decision (arts 39–41, 25), and art 103 requires Belvane to withhold the armoured vehicles despite its treaty. The coalition has no authorisation: "will consider further measures" is not "all necessary means", a vetoed draft is not a decision, and revived or implied authorisation was rejected in 2003. The General Assembly may act under Uniting for Peace, but only by recommendation. A peacekeeping operation would depend on consent and on a Council mandate, which the veto may block. The Council's most realistic further steps are targeted sanctions, a tribunal or an ICC referral under art 41 — each, again, subject to the veto.
