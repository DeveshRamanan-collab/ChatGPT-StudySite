model: |-
  The statement is the strongest reply an Austinian can make to Hart's content objection, but it distorts what such
  rules do. In Chapter III Hart distinguishes duty-imposing from power-conferring rules. Some criminal law resembles
  coercive orders: this is forbidden, and if you do it you go to jail. But contracts, wills and marriage are different:
  the law gives people powers to do things if they follow the steps. There is no duty to make a will; failing to follow
  the procedure produces no valid will, not a sanction. The counterargument, which Hart himself states in the replies
  Austin or someone like Kelsen could make, is that power-conferring rules can somehow be fitted back into the model of
  orders backed by threats; Austin himself recognised such laws. Hart's strategy answers it on Austin's own criteria: a descriptive theory of positive law
  must explain elements of real legal systems as they are, and if the theory does not fit the facts, you change the
  theory. On balance the redescription saves the model only by misdescribing the practice, so the objection stands.
