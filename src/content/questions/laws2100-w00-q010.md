prompt: |
  For Hart, a legal system exists when its valid rules are generally obeyed and its rules of recognition, change and adjudication are "effectively accepted as common public standards of official behaviour by its officials" (CL 109). The rule of recognition is a social rule: it exists because officials converge in accepting it, and it supplies the criteria by which the rules of the system are identified.

  In *Law's Empire* Dworkin identified a feature of legal practice he said this picture cannot explain. Lawsuits raise three kinds of issue: issues of fact, issues of law, and "the twinned issues of political morality and fidelity" (LE 3). Judges often agree about the facts, and even about the text of the relevant statute, yet disagree about what the law is. In *Riggs v Palmer* the statutes on wills were clear, and the judges agreed that Elmer Palmer had murdered his grandfather; they disagreed about whether the law gave him the inheritance. Dworkin calls this theoretical disagreement. If the criteria of validity rest on official agreement, how can officials disagree about what those criteria require? Dworkin's alternative is to treat law as an interpretive practice, studied "from the judge's viewpoint" (LE 14), whose unity comes from the principles of the community's political morality rather than from its structure.

  Critically evaluate the following statement:

  "Theoretical disagreement shows that the rule of recognition cannot be the foundation of a legal system."

  In your essay, explain what Hart's theory needs officials to agree about, and whether theoretical disagreement shows that they do not. Consider whether Dworkin's alternative is a general theory of law or an account of particular legal systems. Draw on material from more than one week of the unit. Take a clear position, support it with reasons, and deal with the strongest counterargument to your view.
model: |
  **Thesis**

  The statement is too strong. Theoretical disagreement is a real feature of legal practice, and it does show that the content of a rule of recognition cannot be fixed only by what officials already agree on. But it does not show that the rule of recognition cannot be the foundation of a legal system. Hart's theory needs officials to converge on the sources of law and on a practice of accepting them as common standards, not on the answer to every question those sources raise. Disagreement at the margin is consistent with convergence at the core. Dworkin's alternative, meanwhile, is better understood as an account of how judges in some legal systems argue than as a general theory of what law is.

  **Argument**

  *What Hart needs.* The rule of recognition provides "authoritative criteria for identifying primary rules of obligation" — enactment, custom, precedent (CL 100). It exists as a practice: it is manifest in the way officials identify rules. For a legal system to exist, valid rules must be generally obeyed and secondary rules must be "effectively accepted as common public standards of official behaviour by its officials" (CL 109). Nothing in these conditions requires agreement on every application. Acceptance is a "critical reflective attitude" to a pattern of behaviour as a common standard (CL 57), and people can share a standard while disagreeing about what it requires in a given case.

  *The argument from disagreement.* Dworkin's challenge is sharp. The three kinds of issue in a lawsuit are empirical (what happened?), legal (what is the pertinent law?) and moral (if the law denies compensation, is that unjust?). In *Riggs* the dispute was of the second kind. The majority held that laws "may be controlled in their operation and effect by general, fundamental maxims of the common law"; the minority held that the laws were silent and that refusing Elmer would impose an additional punishment. Both sides accepted the statute's validity and the facts; they disagreed about what the law is. If the rule of recognition exists only because officials converge, then where they diverge there is no rule, and so no law — which is not how the judges understood their own dispute. They argued as if there were a legal answer to find.

  *Why convergence at the core survives.* The argument assumes that Hart's rule of recognition must be as determinate as the judges' conclusions. But Hart's account of general language applies to secondary rules as well as primary ones. General terms have a core of settled application and a penumbra of doubt (CL 126), and "uncertainty at the borderline is the price to be paid for the use of general classifying terms" (CL 128). There is no reason the criteria of validity should be exempt. In *Riggs*, the judges agreed that statutes and common law maxims are sources of law; they disagreed about how those sources interact when the statute's words and a common law principle point in different directions. That is disagreement within a shared practice, not evidence that there is no shared practice. Hart's inclusive positivism can also accommodate the majority's reasoning: where the rule of recognition itself incorporates moral criteria, a judge may consult them (CL 269).

  **Counterargument**

  Dworkin's strongest reply is that this reading misdescribes what the judges are doing. On Hart's account, disagreement in the penumbra means there is no law to find, so the judge must exercise discretion and make law. But the *Riggs* judges did not see themselves as making law. Each side claimed the law was on its side. If we take the participants' point of view seriously, as Dworkin does in studying legal argument "from the judge's viewpoint" (LE 14), Hart's theory treats their disagreement as confused. Dworkin's own theory takes it at face value: law is an interpretive practice, its unity comes from integrity — consistency in principle — and a judge tests competing readings on the dimensions of fit and justification (LE 255). On this account, theoretical disagreement is disagreement about which interpretation shows the practice in its best light, and the rule of recognition is not needed at all.

  **Evaluation**

  The reply shows that Hart must explain why judges argue as if there were an answer. He can: acceptance from the internal point of view includes treating the rules as reasons to criticise and to act, and a participant who accepts a practice will naturally argue that her reading is the one the practice requires. That she argues this way does not show that the practice determines one answer. Finnis's point about the incommensurability of fit and justification suggests that, in genuinely hard cases, it often does not.

  More importantly, Dworkin's alternative pays a price as a general theory. For Dworkin the point of the legal system is to justify the exercise of state coercion, and its unity comes from the principles of its political morality. That is an attractive account of a liberal legal system whose judges argue in terms of principle. It is harder to apply to a system whose political morality is evil. Does law as integrity fit apartheid South Africa? If the best interpretation of such a practice is still a justification of it, integrity loses its critical edge; if there is no justifying interpretation, the theory seems to say there was no law, which is not what those subject to it experienced. Hart's conditions — structure, not point — describe both kinds of system. This is the difference between a general jurisprudence and an account of a particular legal culture.

  The statement should therefore be rejected. Theoretical disagreement is real, and it shows that Hart must treat the rule of recognition as open-textured. But a rule can be the foundation of a practice without settling every dispute within it, and Dworkin's interpretive alternative explains disagreement in our legal system at the cost of generality.
