  **Issue**

  Four issues arise: (a) whether the ICC can exercise jurisdiction over three sets of conduct — the shelling of Brask, the expulsions inside Kestovia and the bus station bombing; (b) whether a case against General Varn would be admissible, given Kestovia's investigation; (c) whether President Dahl's office, superior orders or Kestovian law shield the accused; and (d) what Australia must do if the Court issues a warrant for Varn's arrest.

  **Rule**

  The ICC's jurisdiction is tested through four gates. First, the crime: jurisdiction is limited to "the most serious crimes of concern to the international community as a whole" — genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and the crime of aggression (art 5). Second, time: only crimes committed after the Statute entered into force, and for a later party after it entered into force for that state, unless it made an art 12(3) declaration (art 11). Third, a trigger: a State Party referral (arts 13(a), 14), a Security Council referral under Chapter VII (art 13(b)), or the Prosecutor acting proprio motu (arts 13(c), 15). Fourth, preconditions: for a State Party referral or a proprio motu investigation, the territorial state or the accused's state of nationality must be a party or have accepted jurisdiction by declaration (art 12(2)–(3)). A Security Council referral needs no such link.

  The Court is "complementary to national criminal jurisdictions" (art 1). Under art 17 a case is inadmissible where a state with jurisdiction is investigating or prosecuting it, "unless the State is unwilling or unable genuinely" to do so. Unwillingness is shown by proceedings undertaken to shield the person, unjustified delay, or proceedings not conducted independently or impartially. The case must also be of sufficient gravity.

  The Nuremberg Principles (GA Res 95(I), 1946) close three doors: that internal law imposes no penalty (Principle II), official position as head of state (Principle III), and superior orders, "provided a moral choice was in fact possible" (Principle IV). Immunity is procedural; it "does not mean … impunity", and prosecution remains possible before international criminal courts (*Arrest Warrant*). States Parties must "cooperate fully" with the Court (art 86) and have procedures under national law for all forms of cooperation (art 88).

  **Application**

  *(a) The shelling of Brask.* Deliberately shelling a town with no military targets, killing more than 200 civilians, on orders "to empty it", is conduct capable of amounting to war crimes (art 8); the IMT Charter's war crimes already included the wanton destruction of towns not justified by military necessity. The conduct occurred in 2025, after the Statute entered into force for Lumeria in 2010, so the time gate is passed (art 11). Lumeria's referral is a State Party referral (arts 13(a), 14). Under art 12(2)(a) it is enough that the state on whose territory the conduct occurred is a party, and Brask is in Lumeria. The precondition is territory or nationality, not both, so the fact that the alleged perpetrators are nationals of a non-party is no bar. Kestovia's first argument fails for the shelling.

  *The expulsions.* Rounding up and expelling thousands of members of a linguistic minority and burning their homes is capable of amounting to crimes against humanity (art 7); deportation and persecution have been listed as crimes against humanity since the IMT Charter. But this conduct occurred in Kestovia, by Kestovian nationals, and Kestovia is not a party. Neither of the art 12(2) states is a party, so on Lumeria's referral the Court cannot exercise jurisdiction over it, and Kestovia's first argument succeeds to that extent. Two routes remain. Kestovia could accept jurisdiction by an art 12(3) declaration — unlikely on the facts. Or the Security Council, acting under Chapter VII, could refer the situation to the Prosecutor (art 13(b)), as it did for Libya in 2011; a Council referral needs no territorial or nationality link, and as a Chapter VII decision it would bind UN members, who agree to carry out the Council's decisions (art 25 UN Charter).

  *The bus station bombing.* This fails the first gate. Terrorism is not among the art 5 crimes, so the Court cannot prosecute the Free Border Group "for terrorism", whatever the merits of Kestovia's fifth argument; the bombing also occurred in a non-party. It is a matter for national courts.

  *(b) Admissibility of a case against Varn.* Kestovia has opened an investigation into Varn, so art 17(1)(a) is engaged, and the question is whether Kestovia is unwilling genuinely to carry it out. The file has been given to a colonel on Varn's own staff, so the investigation is not being conducted independently or impartially. No witnesses have been interviewed and Varn remains in command, which suggests an investigation opened to shield her rather than to bring her to justice. Kestovia's courts have not collapsed, so this is unwillingness, not inability. Gravity is plainly met by a deliberate attack that killed more than 200 civilians. A case against Varn would be admissible.

  *(c) Head of state and superior orders.* Argument (3) fails. Principle III provides that acting as head of state does not relieve a person of responsibility under international law. President Dahl's personal immunity as a serving head of state would bar proceedings against him in another state's national courts while he is in office, but immunity is a procedural bar, and *Arrest Warrant* recognises that prosecution remains possible before international criminal courts. Argument (4) also fails. Under Principle IV, orders from the President do not relieve Varn of responsibility, because a moral choice was in fact possible to a general ordered to shell a town with no military targets. Under Principle II, the National Security Decree is irrelevant: that Kestovian law permits the conduct does not relieve Varn of responsibility under international law.

  *(d) Australia's obligations.* Australia is a State Party, so it must cooperate fully with the Court in its investigation and prosecution of crimes (art 86) and must have procedures under its national law for all forms of cooperation (art 88). If the Court issues a warrant and requests Varn's arrest and surrender while she is in Sydney, Australia must act on the request through those procedures. Varn is a general, not a head of state, head of government or foreign minister, so she does not hold personal immunity.

  **Conclusion**

  The Court can exercise jurisdiction over the shelling of Brask, because it occurred on the territory of a State Party (art 12(2)(a)), even though the alleged perpetrators are Kestovian. It cannot exercise jurisdiction over the expulsions on Lumeria's referral; only an art 12(3) declaration by Kestovia or a Security Council referral under art 13(b) would open that door. Terrorism is outside art 5. A case against Varn is admissible, because Kestovia's investigation lacks independence and appears designed to shield her (art 17). Neither the President's office, nor superior orders, nor Kestovian law excuses the conduct (Nuremberg Principles II–IV), and immunity does not bar proceedings before the ICC. Australia must cooperate with any request to arrest and surrender Varn (arts 86 and 88).
