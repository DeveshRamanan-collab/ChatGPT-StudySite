prompt: A statute lets the State Minister ban dangerous toys, but for twenty years the State has left toy safety to an industry code. It has received consumer complaints about loose magnets in Brand Z toys and has not acted. Leo, aged 4, swallows magnets from a Brand Z toy and is seriously injured. Leo sues the State. Advise whether the State owed Leo a duty of care at common law (ignore the Civil Liability Act).
model: "The issue is whether the State owed an affirmative duty to protect consumers through its power to ban toys. Reasonable foreseeability of injury from magnets is clear, but for a public authority it is not sufficient. The complaint is about an omission — non-feasance — which Gleeson CJ in Graham Barclay Oysters v Ryan said makes the governments' position different from that of ordinary citizens. Leaving toy safety to an industry code is a policy decision; as in Graham Barclay, there is no criterion by which a court can judge the reasonableness of allowing an industry a substantial measure of self-regulation. The banning power exists for the public generally, not a specific class. Amaca v NSW adds that a duty does not arise merely because the State might have prevented harm by exercising its power, and knowledge from complaints is not itself sufficient. On control, the manufacturer and retailers controlled the toy far more than the State, as James Hardie did in Amaca, and consumers rely on them rather than on the State, so Leo was not vulnerable to the State in the relevant sense. Unlike Crimmins, the State did not direct Leo or exercise close supervisory control. Conclusion: the State very likely owed no duty; Leo's claim lies against the manufacturer or supplier."
