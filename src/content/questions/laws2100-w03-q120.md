model: |-
  The separation is not dangerous once it is understood as the narrow claim Hart defends. Bentham, Austin and Hart
  defend the distinction between law as it is and law as it ought to be. Hart takes positivism to be the contention that
  it is in no sense a necessary truth that laws satisfy morality, though in fact they often do (CL 185–186). His Bentham
  and Austin were not dry analysts but reformers who wanted better laws, and they asserted two simple things: that a
  rule's immorality does not alone show it is not law, and its moral desirability does not alone make it law (599). They
  never denied that law mirrors morality in fact, or that morality can enter law by express provision. The strongest
  counterargument is the practical problem of morally evil laws, such as under the Nazi regime: calling such rules law
  may seem to lend them authority. But separation is what makes criticism possible — a clear understanding of law is 'an
  important preliminary to any useful moral criticism of law' (CL 240) — and saying a rule is law is not saying it should
  be obeyed. On balance the danger lies in confusing validity with obligation, not in the separation itself.
