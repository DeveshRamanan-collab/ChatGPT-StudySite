  **Issue**

  Two issues arise. First, whether Northmark satisfies the criteria for statehood and whether Australia should recognise it. Second, whether the Calvenian National Grain Board can claim state immunity from Harbour Mills' contract claim in the Supreme Court of New South Wales.

  **Rule**

  Article 1 of the Montevideo Convention (1933), widely regarded as codifying custom, provides that a state should possess a permanent population, a defined territory, government, and capacity to enter into relations with other states. There is no minimum population or territory, and there is "no rule that the land frontiers of a State must be fully delimited" (*North Sea Continental Shelf* [46]). Effective government — a coherent, effective exercise of public power — is the most demanding criterion, and new entities face a stricter effectiveness test than established states, which benefit from a presumption of continuity. Capacity for relations arises from independence.

  Self-determination is recognised in UN Charter art 1(2) and ICCPR art 1. Outside decolonisation, a right of unilateral secession is highly contentious: in *Re Secession of Quebec* the Supreme Court of Canada held that where a people's internal self-determination is fulfilled, there is no ground for external self-determination.

  Recognition is the act by which a state acknowledges another state. Under the declaratory theory (the most widely held today) recognition only acknowledges pre-existing statehood; under the constitutive theory it creates the state. Non-recognition may be required where the criteria were achieved illegally — the 1970 Friendly Relations Declaration, and the *Kosovo* Advisory Opinion's reference to "the unlawful use of force or other egregious violations of norms of general international law". Security Council resolutions 541 and 550 called the TRNC's declaration legally invalid, and it is recognised only by Turkey.

  State immunity is grounded in sovereign equality and customary international law. The UN Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and their Property (2004) is not in force, but its articles mostly reflect custom, and Australia's *Foreign States Immunities Act 1985* (Cth) grants immunity by default, lost by waiver or for commercial transactions. "State" includes agencies entitled to exercise sovereign authority, acting in that capacity (art 2(1)(b)). Under the restrictive approach, acts jure gestionis are not immune: art 10 removes immunity in proceedings arising out of a commercial transaction with a foreign natural or juridical person, and art 2(2) directs attention primarily to the nature of the transaction. In *Trendex* a state organ that failed to honour a letter of credit was refused immunity; in *Playa Largo* Cuba's diversion of a sugar cargo for foreign-policy reasons remained commercial.

  **Application**

  *Population and territory.* Northmark has a permanent population of 1.2 million living there for generations; there is no minimum, so this criterion is met. The eastern boundary has never been surveyed and a strip remains under Calvenian control. Following *North Sea Continental Shelf*, boundaries need not be fully delimited or undisputed, so this is not fatal on its own.

  *Government.* This is Northmark's weakest point. The Provisional Council administers about 80 per cent of the province, collects taxes and runs schools, which shows a real exercise of public power. But Calvenia still holds the regional capital's airport and part of the territory, and the Council's position depends on Ostravan troops. Because Northmark is a new entity seeking to break away, it faces the stricter effectiveness test; control shared with the parent state and propped up by a foreign army is unlikely to be the coherent, independent government the criterion requires.

  *Capacity for relations.* The Council has written to 40 states, but capacity arises from independence. If its authority depends on Ostrava, its capacity to conduct relations on its own is doubtful.

  *Self-determination.* This is not a decolonisation case. Northmarkers are represented in the national parliament, have a regional assembly with real powers, and have language rights in the courts. Like Quebec, their internal self-determination appears fulfilled, so there is no ground for an external right to secede. The trigger — a dispute over mining royalties — resembles a claim to resource ownership rather than a response to systematic oppression of the kind argued in Kosovo.

  *Recognition.* On the declaratory theory, Australia's recognition would not create Northmark; the question is whether the criteria are met, and they probably are not. On the constitutive theory, Northmark would exist only once recognised; one recognition, by Ostrava, is plainly insufficient. More importantly, Ostrava's troops entered the day before the declaration, and Northmark's control depends on them. If that entry was an unlawful use of force, the Friendly Relations Declaration and the *Kosovo* opinion support non-recognition, as with the TRNC, which meets the criteria yet is recognised only by Turkey. Recognising Northmark now would risk endorsing a situation created by force. Australia should withhold recognition; that it does not open diplomatic relations is consistent with this.

  *Is the Board a "State"?* The Board is statutory, government-owned and holds an export monopoly, but it is not clear that it is entitled to exercise sovereign authority. If it is not, it is simply a commercial entity with no claim to immunity at all. If it is, it must also be acting in that capacity in this instance.

  *Commercial transaction.* Even assuming the Board is a "State", selling wheat to a foreign company is a commercial transaction under arts 2(1)(c) and 10. Under art 2(2) the nature of the transaction — a contract of sale — is decisive; the parties did not agree that purpose would be relevant. Calvenia will argue that the diversion was an act of national security and foreign policy, jure imperii. *Playa Largo* answers this directly: Cuba's diversion of a sugar cargo for foreign-policy reasons did not convert a commercial dealing into a sovereign act. As in *Trendex*, a state entity that fails to perform a commercial obligation cannot shelter behind immunity. The FSIA similarly removes immunity for commercial transactions.

  **Conclusion**

  Northmark probably meets the population and territory criteria but fails the stricter effective-government and independence tests while Calvenian forces hold part of the province and its control depends on Ostravan troops. Northmarkers enjoy internal self-determination, so there is no right to secede (*Re Secession of Quebec*), and the apparent role of Ostrava's use of force supports non-recognition (Friendly Relations Declaration; *Kosovo*). Australia should not recognise Northmark. The Board is not immune: either it is not a "State" at all, or, if it is, its wheat contract is a commercial transaction and the foreign-policy motive for the diversion is irrelevant (arts 2(2) and 10; *Playa Largo*; *Trendex*). Harbour Mills' claim may proceed; enforcement against Calvenian assets would raise a separate immunity question.
