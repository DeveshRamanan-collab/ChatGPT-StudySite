  **Thesis**

  The statement is right about what judges do and broadly right that it should be admitted, but "nothing wrong" goes too far. In the penumbra of open-textured rules the existing law does not determine a single answer, so judges must choose, and a choice that settles the law for the future is law-making. Dworkin's claim that the judge never steps outside the law depends on the one-right-answer thesis, which fails once fit and justification are seen to be incommensurable. But Dworkin's normative objections are not empty. Admitting judicial law-making is the right description only if it comes with the constraints Hart himself placed on it; without them, there would be something wrong with it.

  **Argument**

  *The descriptive claim.* Law must communicate general standards through classifying terms: "If it were not possible to communicate general standards of conduct … nothing that we now recognize as law could exist" (CL 124). General language has a core — "If anything is a vehicle a motor-car is one" — and a penumbra: bicycles, aeroplanes, roller skates (CL 126). Natural languages are irreducibly open-textured, and we legislate in relative ignorance of fact and of aim. Every legal system compromises between rules individuals can apply to themselves and issues left open for "an informed, official choice" in the concrete case (CL 130). In those cases there is, as the Postscript puts it, a law that is "partly indeterminate or incomplete" (CL 272), and the judge must make a choice between open alternatives.

  *The realists add weight, but not too much.* The realists said the Nightmare was the truth: the image of the judge applying existing law is an illusion, and judicial law-making is an essential feature of law. Hart treats this as an exaggeration. Formalism and rule-scepticism "are great exaggerations, salutary where they correct each other, and the truth lies between them" (CL 147). The statement is limited to hard cases, which is exactly the ground Hart concedes; it does not say that every case is hard.

  *Dworkin's alternative fails as description.* Dworkin accepts weak discretion — standards that demand judgment — but denies strong discretion, where the official "is simply not bound by standards set by the authority in question" (TRS 32). The law includes implicit principles, so an obligation "might be imposed by a constellation of principles as well as by an established rule" (TRS 44). Hercules, "an imaginary judge of superhuman intellectual power and patience who accepts law as integrity" (LE 239), tests interpretations on two dimensions, fit and justification (LE 255). Even in the hardest case, where two interpretations fit equally well, the judge is not to make law. That is why Hart called Dworkin "the noblest dreamer of all".

  The difficulty is the one-right-answer thesis. Finnis, in 'On Reason and Authority in *Law's Empire*', argues that fit and justification are incommensurable: there is no common measure by which a better-fitting but less just reading can be ranked against a less well-fitting but more just one. If that is right, then in a genuinely hard case the judge's choice between readings is not dictated by the law. Hart's reply to Dworkin's first criticism makes the same point from the other side: judges "do not just push away their law books", but even Dworkin's own theory incorporates choices.

  **Counterargument**

  Dworkin's normative objections are the strongest case against the statement's second limb. First, judicial law-making is undemocratic: judges are not elected, and a society that admits they legislate hands legislative power to officials who are not accountable in the way legislators are. Secondly, it is unjust: if the judge makes new law and applies it to the parties before the court, the losing party is held to a standard that did not exist when they acted. Litigants consider themselves entitled to have the existing law applied to their disputes, not to have new law made for them. On this view, even if judges do sometimes make law, admitting it as a normal feature of adjudication is wrong, because it licenses exactly the practice that should be minimised.

  **Evaluation**

  Hart's replies meet these objections, but only because of the constraints he imposes. On democracy: a limited judicial power to settle unregulated cases is a necessary and small price, and delegated legislative power is familiar in modern democracies. On retrospectivity: in a hard case, by definition, there is no clear established law to ground expectations, so the losing party is not deprived of a right they could have relied on. Both replies depend on the character of judicial law-making. It is interstitial, not the making of large-scale reforms or new codes; and it is not arbitrary, because judges must justify their decisions by general reasons and act as a conscientious legislator would.

  That is why "nothing wrong" overstates the position. Admitting that judges make law is right, but admission is not permission. The legitimacy of what the judge does depends on its being reasoned, interstitial and responsive to the existing materials — the very features that make Dworkin's picture attractive. There is something wrong with judicial law-making that ignores those constraints; the realist judge who cherry-picks doctrine for a result reached on other grounds would be open to Dworkin's objections in full.

  The better conclusion, then, is that Hart's description is more accurate than Dworkin's, and that his normative defence succeeds for constrained, interstitial law-making. The statement should be accepted with that qualification: in hard cases judges make law, and it is better to admit it than to dream otherwise — provided the law they make is justified by general reasons that others can test.
