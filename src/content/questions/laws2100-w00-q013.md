  In the Postscript to The Concept of Law Hart says that his aim was "to provide a theory of what law is which is both general and descriptive" (CL 239): general in that it is not tied to any particular legal system or legal culture, descriptive in that it is morally neutral and has no justificatory aims. His theory gives explanatory priority to the internal point of view; yet on his account a legal system exists if officials accept its secondary rules and citizens generally comply with its primary rules, perhaps only out of fear.

  Outsider jurisprudence looks at law from the standpoint of those it marginalises. Feminist legal theorists argue that the law is not impartial and objective, and that even when it appears impartial it upholds patriarchal institutions. Critical race theorists argue that racism can coexist with formal "objectivity" and "neutrality".   **Thesis**

  The statement runs together two claims. The first — that a general and descriptive theory describes law from the inside — is largely right about Hart: his central case is the law of the modern state, and the "inside" he privileges is the standpoint of officials who accept the rules. Outsider jurisprudence shows that this choice of standpoint is not neutral, because it decides whose experience of law counts as central. But the conclusion does not follow. What outsider jurisprudence shows is that selecting what is important about law cannot be value-free; it does not show that a theory must take sides on which laws are valid, or that description is impossible. Its strongest version, Naffine's epistemology of largess, is a programme for a better and more complete description, not for abandoning description.

  **Argument**

  *Two criteria, two questions.* Hart's aim was a theory "both general and descriptive" (CL 239): general, because not tied to any legal system or culture; descriptive, because morally neutral and without justificatory aims. The criteria must be tested separately, since a theory can pass one and fail the other. The lecture adds a second distinction. Outsider jurisprudence bears on the question "what is law?" and on the question "what is legal theory?", and the two should not be confused. A claim that the law of a particular state subordinates women is about law; a claim that analytical jurisprudence has treated certain questions as canonical is about legal theory.

  *Hart's inside.* Hart gives explanatory priority to the internal point of view, "a critical reflective attitude to certain patterns of behaviour as a common standard" (CL 57). Yet his minimal conditions for a legal system require only that officials accept the secondary rules and that citizens generally comply with the primary rules — perhaps only out of fear. Many people are subjects of the law, even its victims, and some suffer more than others in particular areas such as family or employment law. Hart does not deny this, but he takes his central case from the participant who accepts the system, and treats the internal point of view itself as an amalgam of very different attitudes (CL 203). Outsider jurisprudence asks why the experience of those who merely comply, or who are oppressed, is treated as peripheral. This is not the trivial outsider, the visitor to a foreign legal system; it is the person who lives under the law without having made it.

  *Whose community?* Lacey's chapter sharpens the point about generality. Community is "one particular discursive figure in contemporary Anglo-American legal theory" (128), and every theory studied in the unit assumes one: Austin's sovereign is identified within an independent political society; Hart's system has the officials and citizens of a modern state; Finnis's central case promotes the common good; Dworkin's integrity rests on a community's political morality. A theory that claims not to be tied to any legal system still presupposes a picture of the community whose law it is. Critical race theory pushes the point further. Race is a socially and legally constructed category, and racism can coexist with formal "objectivity" and "neutrality". Moreton-Robinson's critique of white Australian feminism shows that even a critical standpoint can generalise from a dominant position: white feminists' demands left out Indigenous women's claims to maternity allowances, voting rights and labour rights. Generality can hide the standpoint it is taken from.

  *Neutral description.* The feminist challenge to conventional legal scholarship targets law's neutrality and objectivity, its autonomy and discreteness, its unity and coherence, and its rationality — features analytical jurisprudence often takes as given. Naffine explains why outsiders see them differently: the feminist legal theorist is the 'outsider within', "neither stranger nor native" (77), with an "epistemic privilege of marginality", a particular perspective on the deficiencies of law. This joins an argument from week 4. Finnis holds that "no theorist can give a theoretical description and analysis of social facts without also participating in the work of evaluation" (NLNR 3): deciding which case is central, and from what viewpoint, requires a judgment of importance. Outsider jurisprudence gives that abstract argument a concrete answer: the judgment has usually been made from the standpoint of those the law serves. Kelsen's pure theory, which answers "what the law is, not what it ought to be", is the clearest contrast; it keeps law free of other disciplines, which is exactly what Naffine's generous methodology refuses to do.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest objection is that the statement confuses describing a partial practice with giving a partial description. If law in fact upholds patriarchal institutions or racial hierarchies, an accurate theory will say so, and Hart's framework has room for it: a system can exist where most citizens comply out of fear, and Hart, unlike Kelsen, can accept genuine conflict between valid law and morality (CL 293). Indeed, outsider jurisprudence relies on description. Its central claims — that law reflects, maintains and legitimises the patriarchy, three verbs that each need their own arguments — are claims about what law is and does, and they could be true or false. If no theory of law could be value-free, those claims would be no more than expressions of commitment. The lecture's own test cuts the same way: a theory that aims to change the status quo must also be assessed by whether it brought about change, and that is a descriptive question.

  **Evaluation**

  The objection defeats the statement's conclusion, but not its premise. "Value-free" has two senses. In the first, a theory is value-free if it has no justificatory aims and makes legal validity independent of moral merit. A general theory can be value-free in that sense, and the outsider critique does not show otherwise: the complaint that a facially neutral rule entrenches a hierarchy assumes that the rule is valid law. In the second sense, a theory is value-free if its choice of central cases, viewpoints and important features involves no evaluation. Here Finnis is right, and outsider jurisprudence shows something Finnis did not: whose evaluation it was. Hart changed the problem when he set the internal point of view against Austin's external one, and the puzzled man against Holmes's bad man; outsider jurisprudence changes it again by asking about the person who is subject to the system without accepting it.

  Dworkin, no critical theorist, argued that legal theory must be both descriptive and normative, because one cannot be done without the other. Outsider jurisprudence reaches a similar conclusion from the other direction, and supplies its own standard of good theorising: Naffine's largess, "a willingness to concede that new points of view might be better than well-used ones" (81). That is a methodological virtue, not a political programme. So the better conclusion is that a general, descriptive theory cannot be value-free in its selection of what matters, and outsider jurisprudence reveals whose values were at work. The remedy is a more complete description, one that includes the outsider within, not the abandonment of description.
