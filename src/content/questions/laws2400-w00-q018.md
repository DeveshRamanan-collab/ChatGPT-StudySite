  rule: |
    Landlords once owed no duty for defective premises (Cavalier v Pope), but the High Court rejected that rule in Northern Sandblasting Pty Ltd v Harris (1997) 188 CLR 313. In Jones v Bartlett (2000) 205 CLR 166 Gummow and Hayne JJ said the landlord's duty requires it not to let premises with defects it knows or ought to know make them unsafe for their use, and is discharged by taking reasonable steps to ascertain such defects and reasonable steps to remove them or make the premises safe; landlords also owe other entrants a duty, narrower than an occupier's. An occupier — whoever controls the land — owes lawful entrants a duty whose extent depends on the relationship, the purpose of entry and the type of premises. Where two tortfeasors are liable for the same damage, the plaintiff may recover the whole from either, and contribution is a matter between them (Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1946 (NSW) s 5).

    Parents may owe others a duty to control their child. In Smith v Leurs (1945) 70 CLR 256 a duty was owed when a 13-year-old's sling shot stone injured the plaintiff's eye, but it was not breached, because the parents had told their son not to use the sling shot outside their home.

    For trespassers: "A person trespasses at their own risk and the occupier is under no duty to him except to refrain from intentional or wanton harm" (Commissioner for Railways v Cardy (1960) 104 CLR 274). The position remains unresolved, but the trend places more responsibility on the trespasser.

    Breach is decided under CLA s 5B: a foreseeable, not insignificant risk, and precautions a reasonable person would have taken, considering probability, seriousness, burden and social utility (s 5B(2)). Contributory negligence is judged by the standard of a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position (s 5R).
model: |
  **Issue**

  Three claims arise. First, whether Sam can recover from Bianca as landlord, and from the Okafors as occupiers, for his fall from the balcony. Second, whether Mr Patel can recover from the Okafors as parents for Chidi's sling shot. Third, whether Kyle, a trespasser, can recover from the Okafors for his broken ankle.

  **Rule**

  The old rule that landlords owe no duty for defective premises (Cavalier v Pope) was rejected in Northern Sandblasting Pty Ltd v Harris (1997) 188 CLR 313. Under Jones v Bartlett (2000) 205 CLR 166 a landlord must not let premises with defects it knows or ought to know make them unsafe, and discharges the duty by reasonable steps to ascertain and remove such defects or make the premises safe; a narrower duty is owed to entrants other than the tenant. Occupiers owe lawful entrants a duty of reasonable care. Parents may owe others a duty to control their child (Smith v Leurs (1945) 70 CLR 256). An occupier owes a trespasser no duty except to refrain from intentional or wanton harm (Commissioner for Railways v Cardy (1960) 104 CLR 274), though the position is unresolved. Breach is governed by CLA s 5B, contributory negligence by s 5R, and contribution between tortfeasors by the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1946 (NSW) s 5.

  **Application**

  *Sam v Bianca — duty.* Bianca cannot rely on the old landlord's immunity in Cavalier v Pope; Northern Sandblasting rejected it. Sam is not the tenant, but Jones v Bartlett confirms a landlord also owes other entrants a duty, even if narrower than an occupier's. Whatever its outer limits, the core of that duty — not leaving premises in a condition the landlord knows makes them unsafe without taking reasonable steps to remove the defect or make the premises safe — must cover a guest leaning on a balcony balustrade, an ordinary use of a residential balcony. A duty is owed.

  *Sam v Bianca — breach.* The real argument in landlord cases is about content and reasonable steps. In Jones v Bartlett the glass door was not a dangerous defect and the landlord did not know it failed the current standard. Here Bianca had actual knowledge from the condition report and two reminders, and the defect — a first-floor balustrade that "moves when leaned on" — made the premises unsafe in the most direct way. Under s 5B(1)(a) the risk was not merely foreseeable but known; under s 5B(1)(b) it was not insignificant, because a balustrade exists to stop people falling. Under s 5B(2), the probability that someone would lean on it was high (para (a)); a three-metre fall onto paving risks grave injury (para (b)); the burden of a repair, or even a temporary fix or a locked door, was modest beside that risk (para (c)); and leaving it loose had no social utility (para (d)). A reasonable landlord would not have deferred the repair for months "until after winter". Bianca breached her duty. Causation is straightforward: had the balustrade been fixed it would not have given way (s 5D(1)(a)), and a fall is exactly the risk it posed. Sam, on his first visit, with no sign, leaned on a balustrade as anyone would; a reasonable person in his position (s 5R) was not careless.

  *Sam v the Okafors.* As tenants in control of the house, the Okafors are occupiers, and Sam was a lawful entrant invited to their party. They knew the balustrade was loose — they reported it — and warned their own family, yet left the balcony door unlocked and gave guests no warning. A sign or a locked door would have cost nothing. They are likely liable to Sam as well. Sam may recover the whole of his damages from either Bianca or the Okafors; whichever pays may seek contribution from the other under the 1946 Act, s 5, but that is a matter between them and does not reduce what Sam recovers.

  *Mr Patel v the Okafors.* Smith v Leurs establishes that parents may owe others a duty to control their child, on strikingly similar facts — a 13-year-old with a sling shot injuring an eye. The difference lies in breach. In Smith v Leurs the duty was discharged because the parents had told their son not to use the sling shot outside their home. The Okafors gave Chidi the sling shot, twice watched him fire stones towards the neighbour's yard, and did nothing but laugh. Under s 5B, the risk of a stone striking someone in that yard was foreseeable — they saw where he aimed — and not insignificant, given the repeated conduct; the likely harm, an eye injury, was serious; the burden of telling him to stop or taking the sling shot away was trivial; and firing stones over a fence has no social utility. Reasonable parents would have intervened. The Okafors answer for their own failure to take reasonable steps to control Chidi, and but for that failure the stone would not have been fired at the party (s 5D(1)(a)). Mr Patel has a strong claim.

  *Kyle v the Okafors.* Kyle was a trespasser: uninvited, climbing the side fence at night. On Cardy, the occupier's only duty is to refrain from intentional or wanton harm, and stacking pavers in one's own yard after a garden project is neither. The position is unresolved, but the trend places more responsibility on the trespasser, which cuts against Kyle. Even if a general duty of reasonable care were owed, breach is doubtful: under s 5B(2), the probability that someone would drop over the side fence in the dark onto that exact spot was low, the burden of keeping one's own yard free of every hazard for unexpected intruders is real, and a stack of pavers in a private garden is an ordinary state of affairs. Any damages would also be reduced under s 5R: a reasonable sixteen-year-old in Kyle's position would know that jumping a fence into an unlit yard risks landing on something.

  **Conclusion**

  Sam has strong claims against Bianca, who owed him a landlord's duty and failed for months to fix a balustrade she knew was dangerous, and against the Okafors as occupiers who knew of the defect but did not warn him; he may recover in full from either, leaving contribution to them. Mr Patel has a strong claim against the Okafors: a duty was owed as in Smith v Leurs, but unlike the parents there they took no step at all to control Chidi. Kyle's claim is weak: as a trespasser he was owed at most a duty to refrain from intentional or wanton harm, breach is doubtful even on a wider view, and his own conduct would reduce any damages.
