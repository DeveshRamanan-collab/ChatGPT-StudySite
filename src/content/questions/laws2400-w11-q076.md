  Scope of liability is the second element of causation: s 5D(1)(b) asks whether it is appropriate for the scope of the negligent person's liability to extend to the harm so caused. Under s 5D(4), the court is to consider, amongst other relevant things, whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed on the negligent party. That inquiry embraces policy-based considerations which the courts are required to articulate (Wallace v Kam), and it raises normative questions — whether the defendant ought to be held liable (Ruddock v Taylor). I would state both provisions and those authorities first.

  Scope of liability then raises two distinct common law concepts, and I would run only the one the facts raise. If a later event affected the situation the defendant created, I would ask whether it was a new intervening act breaking the chain of causation. If the harm was of an unexpected kind, extent or manner, I would ask whether it was too remote: was the kind of damage reasonably foreseeable (The Wagon Mound (No 1)), remembering that extent and manner need not be? Either branch can take the harm outside the scope of liability. I would finish with a conclusion on whether the harm falls within the scope of the defendant's liability.
