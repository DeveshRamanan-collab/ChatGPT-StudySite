  The charge has force, but it confuses where a theory starts with what it claims. Austin says he will present the
  principles of jurisprudence "abstracted from every particular system", while illustrating them from the two systems
  he had studied with accuracy, Roman law and the law of England. His core definitions are built to travel: a society
  is political iff the generality of its members is in a habit of obedience to a determinate and common superior, and
  independent iff that superior obeys no one else. The definition needs no territory or modern state, so it fits a
  city state, an absolute monarchy, a council of elders or a constitutional democracy. The strongest case for the
  charge is that all our knowledge of law is of particular systems, and some say we can only speak meaningfully of the
  here and now; Austin's own time in Germany coloured his theory. But a theory must start somewhere; the test is whether
  its concepts fit other systems. My conclusion: Austin's generality is real in form but untested in content, and Hart's
  later objections are where it fails.
