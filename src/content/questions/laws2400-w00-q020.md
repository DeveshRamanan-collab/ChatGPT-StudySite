prompt: |
  Marlee runs a barramundi farm on Kestrel Creek in northern NSW. Her fish live in ponds filled from the creek. Two businesses discharge wastewater into the creek upstream of her farm.

  Ridgeway Tannery Pty Ltd treats its wastewater through a filtration unit before discharging it. Its maintenance log shows that the unit had been faulty for three weeks, letting through untreated, nutrient-rich water; the repair had been "deferred to next month" to save money. Hollis Dairy runs its washdown water into a holding pond. One night a worker left a hose running, and the pond overflowed into the creek for several hours.

  That same night, heavy summer rain washed fertiliser run-off from farms throughout the catchment into the creek. By the next morning the creek's oxygen levels had collapsed, and eight tonnes of Marlee's fish were dead.

  Marlee's expert says the fish died because the combined nutrient load stripped the water of oxygen. Ridgeway's discharge, Hollis's overflow and the rain run-off each added to that load, but on the present state of scientific knowledge it is impossible to say how much each contributed, or whether the fish would have died without either business's discharge. Nobody suggests that the farms' run-off involved any negligence.

  Marlee's ponds have electric aerators, which she normally runs overnight in summer. That night she switched them off to save power while she was away at a wedding, and arranged for no one to check the ponds. Her expert says that running the aerators "would probably have saved some fish, perhaps up to a third".

  As well as the value of the fish, Marlee lost a supply contract with a restaurant group because she could not deliver for three months, losing $60,000 in profit.

  Advise Marlee on her claims in negligence against Ridgeway and Hollis.
model: |
  **Issue**

  Marlee's claims raise five questions: (1) whether Ridgeway and Hollis each breached a duty of care; (2) whether either breach can be shown to be a necessary condition of the fish deaths and, if not, whether factual causation can be established under the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") s 5D(2); (3) whether her losses, including the lost contract, fall within the scope of liability; (4) whether her own conduct reduces her damages; and (5) how she recovers where two defendants are liable.

  **Rule**

  Under s 5B, a person is negligent only if the risk was foreseeable (not far-fetched or fanciful: Wyong Shire Council v Shirt), not insignificant, and a reasonable person in their position would have taken precautions, considering probability, seriousness, burden and social utility (s 5B(2)). Under s 5D(1), causation requires (a) that the negligence was a necessary condition of the harm, and (b) that it is appropriate for liability to extend to the harm. Section 5D(2) provides that "in an exceptional case, in accordance with established principles", where negligence cannot be established as a necessary condition, the court is to consider "whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed on the negligent party". The textbook flowchart describes two kinds of case: multiple sufficient causes — "the cumulative operation of factors" where "the contribution of each factor to that harm is unascertainable" (Bonnington Castings v Wardlaw; March v Stramare); and material contribution — negligent conduct that "materially increases the risk of harm" where "the state of scientific or medical knowledge makes it impossible to prove the cause" (Fairchild v Glenhaven Funeral Services Ltd; McGee v National Coal Board; Amaca Pty Ltd v Ellis; Henville v Walker). There must also be a clear breach and an evidentiary gap.

  Scope of liability asks whether the kind of harm was reasonably foreseeable (The Wagon Mound (No 1)) — a real risk (The Wagon Mound (No 2)) — though its extent need not be; a causally independent and extremely unlikely coincidence can break the chain (Haber v Walker). Contributory negligence reduces damages to what is just and equitable (Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1965 (NSW) s 9(1)), on the standard in s 5R, with a causal requirement (Caswell v Powell Duffryn Associated Collieries) and a comparison of the parties' whole conduct (Pennington v Norris). Concurrent tortfeasors are jointly and severally liable, with contribution between them (Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1946 (NSW) s 5).

  **Application**

  *Breach.* Ridgeway knew for three weeks that untreated, nutrient-rich water was entering a creek others used. Harm to downstream users was foreseeable — not far-fetched or fanciful — and not insignificant (s 5B(1)(a)–(b)). Under s 5B(2), the probability of harm rose each day the fault continued; the likely harm to a fish farm was serious; the burden of repairing the unit, or stopping discharge until it was fixed, was a cost Ridgeway chose to defer; and the tannery's social utility does not justify discharging untreated waste. Hollis's breach is simpler: leaving a hose running until a waste pond overflowed is a foreseeable, real risk that one turn of a tap would have avoided. Both breaches are clear.

  *Factual causation — necessary condition.* Under s 5D(1)(a), Marlee must show each defendant's negligence was a necessary condition of the fish deaths. Her own expert cannot say whether the fish would have died without either discharge. Necessary condition cannot be established against either defendant.

  *Factual causation — s 5D(2).* This is the kind of case the flowchart identifies. The deaths resulted from the cumulative operation of three factors whose individual contributions are unascertainable — the description of multiple sufficient causes (Bonnington Castings; March v Stramare). The material contribution description also fits: each negligent discharge materially increased the risk of oxygen collapse, and the state of scientific knowledge makes the cause impossible to prove (Fairchild; McGee; Amaca v Ellis; Henville v Walker). The further requirements are met: each breach is clear, and the evidentiary gap is scientific, not the result of Marlee failing to gather evidence. There are strong reasons to impose responsibility: each defendant negligently added to the very risk that materialised, in a creek others depended on. Against that, the defendants will say the non-negligent run-off may have been enough on its own, so the loss should be left to lie where it fell — a consideration the flowchart adds, though the NSW text does not mention it. Weighing those considerations, Marlee has a strong argument that this is an exceptional case in which factual causation should be accepted against both defendants.

  *Scope of liability.* Fish deaths from deoxygenated water are the very kind of harm that discharging nutrient-rich waste into a fish-bearing creek risks — a real risk that would not be brushed aside (The Wagon Mound (No 2)); the extent of the kill need not be foreseeable. The rain does not break the chain: it is not human conduct at all, and heavy summer rain in a farming catchment is an ordinary event, not an extremely unlikely coincidence (Haber v Walker). The $60,000 lost contract is consequential economic loss flowing from damage to Marlee's property — her fish — so it is not pure economic loss and is recoverable if foreseeable; losing supply contracts is a foreseeable consequence for a commercial fish farm whose stock is destroyed.

  *Contributory negligence.* Under s 5R, the question is what a reasonable fish farmer in Marlee's position, knowing what she knew, would have done. She normally runs the aerators overnight in summer, which shows she knew the risk; switching them off with no one checking falls below that standard. The causal requirement (Caswell) is met only in part: her expert says the aerators would probably have saved some fish, perhaps up to a third, so her failure contributed to part of the loss. The reduction must be just and equitable, comparing the parties' whole conduct (Pennington v Norris). Ridgeway's three-week, cost-saving neglect and Hollis's careless overflow created the hazard; Marlee's fault was a one-night lapse in guarding against it. A modest reduction is appropriate.

  *Two defendants.* If both are liable for the same damage, Marlee may recover the whole from either — for example, the one better able to pay — and the one who pays may seek contribution from the other under the 1946 Act s 5(1)(c); the risk that one cannot pay lies with the tortfeasors, not Marlee. The textbook flags that proportionate liability provisions may apply to claims for property damage and economic loss, which this is; those provisions are not in the materials, but if they apply they may limit each defendant to its share, and Marlee should sue both.

  **Conclusion**

  Ridgeway and Hollis each clearly breached their duty. Neither breach is shown to be a necessary condition of the fish kill, but the case fits the s 5D(2) categories, with a clear breach and a scientific evidentiary gap, and Marlee has strong arguments for imposing responsibility. Her fish and the consequential $60,000 contract loss are within scope; the rain does not break the chain. Her damages will be modestly reduced for switching off the aerators. She should sue both defendants, subject to any proportionate liability provisions.
