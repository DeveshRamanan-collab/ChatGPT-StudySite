  Explain the basis and nature of state immunity, including the distinction between immunity from adjudication and
  immunity from enforcement, the legal source of the rules, and how a state can lose its immunity.
model: |
  State immunity is grounded in sovereign equality: states are juridically equal, so no state may exercise
  jurisdiction over another without consent, and the courts of one state may decline jurisdiction where the
  respondent is another state. It limits adjudicative and enforcement jurisdiction, not prescriptive jurisdiction.
  Immunity from adjudication means a state cannot be sued before a foreign court unless it consents; immunity from
  enforcement means a judgment, once rendered, cannot be enforced upon the state, e.g. by confiscating its assets.

  Immunity is a procedural barrier, so the court rules on it first; it says nothing about whether the conduct was
  lawful. Its source is customary international law. The UN Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and
  their Property (2004) has 25 of the 30 ratifications it needs and is not in force, but its articles mostly reflect
  custom: art 5 states the principle and art 6 the modalities. A state loses immunity by express consent (art 7),
  by participating in proceedings (art 8) and for counterclaims (art 9), and once waived it cannot reclaim immunity
  when judgment goes against it. The UK State Immunity Act 1978 and Australia's Foreign States Immunities Act 1985
  apply the same approach domestically.
