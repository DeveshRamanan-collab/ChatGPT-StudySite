  Using Hutchins v Maughan, explain what "directness" means in trespass and why the claim in that case failed.

model: |
  Directness is the element that separates trespass, which requires a direct act or interference, from an
  action on the case, which covers indirect or consequential damage. In Hutchins v Maughan, the defendant laid
  poisonous baits on unfenced land, and the complainant's dogs found and ate them some days later, dying as a
  result. Herring CJ held that trespass did not lie, because the baits were laid before the complainant brought
  his dogs onto the land: the complainant himself had to intervene, by bringing his dogs to the land, before the
  injurious consequences could occur. The injury therefore followed the act of laying the baits only
  consequentially, not immediately, and so was properly regarded as a matter for an action on the case, not
  trespass. The court traced this to the historical principle in Reynolds v Clarke and the log-in-the-highway
  example from Scott v Shepherd and Leame v Bray: if a thrown log hits someone as it lands, that is trespass;
  if someone later trips over it lying in the road, that is merely case. Directness is often "an issue of time"
  — had the defendant thrown the baits directly at the dogs, the injury would have been immediate, and trespass
  would have been made out.

  Williams v Milotin shows this distinction still matters practically today, not just historically. A boy hit
  directly by a truck sued in negligence, an action on the case, rather than trespass to the person, because
  negligence carried a six-year limitation period against trespass's three years. The High Court held the same
  facts could found either cause of action, each with different elements, and that the plaintiff was entitled
  to choose which to plead — so the trespass/case classification directly decided which limitation period, and
  ultimately whether the claim succeeded, applied.
