  A café owner, angry after a dispute with a former employee, falsely tells police the employee stole cash from the till, despite having no real evidence and no honest belief the accusation is true. model: |
  Two separate actions on the case arise. First, malicious prosecution requires that the defendant initiated proceedings, they terminated in the plaintiff's favour, the defendant acted maliciously, and acted without reasonable and probable cause; damage must also be shown because the tort is not actionable per se. The owner initiated the theft complaint, the charges were withdrawn (favourable termination), the owner acted maliciously (spite, no honest belief), and there was an absence of reasonable and probable cause (no real evidence) — following A v New South Wales, both malice and absence of reasonable and probable cause must be separately established, and both appear present here, alongside reputational damage. Second, the spiteful rumour about the employee's partner mirrors Wilkinson v Downton: a wilful act (spreading the false, shocking rumour) calculated to cause harm, which did cause psychiatric shock. Even though the owner may not have specifically intended psychiatric injury, Nationwide News v Naidu confirms reckless indifference to the result suffices. The employee therefore has strong grounds for both malicious prosecution and intentional infliction of personal injury.
