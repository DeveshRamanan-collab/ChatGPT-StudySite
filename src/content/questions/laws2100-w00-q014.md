  Rights are among the most familiar concepts in law: we use the word to articulate claims and entitlements that we feel or know are enforceable in a court. Yet the word is used in many ways. Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld identified four different legal positions within the concept of a "right". X has a claim-right against Y with respect to φ if and only if Y is under a duty to X to φ; X has a privilege to φ if and only if X has no duty not to φ; X has a power over Y if and only if X has the ability to alter, through volitional action, Y's normative position; and X holds an immunity against Y if and only if Y lacks the ability to alter X's Hohfeldian incidents.

  Two families of theories answer what looks like a different question: what makes a position a right? On the will theory, a person has a right if they have control or choice over whether others must or must not act in certain ways; rights are tools for exercising free will. On the interest theory, a person has a right if it serves to protect an important interest; rights are justified by the role they play in safeguarding well-being. Critics of the will theory ask about those, such as infants, who cannot exercise choice. Human rights add further features: they are said to be plural, universal and high-priority, and they include group rights such as the right to self-determination.

  **Thesis**

  The statement is wrong, though it is right about something. Hohfeld's analysis shows that "right" is not one thing: a person who says "I have a right" may hold a claim, a privilege, a power or an immunity, and an argument about rights should begin by saying which. In that sense Hohfeld disciplines the debate. But the will and interest theories are not quarrelling about a label. Hohfeld's definitions presuppose an answer to the question they dispute. X has a claim-right when Y is under "a duty to X": what makes the duty owed to X, rather than merely about X, is exactly what the two theories answer differently. Hohfeld settles the form of a right; the will and interest theories dispute its point.

  **Argument**

  *Four positions, two pairs.* Hohfeld's positions fall into two pairs. Claims and privileges are defined by duties about φ: a claim is someone else's duty owed to me; a privilege is the absence of my own duty not to act. Powers and immunities are defined by the ability to alter normative positions: a power is my ability to change yours; an immunity is your inability to change mine. The structure has a history in the unit. Austin made command and duty correlative — wherever a duty lies, a command has been signified — and had little room for anything else. Hart's first group of objections in Chapter III was that the law also confers powers: to make a contract, write a valid will or marry, where there is no duty to do anything and the rule sets out a procedure to follow. Hohfeld's power is the position those power-conferring rules create. Hart's account of social rules also explains why rights are normative at all: "Habits are not 'normative': they cannot confer rights or authority on anyone" (CL 60).

  *Two theories, a different question.* On the will theory, a person has a right if they have control or choice over whether others must or must not act in certain ways; rights are tools for exercising autonomy. On the interest theory, a person has a right if it protects an important interest; rights are justified by their role in safeguarding well-being. These are not rival lists of positions. Each is an account of what makes a normative position a right, and so of who holds it.

  *A contract right.* If you and I sign a contract, I acquire a claim that you perform, and you are under a duty to me. I usually also hold powers: to enforce the duty, or to release you from it. Both theories accept this translation, but they explain it differently. For the will theorist, my right consists in my control over your duty; the power to waive or enforce is the mark of the right. For the interest theorist, my right exists because your duty protects my interest in performance; the power to release you is a further position I happen to hold. The Hohfeldian description is the same on both accounts. The explanation is not.

  *A human right.* Freedom from slavery is, in Hohfeld's terms, a claim against everyone that they not enslave me, and arguably an immunity: no one has the power to alter my normative position into that of a slave. Many now see it as an absolute right, and art 15(2) of the European Convention allows no derogation from art 4(1), "No one shall be held in slavery or servitude", even in a public emergency. If the right is absolute, it seems its holder cannot waive it either. Then the holder lacks the very control the will theory treats as the mark of a right, yet this is the paradigm human right. The interest theory has no such difficulty: the right protects a fundamental interest whether or not the holder can release others from the duty. Infants raise the same problem. They cannot exercise choice as adults can; if the will theory says they have no rights, it is at odds with the claim that human rights belong to every human being — "Everyone is entitled to all the rights and freedoms set forth in this Declaration" (UDHR art 2). Group rights such as self-determination raise a further question that Hohfeld's X-and-Y form does not answer: who, or what, is X?

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the statement is that the will theory can be restated entirely in Hohfeld's terms. A right, on the will theory, is a claim plus a power to waive or enforce it; on the interest theory, it is a claim whose correlative duty protects the holder's interest. Once each position is named precisely, the theorists agree on every normative fact: who owes what to whom, and who can change what. The only remaining question is which bundle deserves the word "right", and that is a question about language, not law. The lecture's warning about the inflation of rights talk supports this: if every entitlement can be cast as a right, the category loses its point, and the better course is to drop the word in favour of the four positions.

  **Evaluation**

  The restatement shows that the will theory can be expressed in Hohfeld's vocabulary, which is a real gain in precision. It does not show that the dispute is verbal. To count only claim-plus-power as a right is to take a substantive view about what rights are for — protecting autonomy rather than welfare — and that view has consequences that are not linguistic. It decides whether infants hold rights or are only the beneficiaries of other people's duties; whether an unwaivable human right is a right at all; and whether a group can hold one. Those questions matter because rights carry priority. Human rights are said to rank above the general interest, so deciding who holds a right decides whose interests receive that priority.

  There is a further point. Hohfeld's claim-right is defined by a duty owed "to X". A duty to feed a child is owed to the child on the interest theory, because it protects the child's interest; on the will theory, if the child cannot control the duty, whatever right exists belongs to whoever can. Hohfeld cannot decide between these answers, because his analysis begins once the relation between X and Y has been fixed. That is why the four positions and the two theories are complementary rather than competing: Hohfeld tells us what a rights claim says; the will and interest theories tell us why some such claims are rights, and whose.

  The statement should therefore be rejected. Translating a rights claim into Hohfeld's terms is the right first step — zoom in on which position is at stake, and against whom — but the dispute between the will and interest theories survives the translation, because it is a dispute about the point of rights, and that point is what gives the vocabulary its use.
