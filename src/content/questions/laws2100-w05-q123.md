model: "It is better read as a theory with particular roots that claims generality it struggles to earn. Dworkin builds his theory on constructive interpretation: pre-interpretive examples, the point of the practice, then post-interpretive revision. For him, the point of the legal system is to justify the exercise of state coercion — a liberal answer — and integrity, consistency in principle, gives the system its unity, as tested on the dimensions of fit and justification by Judge Hercules. That picture may work for a healthy liberal constitutional democracy. The counterargument is the evil legal system: what principles give unity to a racist regime such as apartheid South Africa? Some argue that the theory made sense for the United States rather than as a general explanation of law — a question of general versus particular jurisprudence. Finnis adds that fit and justification are incommensurable, weakening the one-right-answer thesis. On balance integrity explains an argumentative, rights-protecting practice well, but its generality depends on finding a point for law that evil systems share."
