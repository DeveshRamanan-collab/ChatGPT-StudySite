model: "Largely yes, if the distinction holds in practice. Dworkin says the difference is \"a logical distinction\": rules apply in an all-or-nothing fashion, while principles state a reason that argues in one direction and have the dimension of weight. Two conflicting rules cannot both apply; conflicting principles are balanced, and the outweighed principle remains law. A principle is observed because it is a requirement of justice or fairness, unlike a policy, which sets a goal. Because principles cannot be identified by pedigree, a master rule of recognition cannot capture them, and the rule of recognition cannot simply be modified to embrace them. The counterargument is Dworkin's own concession that it is not always clear from the form of a standard whether it is a rule or a principle; the positivist can also say principles like those in Riggs are extra-legal, as Raz does. On balance the distinction works only if we accept, from how judges reason, that principles are legal — so the argument turns on the internal point of view of lawyers and judges."
