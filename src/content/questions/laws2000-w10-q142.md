prompt: Distinguish UN peacekeeping operations from Security Council authorisations to use force, and explain how peacekeeping has evolved.
model: |-
  In the absence of a standing UN army (art 43 has never been implemented), the Security Council may either decide on
  a UN-run peacekeeping or peace operation, or authorise the use of force.

  An authorisation empowers one state, a coalition of states or a regional organisation (art 53) to use force — for
  example Res 678 (1990), authorising members co-operating with Kuwait to use "all necessary means". It is an exception to
  art 2(4) under art 42, decided as a last resort where art 41 measures would be or have proved inadequate.

  Peacekeeping operations are not explicitly mentioned in the Charter. They were primarily created to keep the peace,
  not to enforce it, and, unlike authorisations, they are UN operations. Their traditional principles are consent of
  the parties, impartiality, and non-use of force except in self-defence.

  Peacekeeping has gradually evolved: the UN has acted as a transitional administration (Kosovo, Timor-Leste), and
  there is a changing attitude to the use of force, with some missions authorised to use force to carry out their
  mandate — in the DRC (including targeted offensive operations) and Mali. Deployments passed 100,000 by 2015, mostly in
  sub-Saharan Africa.
