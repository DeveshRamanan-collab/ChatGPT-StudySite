  Compare how the UK, the US and Australia each determine who has authority to conclude a treaty, and explain
  the role art 7 VCLT plays in identifying who can represent a state at the negotiating table.
model: |
  International law does not itself say who can bind a state — that question is left to each state's own
  domestic law. In the UK, the Crown (government, acting under the royal prerogative) concludes treaties, but
  s 20 of the Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010 (UK) requires the treaty to be laid before
  Parliament before ratification. In the US, a formal "treaty" needs the advice and consent of at least
  two-thirds of the Senate, but the President can instead make an "executive agreement", binding under
  international law, without Senate involvement — as the Paris Agreement's repeated joining and withdrawal
  by executive order shows. In Australia, art 61 of the Constitution gives the executive the power to
  conclude treaties, exercised in practice by the Minister for Foreign Affairs, DFAT and the Attorney-
  General's Office of International Law. Article 7 VCLT then solves the separate, practical problem of who,
  at the negotiating table, can be assumed to represent a state: a head of state, head of government or
  minister for foreign affairs needs no letter of full powers, while anyone else must produce one.
