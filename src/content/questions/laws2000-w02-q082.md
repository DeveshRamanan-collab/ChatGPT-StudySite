model: |
  Customary international law generally binds every state once its elements — general practice and opinio
  juris — are established. This week's material recognises two exceptions to that universal reach.

  The first is the persistent objector rule: a state that has persistently and consistently objected to a
  rule from the very beginning of its formation is not bound by it. In Fisheries (United Kingdom v Norway),
  the ICJ found the UK's claimed 10-mile rule for closing bays had not, on the evidence, achieved the status
  of customary international law at all. But the Court added, in the alternative, that even had the rule
  existed, Norway had consistently and from the outset rejected any attempt to apply it to the Norwegian
  coast, always calculating its territorial sea by straight baselines instead — so Norway would not have been
  bound regardless. This shows the objection must be maintained from the beginning of the rule's development;
  a state cannot comply for years and then, once the rule becomes inconvenient, retroactively claim it always
  objected.

  The second exception is particular customary international law: a customary rule need not be universal, and
  can instead bind only a limited group of states, including as few as two. In Right of Passage over Indian
  Territory (Portugal v India), the ICJ accepted that, even in the absence of any treaty, Portugal's
  centuries-long, consistent practice of accessing its enclave through Indian territory had crystallised into
  a customary right of passage binding as between Portugal and India specifically — a rule that need never
  apply to any other state.

  Both exceptions ultimately serve the same underlying idea that international law is based on consent: the
  persistent objector rule lets a state that never accepted a rule opt out of it, while particular custom
  confines a rule's binding force to the states whose own practice and acceptance actually produced it.
