  Explain when a reservation to a multilateral treaty is permissible under art 19 VCLT, what happens once a
  permissible reservation is made (art 20), and what the unresolved problem is with an impermissible one.
model: |
  Article 19 permits a reservation unless the treaty prohibits reservations altogether, prohibits that
  specific reservation, or the reservation is incompatible with the treaty's object and purpose — the
  lecture's example being a state trying to reserve against its core emissions-reduction duty in an
  ozone-depleting-substances treaty, which would conflict with the treaty's very objective. Once a reservation
  passes art 19, art 20(4) governs its effect: another state may accept it (both become parties on the
  modified terms) or object to it (the treaty then does not enter into force between the reserving and
  objecting states); the reservation only takes effect once at least one other state has accepted it. Special
  categories exist too — art 20(2) requires unanimous acceptance where the treaty's limited membership and
  object and purpose show it must apply in its totality, and art 20(3) requires the acceptance of an
  international organisation's own competent organ for a reservation to its constituent instrument. What
  happens to an impermissible reservation is genuinely unresolved: the reserving state might not become a
  party at all; the reservation might be severed, leaving the state fully bound including the reserved
  provision (in tension with the principle that consent defines a state's obligations); or the state might be
  presumed a full party without the reservation's benefit, unless it expressly conditioned its consent on
  the reservation being accepted. Either way, an impermissible reservation is void from the outset, regardless
  of whether any other state formally objects.
