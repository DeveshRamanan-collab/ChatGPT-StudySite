  Maria is standing in a queue when a stranger, Tom, taps her firmly on the shoulder to ask if she has dropped her wallet. Maria is startled and later complains that Tom battered her. Advise whether Maria has an action in battery against Tom, applying the elements of battery and the principle in Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd.
model: |
  Battery requires a voluntary and positive act, direct interference, intentional or negligent contact, physical interference with the plaintiff's body, and that the touching was unlawful. Tom's tap is a positive, direct, intentional act causing physical contact with Maria's body, so the first four elements are satisfied. The live issue is whether the touching was unlawful. Following Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd, the absence of anger or hostility does not by itself make a touching lawful or unlawful; the test is whether the contact exceeds what is generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of daily life. Tapping a stranger's shoulder to get their attention, using no more force than necessary, is a recognised example of contact accepted in everyday life (the exigencies-of-everyday-life exception), just as it was in Rixon itself. Maria being startled does not change this objective assessment. On these facts, Tom's touching falls within acceptable everyday contact, so Maria has no action in battery against him.
