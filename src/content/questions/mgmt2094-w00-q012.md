  **Thesis**

  The statement is right that creativity has become a scarcer and more valuable advantage, and wrong to set it against expertise. Almost every account of creativity in the unit depends on domain knowledge: Wallas' Preparation, Markman's collective memory, the first stage of the creativity gym, and the prepared mind behind every "accident". If AI makes technical knowledge cheap, the advantage shifts to people who can combine knowledge with the flip — asking new questions, connecting distant fields and acting on counter-intuitive ideas. Creativity matters more because expertise is more widely available, not because expertise matters less.

  **Argument**

  *The flip.* The unit's aim is a flexible, counter-intuitive mindset: "flip" means repeatedly moving between different perspectives and paradigms, in real time and on demand. The lecture's reasons are that business changes very quickly and that AI can supply technical knowledge and skill to anyone, so what sets people apart is creativity and innovation, including learning to work with AI to boost them. Iger's 2005 deal putting ABC's *Lost* and *Desperate Housewives* on iTunes — the first deal of its kind, seemingly at odds with Disney's business model — is the flip in action; Tim Cook said Iger "understands the tradition of Disney but isn't wedded to it".

  *Creativity needs knowledge.* The Week 2 lecture gives three reasons creativity resists a standardised test, and one of them is that creativity implies domain knowledge. Wallas' first stage, Preparation, focuses on the problem and explores its dimensions, and Sadler-Smith stresses that it depends on domain-specific knowledge: Becquerel could not have discovered radioactivity by chance had he not already been immersed in the research. Markman argues that most creative solutions are found inside the collective memory of the people working on the problem, and that the problem statement is the cue that retrieves it. Dyson's bagless vacuum came from describing the problem as separating dirt from air, which brought industrial cyclones to mind — but only to someone who knew about industrial cyclones. The creativity gym begins with "starting off": learning the skills and understanding the industry you are entering. Mark, who has run a furniture business for more than 30 years, calls his years working in a recycled-timber furniture business his university.

  *Knowledge without creativity.* The reverse is also true, and here the statement has force. Dow Corning had analytical capabilities among the best in the world and spent 10% of revenue on R&D, yet its share of revenue from new products fell from about 25% to 13% by 1995: expertise was abundant, and conversion was the problem. de Langhe, Puntoni and Larrick show that even experts who know about nonlinearity can fail to account for it and rely on their gut. Claman warns that experienced people slide into ruts, saying "That won't work. It's been tried too many times before."

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the statement is that expertise can itself be a barrier. Shelley Simpson built a porcelain business with no training in ceramics, design or business, and her porcelain carries its pigment in the clay itself — something she learned to do because she didn't know she shouldn't. Claman notes that on your first day at work you felt entitled to look at things differently. The Week 8 lecture warns that a 20-year veteran may be stuck in old routines while an intern of two days may have great ideas. If AI supplies the facts, an outsider's fresh perspective may be worth more than an insider's knowledge.

  **Evaluation**

  These examples show the value of different perspectives, not of ignorance. Simpson described every single thing she touched as a point of growth, and as the business grew her own development had to grow with it — logistics, packaging, freight and compliance — because the business could not grow without that knowledge. The Week 8 ideation process asks for a team diverse in age, seniority, experience and customer knowledge, not one without experience. Creative association, and Darwin's use of Malthus' political economy, show that valuable knowledge often comes from another field; AI may make such knowledge easier to reach, but seeing the connection is still the creative act. And the Week 5 lecture stresses Verification: someone must judge logically whether an idea actually fits and solves the problem, which takes expertise.

  So the statement should be qualified. AI may make technical knowledge more widely available, and the flip mindset is what turns that knowledge into an advantage. But creativity works on knowledge, and the people best placed to flip are those who know the paradigm well enough to see what flipping it would mean. The advantage belongs not to creativity instead of expertise, but to expertise used creatively.
