  **Issue**

  Whether Priya's entry onto Owen's land, and her conduct once there, amount to trespass to land, and whether she can rely on the defence of necessity to excuse the entry and the resulting damage.

  **Rule**

  Trespass to land is direct interference with land in the plaintiff's possession. The elements are: a direct and positive act interfering with the land; done intentionally or negligently; while the land is in the plaintiff's possession; without the plaintiff's consent or other lawful justification. Trespass is actionable per se, so Owen need not prove damage to establish the tort, though consequential damage can still be recovered once trespass is made out. Mistake is no defence — even an honestly motivated entry is trespass unless a recognised lawful excuse applies.

  Necessity may excuse what would otherwise be a tort in three broad situations, including protecting one's own property and protecting the property of another; it requires that the action was necessary to prevent serious harm to person or property, and that the response was reasonable and proportionate to the risk. The doctrine is narrow. In Southwark London Borough Council v Williams [1971] Ch 734, homeless squatters pleaded necessity against a possession order; Lord Denning rejected it, warning that the doctrine "must be carefully circumscribed" and that admitting an ongoing, foreseeable problem like homelessness as necessity would mean "no one's house would be safe." By contrast, in Proudman v Allen [1954] SASR 336, a defendant who turned a driverless car's wheel to avoid a collision, sending it into the sea instead, successfully relied on necessity — the defence is available even to a stranger acting to protect another's property, provided the emergency is genuine and sudden and the response proportionate.

  **Application**

  *Trespass to land.* Priya deliberately broke the padlock and entered Owen's yard — a direct, positive and intentional act. The land was in Owen's possession (he is the owner, resident and had locked it in his absence), and Priya had no consent, express or implied: Owen left with nobody holding a key, and there is nothing to suggest any general licence extended to forced entry through a locked gate. The first three elements of trespass to land are clearly established. The only question is whether Priya has a lawful excuse — here, necessity.

  *Necessity — the genuineness of the emergency.* Unlike the squatters in Southwark, whose problem (homelessness) was an ongoing, foreseeable state of affairs rather than a sudden crisis, Priya was responding to a fast-moving grass fire, already smouldering on the fence, that threatened to spread to her own house within minutes. This is much closer to the sudden, genuine emergency in Proudman v Allen — a rapidly developing danger with no time to seek Owen's consent or wait for the fire brigade, rather than a slow-burning or self-inflicted problem the defendant should simply have avoided creating. On these facts, necessity is available in principle: Priya was acting to protect both Owen's property (his fence and shed) and her own adjoining property from a genuine and immediate danger, and Proudman confirms the defence is not limited to protecting one's own property but extends to a stranger acting on another's behalf.

  *Necessity — reasonableness and proportionality.* The next question is whether Priya's conduct was reasonable and proportionate to the risk she faced, examined activity by activity rather than as one indivisible act. Breaking the padlock to gain entry, connecting Owen's own hose, and dousing the smouldering fence and surrounding grass for the twenty minutes it took to bring the fire under control were all reasonably necessary steps to prevent the fire spreading to two homes — there was no less damaging way to achieve the same protective result in the time available, and the cost of a broken padlock is modest against the risk of a house fire. The cracked hose nozzle, dropped in the course of urgently fighting the blaze, is also readily explained as an incidental, proportionate consequence of the emergency response, rather than a separate or gratuitous act.

  The vegetable garden stands somewhat differently. Necessity protects conduct that is reasonably necessary to meet the emergency, not damage beyond that. If hot spots genuinely extended into or along the edge of the garden bed, trampling that specific area while extinguishing them would fall within the defence, just as the fence-dousing does. But running "back and forth across his vegetable garden bed to reach every hot spot" suggests Priya may have used the bed as a convenient thoroughfare beyond what fighting the fire strictly required, rather than confining her movement to what was necessary to reach the fire itself. Necessity is not a licence to cause any damage that occurs to be convenient while responding to a genuine emergency; it protects only the response that was actually proportionate to the danger. Without more detail on exactly where the fire threat reached, this element of the claim is more finely balanced than the entry, hose use and fence dousing, and Owen has a reasonable argument that at least some of the trampling exceeded what necessity can excuse.

  **Conclusion**

  Owen can establish all the elements of trespass to land against Priya: she directly, positively and intentionally entered land in his possession without consent. However, Priya has a strong necessity defence covering the forced entry, the hose use, the cracked nozzle and the dousing of the fence and its immediate surrounds, because she was responding to a genuine, sudden fire emergency of the kind recognised in Proudman v Allen, not the ongoing or self-created problem rejected in Southwark. That defence should defeat Owen's claim for the padlock, the gate and the hose. The vegetable garden damage is more doubtful: to the extent the trampling was reasonably incidental to reaching and extinguishing fire in or near the bed, necessity should also excuse it, but any trampling beyond what was needed to fight the fire falls outside the defence, and Owen may recover compensatory damages — assessed as the diminution in value of the destroyed produce, roughly $600 — for that excess. Because trespass is actionable per se, Owen could in any event obtain nominal damages even if necessity is found to excuse every act, but on the facts as given his stronger claim is for whatever portion of the garden damage necessity cannot justify.
