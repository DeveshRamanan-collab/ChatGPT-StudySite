model: |
  **Thesis**

  The founder is wrong on both counts. Being first is not everything: the unit's materials include successful companies that won by implementing an idea better than whoever had it first, by improving a business model, or by reacting quickly. And not every company should aim to be a creator. BCG's study of the world's most innovative companies found six distinct innovation models, and its central finding is that the right one depends on the industry's and the company's own context. A mid-sized retailer should choose the model its industry rewards and its capabilities can support — which may well not be the creator.

  **Argument**

  *Six models, not one.* BCG studied more than 100 of the world's most innovative companies and found six models. Creators ("We know best") are typically led by a strong, bold, visionary leader, disrupt their core markets, protect their intellectual property and make a limited number of highly focused big bets, with a closed innovation approach; Apple is the classic example. Solution builders ("We're listening") draw on observation and deep customer insight, as Nike does with shoe-based sensors that give personalised feedback. Leveragers ("We do it best") create a superior business model and keep improving it, as Zara does with processes that shorten the time new items take to reach stores. Expanders ("Seeking share to steal") carry core capabilities into adjacent markets, as Amazon does. Defenders ("Protecting our turf") win in mature or slow-changing industries, as Allstate did by acquiring Esurance. Fast followers ("Ready to react") respond quickly to — and often improve upon — competitors' innovations. Innovators typically fall short, BCG says, because they pursue the wrong model for their business and competitive context, or fail to support a good model with the capabilities it requires.

  *Context decides.* Only a subset of models succeeds in most industries. In consumer retail — the founder's industry — BCG found four models driving shareholder-return premiums: creators, solution builders, leveragers and expanders. Even within retail, companies succeed with different models: Amazon as an expander and Costco as a leverager, with everyday low prices, a lean supplier network and a members-only approach. BCG's questions for choosing a model — is innovation a growth engine or a defensive tool, how strong and durable is the competitive position, how important is brand, how robust are the innovation capabilities, how much can the company invest, and how quickly does the sector change — have different answers for every firm.

  *First is not everything.* Uber, the Week 8 lecture's example of value innovation, was not necessarily the first to think of the idea; it was the most successful implementation. Reckitt Benckiser, BCG's best-in-class fast follower, deliberately spends minimal energy up front on consumer insight and ideation, investing instead in product testing, and its flat structure maximises speed to market. Mark built a furniture business over more than 30 years by "sticking to your knitting" — making the core business successful before branching out — and by making timeless pieces rather than constant new ranges.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the founder comes from Disney. Iger made big and early bets, some seemingly at odds with the business model: ABC shows on iTunes in 2005, the first deal of its kind, and free full-length TV shows online in 2006. With MagicBands, while many corporations were still waiting to see what the "killer app" for wearables was, Disney invented one. BCG itself warns that the defender model becomes increasingly risky as technology transforms more industries, and Sears, a defender counting on its brand and its stores, lost its edge when agile online players upended retail. On this view, a company that is not leading is waiting to be overtaken.

  **Evaluation**

  Disney's lesson is about the flip mindset — Tim Cook said Iger "understands the tradition of Disney but isn't wedded to it" — more than about being first in every market. Being early also has costs: Disney's interactive division lost more than $1 billion over five years, and Iger's comment was that "we learned from that". Sears shows the danger of the wrong model for an industry, not proof that only creators survive; BCG's other retail successes are solution builders, leveragers and expanders. The creator model also demands what a mid-sized retailer may lack: a visionary leader, protected intellectual property and the capacity to make a few large bets. BCG's advice is to look for a model competitors are not using, or are using poorly, and then align structure and culture, tools and processes, and metrics and incentives behind it. Collis makes a similar point about focus: the single best piece of advice for any company builder is to know what not to do.

  So the statement should be rejected. Innovation may well distinguish leaders from followers, but leadership can be won by understanding customers, perfecting a business model or reacting fast and well, not only by being first. The founder's first question should not be "how do we become a creator?" but "which model fits our industry, our strengths and our ambition?"
