  **Issue**

  Three questions arise. First, whether Tomas can recover in negligence from Ridge Line Trails for his collarbone injury. Second, whether Elena can recover from Yusuf for the shoulder-barge, and on what basis. Third, whether Ridge Line Trails might itself be liable for Yusuf's conduct.

  **Rule**

  Duty in sport may be owed to fellow participants, but participants accept the risks inherent in their sport: "participants may be held to have accepted risks which are inherent in that sport or pastime … but this does not eliminate all duty of care of the one participant to the other" (Barwick CJ, Rootes v Shelton (1967) 116 CLR 383) — whether a duty arises, and its scope, depends on the circumstances. Agar v Hyde (2000) 201 CLR 552 shows the same reasoning can defeat a claim: injured rugby players argued the sport's governing body owed a duty to change the rules to cut "unnecessary risk," but the High Court found none, since the sport was "based on activities … which, by the standards of most members of the community, are obviously dangerous" — the answer is fact-specific to the activity and the risk in question.

  The Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") gives dangerous recreational activities their own regime. Section 5L removes liability for harm resulting from the materialisation of an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity engaged in by the plaintiff, whether or not the plaintiff was actually aware of the risk. Section 5M separately removes the duty of care over a recreational risk once it has been the subject of a risk warning — spoken or written, general or specific — regardless of whether the plaintiff received, understood, or was capable of understanding it.

  Separately, battery is a direct and intentional act causing physical contact without consent or other lawful justification; any touching without lawful excuse is unlawful, however slight (Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd (2001) 53 NSWLR 98). Consent to play a contact or physical sport extends to forcible contact ordinarily incidental to the game as played, but not to a deliberately inflicted injury outside it (McNamara v Duncan (1971) 26 ALR 584). Vicarious liability makes an employer liable for a tort its employee commits in the course of employment; it does not extend to torts committed by an independent third party with no employment relationship to the defendant, and there is generally no duty to control the conduct of a third party who goes on to harm the plaintiff absent a special relationship, knowledge and control over the specific source of risk (Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre Pty Ltd v Anzil (2000) 205 CLR 254).

  **Application**

  *Tomas's claim.* Mountain biking on purpose-built jump trails is a dangerous recreational activity, and Tomas's injury arose from the ordinary materialisation of the risk inherent in attempting a jump of this kind — nothing about the trail or the jump had changed, and the accident occurred "in the ordinary course of riding the jump as designed." Applying Rootes v Shelton and Agar v Hyde, a rider who chooses to attempt a jump on a trail designed for that purpose accepts the inherent risk of a bad landing; this is precisely the kind of activity that, by ordinary community standards, carries obvious risks of exactly this sort. Section 5L squarely applies: the risk of a fall or crash attempting a jump is an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity, and s 5L removes Ridge Line Trails' liability for that materialisation whether or not Tomas was actually thinking about the risk at the time. Section 5M provides an independent and equally complete answer: the entrance sign was a clear, prominent warning of exactly this kind of risk (falls, serious injury), given in writing before Tomas rode the trail; s 5M does not require the warning to be specific to the particular jump or manoeuvre, nor does it matter that Tomas, as an experienced rider, may barely have registered it having read it many times before — the section removes the duty regardless of whether the warning was received, understood, or specific.

  *Elena's claim against Yusuf.* Elena's claim is fundamentally different in character: it is not a negligence claim about an inherent risk of riding, but an intentional tort. Yusuf's shoulder-barge was a direct, intentional act causing physical contact without any lawful justification, satisfying battery. The question is whether Elena's participation in shared trail riding meant she consented to this kind of contact. Applying McNamara v Duncan by analogy, riding on a shared trail alongside other riders implies acceptance of the incidental contact and jostling that can occur during ordinary overtaking and riding, but it does not extend to a deliberate, retaliatory assault unconnected to any riding manoeuvre. Yusuf's own admission that he "wanted to teach her a lesson" and that the contact "had nothing to do with overtaking or riding technique" removes any doubt: this was not an inherent risk of trail riding, forcible-but-incidental contact, or anything resembling the ordinary conduct of the activity, but a deliberately inflicted injury of exactly the kind McNamara distinguishes from contact within the rules of an activity. Elena's consent to ride the trail does not extend to this conduct, and battery is made out against Yusuf.

  *Ridge Line Trails' liability for Yusuf.* Sections 5L and 5M are no answer to Elena's claim against Yusuf, since they operate only within the negligence-based duty framework for materialising risks of a dangerous recreational activity; a deliberate battery by another rider is not a risk "of" the activity but an independent intentional wrong. As for Ridge Line Trails' own position, vicarious liability requires an employment relationship between the wrongdoer and the defendant; Yusuf is a paying customer, not an employee or agent, so no vicarious liability arises. A separate question is whether Ridge Line Trails itself owed Elena a duty to protect her from another rider's violence — but, applying Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre v Anzil, an occupier's control over its premises does not extend to a duty over the independent, deliberate conduct of a third party it has no special relationship with or advance knowledge of; nothing here suggests Ridge Line Trails knew or should have known Yusuf posed any risk before the sudden, spontaneous assault.

  **Conclusion**

  Tomas's claim against Ridge Line Trails should fail: his injury was the materialisation of an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity, so s 5L removes liability outright, and s 5M provides an independent and equally complete defence given the clear entrance warning. Elena, by contrast, has a strong claim in battery against Yusuf personally: his deliberate, retaliatory shoulder-barge falls entirely outside the scope of any consent she gave by riding the trail, applying McNamara v Duncan, and she should recover compensatory and likely aggravated damages given the deliberate and retaliatory nature of the attack. Elena's claim against Ridge Line Trails itself, however, is unlikely to succeed: it is not vicariously liable for a customer's independent tort, and, applying Modbury Triangle, its control over the trail network does not translate into a duty to prevent one rider's sudden, unforeseeable act of deliberate violence against another absent any special knowledge or relationship placing it on notice of that specific risk.
