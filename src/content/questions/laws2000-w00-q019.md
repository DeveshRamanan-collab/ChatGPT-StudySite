  **Issue**

  For Calder: (1) whether the RRB's attacks are an armed attack giving Calder a right of self-defence, although the RRB is a non-state actor operating from Tolmar; (2) whether options (a), (b) and (c) would be lawful; and (3) whether Tolmar is responsible for the RRB's attacks. For Nordland: (4) whether forcible humanitarian intervention without Security Council authorisation is lawful, and what the UN route offers.

  **Rule**

  Art 2(4) of the UN Charter prohibits the threat or use of force; the prohibition is also custom (*Nicaragua*, para 34) and jus cogens. The exceptions are Security Council authorisation (art 42) and self-defence (art 51); force at the territorial government's invitation does not breach art 2(4) at all. Self-defence requires an armed attack — one of "the most grave forms of the use of force", judged by scale and effects — and a necessary, proportionate response, reported to the Security Council (*Nicaragua*). The ICJ has left open whether smaller attacks can amount to an armed attack by their cumulative effect.

  Art 51 does not say who must attack. Supplying arms or funds to a group is not enough, and its acts are attributable to a state only under that state's effective control (*Nicaragua*; art 8 ARSIWA). After September 2001 the Security Council reaffirmed the right of self-defence (Res 1373), and state practice increasingly invokes it against non-state actors abroad; but the ICJ treats attribution to the host state as important (*Wall*; *Armed Activities*), and the "unwilling or unable" argument remains controversial. Anticipatory self-defence is contested because art 51 applies "if an armed attack occurs"; support is greater where a specific attack is manifestly imminent. Proportionality looks at the scale of the whole operation (*Oil Platforms* [77]) and its geographic scope (*Armed Activities* [147]). A state may not "allow knowingly its territory to be used for acts contrary to the rights of other States" (*Corfu Channel*).

  Humanitarian intervention is not addressed in the Charter and appears to conflict with art 2(4); NATO's 1999 Kosovo intervention was called "illegal, yet legitimate". The responsibility to protect (ICISS 2001; 2005 World Summit Outcome) covers genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity; its third pillar is a response through UN mechanisms. Under Uniting for Peace (GA Res 377(V)) a deadlocked Council's place may be taken by General Assembly recommendations of collective measures.

  **Application**

  *(1) Armed attack.* Over six months the RRB's rockets and drones have killed 60 Calderan civilians. Individually some attacks may be small, but their scale and effects are serious, and Calder can argue that, taken together, they amount to an armed attack — a point the ICJ has left open. The harder question is whose attack it is. Tolmar's past supply of weapons and money is the kind of support *Nicaragua* found insufficient, it ended before the attacks on Calder began, and nothing suggests Tolmar controls RRB operations. The attacks are not attributable to Tolmar (art 8).

  Calder must therefore rely on self-defence against a non-state actor on Tolmar's territory, pointing to Resolution 1373 and growing state practice. Tolmar has refused consent, so Calder needs the "unwilling or unable" argument: Tolmar is willing — its President condemns the RRB — but unable, having lost control of the north. That argument is controversial, and the ICJ's emphasis on attribution in *Wall* and *Armed Activities* cuts against it. Calder's position is arguable, not settled.

  *(2) Option (a).* If self-defence is available, strikes on the RRB's launch sites and camps are directed at the source of the attacks. The attacks are continuing and Tolmar has refused cooperation, so there is a close temporal link and no obvious peaceful alternative; the evidence of an imminent offensive strengthens necessity. Strikes confined to launch sites and camps are proportionate to halting and preventing further attacks. Calder must report them to the Security Council immediately.

  *Option (b).* Strikes on Tolmar's army bases 200 kilometres away fail. Tolmar's army is not the attacker, so striking it does nothing to halt the RRB, and the stated purpose — so that Tolmar "learns to control its territory" — is punitive. Like the taking of towns hundreds of kilometres from the border in *Armed Activities*, the geographic scope is excessive, and the Court would look at the scale of the whole operation (*Oil Platforms*). Option (b) would be an unlawful use of force against Tolmar and might itself give Tolmar grounds to claim self-defence.

  *Option (c).* Waiting avoids reliance on anticipatory self-defence, which attracted little support in the expanded form of the US National Security Strategy 2002. But Calder's position is not purely anticipatory: the attacks are already occurring, and the announced offensive, with launchers 10 kilometres from the border, is specific and manifestly imminent. Calder need not wait, though any response must still be necessary and proportionate.

  *(3) Tolmar's responsibility.* The RRB's attacks are not attributable to Tolmar, so Tolmar is not responsible for them as its own acts. Calder's better argument is that Tolmar breached its own obligation not to allow knowingly its territory to be used for acts contrary to Calder's rights (*Corfu Channel*); but Tolmar has lost control of the north, and this due diligence duty is not examined further in the unit.

  *(4) Nordland.* Nordland has suffered no armed attack and has no invitation from Tolmar, and the Security Council has not authorised force — the draft was vetoed. The proposed intervention would therefore breach art 2(4). Kosovo does not supply a legal basis: the UK's justification of "overwhelming humanitarian necessity" was contested, and the Commission's verdict of "illegal, yet legitimate" concedes illegality. Starving a besieged minority may involve crimes within R2P, and Tolmar appears unwilling to protect the Vash. But R2P's third pillar is a response through UN mechanisms; it does not create a unilateral right to use force. Critics point to this very problem: R2P does not solve Council paralysis, since the World Summit Outcome only asks the permanent members to refrain from the veto in such cases.

  The lawful route lies through the UN. Because the Council failed to act for lack of unanimity of the permanent members, the General Assembly may recommend collective measures under Uniting for Peace. Its recommendations do not bind, and only the Council decides under Chapter VII. Nordland should press for humanitarian access by negotiation, renewed Council action and an Assembly recommendation.

  **Conclusion**

  The RRB's attacks are serious enough, cumulatively, to be an armed attack, but they are not attributable to Tolmar. Calder's right to strike the RRB in Tolmar depends on the contested "unwilling or unable" argument, strengthened by the imminent offensive. If available, option (a) is necessary and proportionate if confined to RRB targets and reported; option (b) is punitive and disproportionate and would breach art 2(4); option (c) is not required. Tolmar is not responsible for the RRB's acts as such, though its duty under *Corfu Channel* may be engaged. Nordland's intervention would breach art 2(4); R2P works through the UN, and the General Assembly may recommend, but not authorise, collective measures.
