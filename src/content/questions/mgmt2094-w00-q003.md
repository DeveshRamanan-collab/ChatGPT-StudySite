  **Thesis**

  The chief executive is right that agile can make an organisation more innovative, and wrong that every organisation — or every part of one — should become agile. The article she relies on says agile is "not a panacea": it works best where problems are complex, solutions unknown, work can be broken into modules and customers can give rapid feedback, and it is less suited to routine operations. A sealants manufacturer should use agile for the innovation work that meets those conditions, introduce it the way the reading recommends — small and voluntary — and keep disciplined processes where errors are costly. Imposing scrum on every department within a year would break the reading's own rules.

  **Argument**

  *What agile is.* Agile innovation is a series of methods from software development: self-managed, high-intensity teams (scrums); rapid prototyping, testing and learning; methods more open-ended than traditional project management; and creativity tools such as design thinking, with two key roles, the team leader and the process facilitator. Its four values — people over processes and tools, responding to change rather than following a plan, working prototypes over excessive documentation, and customer collaboration over rigid contracts — were written in 2001 as an alternative to waterfall development, which suited stable environments but not rapidly changing markets.

  *The five conditions.* Rigby, Sutherland and Takeuchi set favourable against unfavourable conditions. Agile fits where customer preferences and solution options change frequently, where close collaboration and rapid feedback are feasible, where problems are complex and solutions unknown, where work can be modularised and late changes are manageable, and where interim mistakes provide valuable learning. It fits poorly where conditions are stable, requirements are clear at the outset, similar work has been done before, customers cannot test parts until everything is complete, and interim mistakes "may be catastrophic". Applied to the scenario, new-product development in R&D and campaigns in marketing may well pass the test. Plant maintenance, purchasing, sales calls and accounting are the reading's own examples of routine operations where these conditions are less common, and in a chemical plant an interim mistake may be catastrophic rather than educational. OpenView's founder, Scott Maxwell, found agile worked for strategic planning and marketing but not for selling, because any sales call can change a representative's to-do list on the spot.

  *How agile spreads.* The six practices for leaders cut against a one-year mandate. "Start small and let the word spread": at John Deere, George Tome began applying agile in corporate IT in 2004 and it spread over several years, helped by a coach who could work with people without a software background, until innovation cycle times fell, in some cases by more than 75%. Agile also depends on a cadre of eager participants, and the reading says it is better to enlist passionate volunteers than to coerce resisters; OpenView offered its portfolio companies training and let them decide.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the chief executive is that agile has already left software. John Deere uses it to develop new machines; Mission Bell Winery uses it for everything from wine production to running its senior leadership group; GE Digital and Systematic run their executive teams as agile teams. The reading says "innovation is what agile is all about". A manufacturer of the same kind, Dow Corning, took an average of four years to commercialise a new product, and its share of revenue from new products fell from about 25% to 13% by 1995. On this view only a wholesale shift will break an industrial firm's slow habits; a pilot would let the old culture smother the new one.

  **Evaluation**

  The examples show that agile can work outside software, not that it fits everything. John Deere's success came from starting small, the opposite of a company-wide mandate. And Dow Corning's cure for slow commercialisation was not agile but Design for Six Sigma: a structured process that front-loaded customer research, a six-factor design of experiment and concurrent process engineering. Its pilot, the ArmorAll Deck Protector, was built to an 18-month deadline and produced US$40 million of revenue in its first 12 months. Disciplined processes can speed innovation too. The financial services app pilot shows the real risk of partial adoption — a praised app held up by annual funding and waterfall testing queues — but the reading's answer is to destroy the barriers between agile teams and the rest of the organisation, not to turn every function into a scrum.

  Two further frameworks support a narrower conclusion. Collis warns that "entrepreneurship without strategy leads to chaos": experiments need a strategy that screens them. BCG finds that companies fall short when they choose the wrong innovation model for their context. The chief executive should therefore identify the innovation work that meets agile's conditions, start there with volunteers, practise agile in her own leadership team, and leave routine operations to the processes that suit them. Every organisation that wants to innovate should use agile where it fits, which is not the same as becoming agile everywhere.
