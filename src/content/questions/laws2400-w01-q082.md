  Explain what vicarious liability is and outline two of Fleming's policy reasons for imposing it on employers,
  as quoted in the lecture.
model: |
  Vicarious liability makes one person or entity liable for a tort committed by someone else. The typical
  example introduced in the lecture is an employer's liability for a tort committed by an employee within the
  course of their employment; it does not replace the requirement that the employee committed a tort — it is
  liability for that tort, attached to the employer rather than (or as well as) the employee. The detailed
  tests for establishing when an employee's conduct falls within the course of employment are covered later, in
  Topic 12, but the underlying concept is introduced early because it recurs throughout the unit.

  Professor Fleming, quoted from Sappideen, Vines and Eldridge at [13.45], gives several policy reasons for the
  rule. First, fairness: an employer who engages employees to advance its own economic interest should, in
  fairness, bear a corresponding liability for losses incurred in the course of that enterprise. Second, the
  employer is a more promising source of recompense than the employee, who is often "a man of straw without
  insurance", and the rule promotes wide distribution of tort losses because the employer can pass them on
  through liability insurance and higher prices. Fleming also identifies an admonitory (preventive) value:
  deterrent pressure is most effective on larger units like employers, who can organise and supervise staff to
  reduce accidents, and because employees are rarely worth suing, holding the employer liable creates an
  incentive to discipline wrongdoing staff.
