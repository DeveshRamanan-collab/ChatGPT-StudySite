prompt: |
  The harm principle and the offence principle are negative in nature: they set limits on public force. Legal paternalism and legal moralism look instead at positive action the state might take to help people live a good life. Week 10 set two other liberal approaches beside them. John Rawls distinguishes the right, the sphere of principles of justice including the protection of rights, from the good, the sphere of value; the state should be "neutral" on all matters of the good life, and the only justification for state interference is to prevent rights violations and to punish those who violate the rights of others. Joseph Raz rejects state neutrality. The state is entitled to, and should, promote intrinsically worthy ways of life and discourage intrinsically unworthy ones. Yet Raz is a liberal perfectionist: the state should not use the criminal law to promote worthy ways of life or pursue perfectionist ideals, though it may promote virtue by non-coercive means — for instance, by taxing activities of which the majority disapproves.

  On the other side stand the legal moralists. Lord Devlin held that the majority has the right to translate conventional moral judgments into criminal law; Robert George, that the law should protect our moral character by prohibiting vice, even if the conduct is harmless, according to the standards of critical morality. Mill, for his part, held that our own good is not a sufficient reason for "compulsion and control, whether the means used be physical force in the form of legal penalties, or the moral coercion of public opinion".

  Critically evaluate the following statement:

  "Liberal perfectionism is an unstable compromise. A state that may tax a way of life because it is unworthy has already abandoned the liberal limits on state power; it differs from Devlin and George only in its choice of weapon."

  In your essay, distinguish the question whether the state may promote a view of the good life from the question by what means it may do so, and say where Rawls, Raz, Devlin, George and Mill stand on each. Draw on material from more than one week of the unit. Take a clear position, support it with reasons, and deal with the strongest counterargument to your view.
model: |
  **Thesis**

  The statement is wrong, though it finds a real pressure point. Raz's position is not a halfway house between Rawls and Devlin; it answers a different question from each of them. Rawls and Raz disagree about whether the state may promote a view of the good life. Raz and the legal moralists disagree about whether it may use the criminal law to do so. The choice of weapon is not a detail: on the liberal view, the means of coercion is exactly where the limits on state power lie. The statement is right, however, that Raz's own example, taxing activities of which the majority disapproves, strains his position twice over: taxation is not obviously non-coercive, and majority disapproval is the test of popular morality, not of intrinsic worth.

  **Argument**

  *Two questions.* The week's positions can be placed on two axes. The first asks whether the state may promote a view of the good life. Rawls says no: the state should be neutral on all matters of the good life and should interfere only to prevent and punish rights violations. Raz, Devlin and George say yes. The second asks whether the state may use the criminal law to make people good. Devlin and George say yes; Devlin would enforce popular morality and George critical morality, but both would criminalise harmless vice. Raz says no. Mill stands apart from both: power may be exercised over an adult against their will only to prevent harm to others. The statement collapses the two axes. It assumes that once the state answers yes to the first question, the second can only be a question of tactics.

  *Why the weapon matters.* The liberal tradition has never treated the means as incidental. Mill's principle is a principle about "compulsion and control". Hart's deeper criticism of Devlin asks whether the misery and evil consequences of criminal punishment are well understood. The rationale for legal paternalism, which the lecture connected with Raz, includes the necessity of the risk: is there a less risky way of achieving the goal, and could measures short of public coercion do the job? Raz's position applies that question to perfectionism. A state may care whether its citizens live worthwhile lives, but it must choose the least coercive means of helping them, and the criminal law is the most coercive means it has. That is not a compromise between liberalism and moralism; it is liberalism's concern with coercion applied to a state that is allowed to have views about the good.

  *Why Raz is not Devlin.* Devlin's case for enforcement rests on social cohesion: without a shared morality the social order would break down, so there are no limits to legislation against immorality, as there are none to legislation against treason and sedition. Raz needs none of this. He does not claim that society will collapse without perfectionist policy, and his view is consistent with Hart's denial that morality is a seamless web. George's critical moralism is closer, since it too appeals to what is objectively worthy. But George would protect moral character by prohibiting vice; Raz would leave vice lawful and make worthier options more attractive. A person who chooses a disfavoured way of life in Raz's state remains free to choose it; in George's state that person is punished.

  *Why Raz is not Rawls.* Rawls's neutrality is the cleaner position, and it avoids every difficulty this essay raises, because a neutral state never ranks ways of life at all. But it also gives up something Raz thinks the state should be able to do: say that some ways of life are worthier than others, and act on that judgement by means that leave people free to disagree. The difference between them concerns the state's aims; the difference between Raz and the moralists concerns its means. The statement treats only the second as real.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the statement is that Raz's line between coercive and non-coercive means cannot be held. Taxation is compulsory. The welfare principle itself describes compulsory taxation as forcing willing and unwilling citizens to support public projects they may not benefit from, and the lecture treated it as a limit on individual freedom that needs justifying. Mill's principle reaches beyond legal penalties to "the moral coercion of public opinion", so Mill would object to state disapproval backed by a tax, not only to the criminal law. A tax on an activity because it is unworthy is a financial penalty for living a certain way, enforced ultimately by law. And Raz's example makes matters worse: the activities to be taxed are those "of which the majority disapproves". That is Devlin's test. If the state taxes what the majority disapproves of, it enforces popular morality with a different weapon, which is exactly what the statement says.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument shows that Raz's example is badly chosen, not that his position is unstable. There are two replies.

  First, coercion comes in degrees, and the liberal objection to the criminal law is an objection to its degree. Hart's question about the misery of criminal punishment marks the difference between a tax and a prison sentence: a tax makes an option more costly, while imprisonment removes the option and punishes the person who takes it. The paternalist factors give the same structure: the more serious the interference, the more weight the goal must bear, and the more clearly less coercive means must have failed. Raz can concede that taxation is coercive in some sense and still hold that it can be a proportionate means where the criminal law is not. Mill's objection to the moral coercion of public opinion shows that Raz is less liberal than Mill, not that he is no more liberal than Devlin.

  Second, the majority-disapproval example can be separated from Raz's principle. The principle refers to intrinsically worthy and unworthy ways of life, a standard of critical, not popular, morality. Hart's warning that popular morality may rest on ignorance, superstition or misunderstanding applies to the example as much as to Devlin, and a Razian should accept it. Read this way, Raz shares George's standard but not his means, and shares the liberal concern with coercion but not Rawls's neutrality.

  The statement therefore fails. Liberal perfectionism is not Devlin's project with a different weapon; it is a refusal to pursue that project with the criminal law at all. But it is stable only if it keeps two commitments that its own example blurs: that the worth of a way of life is judged by critical morality rather than by majority disapproval, and that the non-coercive means it uses really are less coercive than the law it renounces.
