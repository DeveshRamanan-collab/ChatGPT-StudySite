  **Issue**

  The issues are: (1) the content of the obligation of peaceful settlement and whether Kamsai's walk-out breaches it; (2) whether Kamsai's denial prevents a dispute from existing; (3) which means of settlement are available and which bind; (4) the Security Council's role; and (5) the effect of Kamsai's platform.

  **Rule**

  Art 2(3) of the UN Charter requires members to "settle their international disputes by peaceful means in such a manner that international peace and security, and justice, are not endangered"; the obligation is also binding as custom (*Nicaragua*, para 290). It is an obligation of conduct, not of result: states must try to settle peacefully, and keep trying, but are not obliged to reach a settlement, and the choice of means rests on consent. Art 33(1) lists the means and ends with "other peaceful means of their own choice". Negotiation presupposes "a genuine attempt by one of the disputing parties to engage in discussions with the other disputing party, with a view to resolving the dispute" (*North Sea Continental Shelf*, para 85); GA Resolution 53/101 calls for good faith, a constructive atmosphere and refraining from "any conduct which might undermine the negotiations and their progress".

  The diplomatic modes — negotiation, good offices, mediation, inquiry and conciliation — do not bind. Arbitral awards and ICJ judgments do, and both rest on consent: for arbitration, a treaty providing for arbitration of future disputes, such as UNCLOS, or a compromis; for the ICJ, a compromis, treaty clause, optional clause declarations or forum prorogatum.

  Whether a dispute exists "is a matter for objective determination", and "the mere denial of the existence of a dispute does not prove its non-existence" (*Peace Treaties*). One party's claim must be "positively opposed" by the other, and the respondent aware of it (*South West Africa*). The determination "requires an examination of the facts" (*Georgia v Russian Federation*, para 30), and bilateral diplomatic exchanges showing conflicting claims are usually the strongest evidence of a territorial dispute.

  Under art 52, members of regional arrangements "shall make every effort" to settle local disputes through them before referring them to the Security Council, whose Chapter VI powers are to investigate and recommend (arts 34–37). An island is "a naturally formed area of land, surrounded by water, which is above water at high tide" (UNCLOS art 121(1)); artificial islands "do not possess the status of islands" (art 60(8)).

  **Application**

  *(1) Negotiation and the obligation of conduct.* Kamsai agreed to negotiate, attended two rounds, then walked out without explanation and has refused all further meetings while granting gas licences in the disputed waters. Withdrawing suddenly without justification is a classic sign of negotiating without good faith, and granting licences during the dispute is the kind of conduct that "might undermine the negotiations" (Res 53/101). Kamsai is not obliged to agree to anything, but by refusing all further engagement it is arguably no longer making the "genuine attempt" the obligation of conduct requires (*North Sea*). Pelora, by contrast, would not breach art 2(3) merely because the dispute is still unresolved in ten years: Canada and Denmark disputed Hans Island peacefully for about fifty years. Pelora's obligation is to keep trying peacefully and never to use force.

  *(2) Is there a dispute?* Kamsai's statement that "there is no dispute" is a mere denial, which does not prove the dispute's non-existence (*Peace Treaties*). For 15 years the states have exchanged notes asserting sovereignty and protesting each other's licences — conflicting claims communicated directly, of which Kamsai was plainly aware. Pelora's claim is positively opposed (*South West Africa*), and on an examination of the facts (*Georgia v Russian Federation*) these bilateral exchanges are the strongest evidence of a territorial dispute. A dispute exists, so Kamsai cannot defeat a third-party process this way.

  *(3) The means available.* Good offices or mediation by a trusted third party, perhaps the UN Secretary-General, whose good offices role has expanded, could bring Kamsai back to the table, but the outcome would not bind. An inquiry could establish the facts, including whether the reef is above water at high tide, which matters for question (5). Conciliation through the CSCC Conciliation Commission would produce a report setting out the facts and the parties' positions with non-binding recommendations — as the Timor Sea conciliation between Timor-Leste and Australia did, which led to a boundary treaty — but Kamsai could reject it.

  For a binding result Pelora needs adjudication. Both states are parties to UNCLOS, which provides for arbitration of future disputes, so their consent has already been given by treaty, as in the *South China Sea Arbitration* brought by the Philippines against China. Questions about the status of the feature and the parties' entitlements under UNCLOS fit that process. The award would be final and binding, and, as in arbitration generally, each party would have a say in the composition of the tribunal. The ICJ is not available unless Kamsai consents: neither state has made an optional clause declaration, and Kamsai will not agree to a compromis. Kamsai could consent later (forum prorogatum), and an ICJ judgment would also bind, but Pelora cannot compel it.

  *(4) The Security Council.* Any member may bring a dispute to the Council's attention (art 35), and the Council may investigate whether its continuance is likely to endanger international peace and security (art 34), call on the parties to settle it by peaceful means (art 33(2)), and recommend procedures — including, as a general rule, the ICJ for a legal dispute (art 36(1), (3)). Under Chapter VI these are recommendations, not coercive Chapter VII measures. And because both states belong to the CSCC, art 52(2) expects them to make every effort to use the regional arrangement first; Pelora should invoke the CSCC Conciliation Commission before going to the Council.

  *(5) The platform.* The reef is exposed only at low tide, so it is not "above water at high tide" and is not an island (art 121(1)). Building a concrete platform and helipad cannot change that: artificial islands "do not possess the status of islands" (art 60(8)), and the *South China Sea* tribunal held that a feature's status depends on its natural condition, so artificial installations cannot establish sovereignty over it. Artificial islands and low-tide elevations are not subject to sovereignty claims. The platform does not strengthen Kamsai's claim.

  **Conclusion**

  Kamsai's walk-out and continued licensing are hard to reconcile with good-faith negotiation and arguably breach its obligation of conduct under art 2(3), but no state breaches that obligation merely because a dispute remains unresolved. Kamsai's denial cannot make the 15-year dispute disappear (*Peace Treaties*; *South West Africa*). Mediation, inquiry and CSCC conciliation would not bind; arbitration under UNCLOS, to which both states have already consented, would. The ICJ needs Kamsai's consent. Pelora may bring the dispute to the Security Council, which can recommend but not impose a settlement under Chapter VI, after first using the CSCC (art 52). Kamsai's platform adds nothing to its claim (UNCLOS arts 60(8), 121(1); *South China Sea*).
