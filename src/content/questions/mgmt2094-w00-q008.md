  **Thesis**

  The statement confuses an outcome with a theory. Upending an established industry is where a disruptive innovation may end up, but Christensen's theory, as the class activity summarises it, describes a particular route there: starting with overlooked, low-end or new-market customers, offering something simpler, cheaper or more accessible, and then improving enough to challenge established firms. Innovations can upend industries by other routes, such as redefining value for non-customers. Calling them all disruptive empties the word of meaning and, more importantly, misleads incumbents about how to respond.

  **Argument**

  *The five questions.* The class activity turns the theory into five questions. Who is the incumbent? Was the initial customer underserved, overlooked or a non-consumer? What made the offering cheaper, simpler, more convenient or more accessible? Did it start outside the mainstream and then move toward mainstream customers? And what is the verdict — disruptive, potentially disruptive, or not disruptive? The statement skips straight to the end of the fourth question.

  *A case that fits.* Nickless's account of Gilbert + Tobin passes every question. The incumbents are prestigious law firms such as G+T, whose expertise was bespoke, high-value work. NewLaw firms such as LegalVision began with lower-value, higher-volume transactional work — wills, loan documents, first-round document reviews — and LegalVision served the underserviced small-to-medium enterprise market. The value proposition was work done faster and cheaper through standard processes and digital automation. And G+T believed disruption would move up the market from the lower-value work towards the higher value, where the bulk of its business lay: simple document review could turn into running the A$5 million bolt-on acquisition. That is Christensen's pattern, and Nickless names Christensen's work as still highly relevant. It also explains G+T's response: by buying a 20 per cent stake in LegalVision it gained exposure to low-value but high-growth work and the ability to learn from the disruption as it arrived.

  *A case that does not.* Yellow Tail changed the US wine market, but on the unit's frameworks it is value innovation, not disruption. Using the four actions framework, it eliminated enological terminology, ageing qualities and above-the-line marketing; reduced wine complexity, range and vineyard prestige; raised its price above budget wine and its retail store involvement; and created easy drinking, ease of selection, and fun and adventure. It did reach non-customers — people who were not generally wine buyers — which resembles a new-market entry. But it priced itself above budget wine rather than below the market, and the materials describe it almost creating a new category, not moving up to displace premium wines. Value innovation seeks radically superior value to capture the mass market by following non-customers; the disruption questions trace a path from the margin to the mainstream. They are different tests.

  *Where it sits.* The innovation map places disruptive innovation inside strategic innovation — external and discontinuous — alongside new markets, new offerings, channels and Blue Ocean Strategy. Disruption is one kind of strategic innovation, not a synonym for it.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the statement is practical. For an incumbent the route hardly matters: Sears, a defender counting on its brand recognition and its stores, lost its edge when agile online players upended retail, and its shareholders lost either way. The class activity's own cases resist clean verdicts — it asks whether Airbnb is disruptive or simply a new platform business model, and where Spotify's disruption actually occurred — which suggests the theory's boundaries are blurred. On this view "disruptive" is a fair everyday word for any innovation that overturns an industry.

  **Evaluation**

  The blurred cases are a reason to apply the questions carefully, not to abandon them; the activity asks for a defended verdict precisely because the answer is not obvious. And the route matters most to incumbents. It was the route — lower-value work first, then moving up — that told G+T to act while the threat was still in work it could not service economically, and to partner rather than compete. Other threats call for other responses: BCG's defender example, Allstate, monitored the shift to online and app-based insurance and acquired the pioneer, Esurance. An incumbent that calls every new rival disruptive loses the ability to tell which response fits.

  So the statement should be rejected. Many innovations upend industries; only some get there by disruption. Keeping the word for Christensen's route keeps the five questions useful, and the questions tell managers what to watch for and when to move.
