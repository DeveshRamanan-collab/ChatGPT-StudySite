  **Issue**

  The issues are: (1) whether the SRSO has international legal personality; (2) whether the Council had power to create the Special Tribunal; (3) to whom the battalion's conduct at Merrin is attributable; and (4) whether the families' claims against the SRSO and Brenmark in Brenmark's courts can proceed.

  **Rule**

  ARIO art 2(a) defines an international organisation as one "established by a treaty or other instrument governed by international law and possessing its own international legal personality". Personality may be conferred expressly (as in WTO Agreement art VIII) or implicitly: in *Reparation for Injuries* the ICJ held the UN to be an international person although its Charter is silent — "not the same thing as saying that it is a State". Personality is distinct from that of the members, and includes the capacity to bring claims and to be responsible for wrongdoing.

  IOs have no general competence. In the *Nuclear Weapons* Advisory Opinion the ICJ said IOs are governed by "the principle of speciality", so the WHO could not address the legality of nuclear weapons. But an IO "must be deemed to have those powers which, though not expressly provided in the Charter, are conferred upon it by necessary implication as being essential to the performance of its duties" (*Reparation*). Implied powers can be disputed, as when the Security Council created the ICTY and ICTR.

  Every internationally wrongful act of an IO entails its responsibility (ARIO art 3), where attributable conduct breaches its obligations (art 4). Conduct of its organs or agents in performing their functions is attributable to it (art 6). Under art 7, conduct of a state organ placed at an IO's disposal is attributed to the IO "if the organization exercises effective control over that conduct" — so a contributing state that kept control of its troops can be responsible.

  IO immunities rest on treaty. On the CPIUN model, the organisation and its property are immune from every form of legal process unless expressly waived (art II s 2). IOs may have to provide alternative modes for settling private claims. In *Mothers of Srebrenica* the Dutch Supreme Court (2012) and the ECtHR (2013) recognised the UN's immunity, and the case went on against the Netherlands alone.

  **Application**

  *(1) Personality.* The SRSO was established by treaty, the SRSO Charter, and it has organs of its own — a Council and a Secretariat — distinct from its members. The Charter is silent on personality, but following *Reparation* personality can be implied from the functions the members intended the organisation to exercise. The SRSO is empowered to deploy missions, which requires it to act on the international plane, to agree with host states and to receive contributed troops. The members' conclusion of a Headquarters Agreement granting it immunity also treats it as an entity distinct from themselves. The SRSO therefore has international legal personality, though, like the UN, it is not a state and its capacities are only those its functions require.

  *(2) The Special Tribunal.* The SRSO's express power is to deploy missions to restore order and protect civilians. Under the principle of speciality, it has no general competence. The Council will argue that trying rebel leaders is necessarily implied, as essential to restoring order. The families may argue that a judicial function is a significant step beyond deployment, not "essential to the performance of its duties" — a mission can restore order without a tribunal. The argument mirrors the controversy over the Security Council's creation of the ICTY and ICTR. It is genuinely contestable; on balance, a court is more likely to find that the power is not implied, because the Charter confines the SRSO to deploying missions, and trying individuals goes well beyond that.

  *(3) Attribution of the conduct at Merrin.* The battalion is an organ of Brenmark placed at the SRSO's disposal, so the governing rule is art 7, not art 6. The question is whether the SRSO exercised effective control over the specific conduct — the withdrawal and the expulsion of civilians. The facts point firmly to Brenmark. The SRSO Force Commander issued only general orders; Brenmark's colonel retained command over the battalion's movements; Brenmark's defence ministry approved each operation in advance; and the decisive withdrawal was ordered on direct instructions from Brenmark's defence ministry. The SRSO did not effectively control that conduct, so under art 7 it is not attributable to the SRSO. Because the battalion remained a Brenmarkian organ and Brenmark controlled the specific conduct, it is attributable to Brenmark. A contributing state that keeps control of its troops can itself be responsible, and that is this case.

  *(4) The claims in Brenmark's courts.* The claim against the SRSO faces its immunity under the Headquarters Agreement, which, like the CPIUN, grants immunity "from every form of legal process" unless expressly waived. The SRSO has refused to waive. *Mothers of Srebrenica* shows that domestic and European courts have upheld such immunity even in claims arising from mass killings. The families can argue that the SRSO's failure to provide any alternative mode of settlement should weigh against immunity, because IOs may have to provide such mechanisms; but the case law points to the immunity being upheld, leaving the families to press the SRSO politically. The claim against Brenmark is different: a state has no immunity in its own courts, so that claim can proceed, and since the conduct is attributable to Brenmark, it is the stronger claim — just as the Srebrenica case continued against the Netherlands alone.

  **Conclusion**

  The SRSO has implied international legal personality, following *Reparation*, from the functions its members gave it. Its power to create a Special Tribunal is doubtful under the principle of speciality, since trying individuals is not necessarily implied by a mandate to deploy stabilisation missions. The conduct at Merrin is attributable to Brenmark, not to the SRSO, because Brenmark, not the SRSO, exercised effective control over the withdrawal (ARIO art 7). In Brenmark's courts, the claim against the SRSO will probably fail on immunity (*Mothers of Srebrenica*), though its failure to offer any alternative mode of settlement is a point to press. The claim against Brenmark can proceed and is the families' best route.
