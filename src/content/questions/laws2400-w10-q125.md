model: |-
  The issue is whether State Rail's liability for its negligence (the defective torch) extends to Peter's imprisonment.

  Factual causation (CLA s 5D(1)(a); 'but for'): Peter can argue that but for the fall he would not have been on workers' compensation, would not have been cut off, and would not have turned to growing hemp. Even if that chain is accepted on the balance of probabilities (s 5E), causation is not finished.

  Scope of liability (s 5D(1)(b)) is a normative question: is it appropriate for liability to extend to this harm? Under s 5D(4) the court considers whether and why responsibility should be imposed. Peter's deliberate decision to commit a crime is his own later act, and a novus actus interveniens can be the plaintiff's own act. If it breaks the chain, State Rail is not liable for harm after the break. In McKew the plaintiff's unreasonable conduct broke the chain. Not every later act does: in March v Stramare the defendant's conduct generated the very risk that materialised. A defective torch creates a risk of falling, not of the plaintiff choosing to commit a crime. The wrongful cutting off of compensation may be argued as a pressure, but it does not make the crime part of the original risk.

  Peter is likely to fail: his deliberate criminal conduct breaks the chain, so the imprisonment falls outside the scope of State Rail's liability for the fall.
