  Issue: whether the people of Western Australia have a right under international law to secede unilaterally.

  Rule: peoples have a right of self-determination — a principle in UN Charter art 1(2) and a human right in
  ICCPR art 1 ("All peoples have the right of self-determination"). In the decolonisation context the right is
  generally accepted, as the ICJ affirmed in Chagos (2019). Outside that context the right has internal and
  external dimensions, and whether it includes a right to unilaterally secede from an existing state is highly
  contentious. Supporters of Kosovo's 2008 secession argued for a right in extreme cases of systematic
  discrimination and oppression; opponents stressed territorial integrity and sovereignty. The ICJ said Kosovo's
  declaration was not in itself illegal but did not endorse a right to secede. In Re Secession of Quebec (1998)
  the Supreme Court of Canada held unilateral secession inconsistent with international law: Quebec's internal
  self-determination was fulfilled, so there was no ground for external self-determination.

  Application: WA is not a colony, and its population participates fully in Australia's democratic government; a
  wish not to be governed from Canberra and to own resources is not systematic oppression. Its internal
  self-determination is fulfilled.

  Conclusion: WA has no right to secede under international law; any statehood would also have to satisfy the
  Montevideo criteria and would face the politics of recognition.
