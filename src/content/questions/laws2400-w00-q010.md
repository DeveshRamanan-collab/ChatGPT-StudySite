  Roast & River is a boutique manufacturer of bottled cold-brew coffee, sold sealed in glass bottles through supermarkets. Eighteen months ago it switched to a new, cheaper eco-friendly bottle cap supplied by a new manufacturer. Internal quality records show that, during trial runs before the switch, random batch testing occasionally detected a small proportion of caps that failed to seal properly, allowing air in and creating a risk that mould could develop inside an affected bottle well before its printed use-by date. Roast & River's operations manager decided that testing every bottle would be too slow and expensive for a small company, and instead set quality control to test only one bottle in every two hundred from each production run. Internal emails show the manager was aware this sampling rate meant "a few dodgy seals could get through and sit on a shelf for a couple of weeks before anyone noticed a pattern," but concluded the residual risk was "acceptable" given how much continuous testing of every bottle would cost and slow down production.

  Farid bought a bottle of Roast & River cold-brew from his local supermarket, well within the printed use-by date. The cap seal on his bottle had in fact failed. He did not notice anything unusual about the taste until he had drunk most of the bottle. He became seriously unwell that night with vomiting and severe abdominal pain, and was hospitalised for four days. Testing confirmed the bottle contained mould toxins consistent with a compromised seal that had allowed contamination to develop over roughly ten days on the shelf.

  Farid had no way of inspecting the seal's integrity before opening the bottle; the defect was not visible from the outside, and the bottle looked completely normal.

  Advise Farid on a claim in negligence against Roast & River.
model: |
  **Issue**

  Whether Roast & River owed Farid a duty of care as manufacturer of the cold-brew coffee he consumed, and, if so, whether its decision to test only one bottle in two hundred, despite known evidence of intermittent seal failures, amounted to a breach of that duty.

  **Rule**

  Before Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, the owner of "dangerous things" had a duty to take reasonable precautions to keep them from third parties, but no equivalent duty existed for non-dangerous things. After Donoghue, and Grant v Australian Knitting Mills [1936] AC 85, the ordinary principles of negligence became a basis for liability for defective products generally: a manufacturer who sells a product intending it to reach the consumer in the form it left them, with no reasonable possibility of intermediate examination, owes that consumer a duty to take reasonable care. This is now an established category of duty: where a case falls within an established category, a court can simply point to the case law recognising it — "this is a manufacturer/consumer relationship, an established category since Donoghue v Stevenson" — without needing to run a full salient features analysis.

  Establishing a duty does not, however, establish breach. Graham Barclay Oysters Pty Ltd v Ryan (2002) 211 CLR 540 shows that foreseeability of harm does not automatically mean a duty has been breached: there, consumers contracted Hepatitis A from oysters contaminated by faecal matter after a storm; although the risk was reasonably foreseeable and its magnitude significant, the High Court held the grower's depuration process — which reduced but could not eliminate the viral load — was not a breach, because "the degree of probability of its occurrence" did not justify the "difficult, expensive and inconvenient alleviating action" (eliminating the risk entirely) the consumers argued for. Breach requires weighing the probability of the risk occurring and the magnitude (seriousness) of the harm if it does, against the burden — the cost, difficulty and inconvenience — of taking precautions that would have avoided it.

  **Application**

  *Duty.* Roast & River is a manufacturer selling a sealed product intended to reach consumers such as Farid in the form it left the factory, with no reasonable possibility of intermediate examination — the defect was internal to the seal and invisible from outside the bottle, exactly the situation Grant v Australian Knitting Mills contemplates. This falls squarely within the established manufacturer/consumer category recognised since Donoghue v Stevenson. There is no need to run a full salient features inquiry, since the relationship here is not novel: Roast & River owed Farid a duty of care as manufacturer of the product he consumed.

  *Breach.* The duty being established, the question becomes whether Roast & River's conduct fell below the standard of reasonable care — and here, unlike a case where the risk was truly unknown, foreseeability alone does not settle the matter, as Graham Barclay Oysters makes clear. Applying that case's weighing exercise: the probability of the risk occurring was not speculative but demonstrated — Roast & River's own trial testing had already detected that "a small proportion" of the new caps failed to seal properly, and its own operations manager recorded in writing that "a few dodgy seals could get through" under the chosen sampling regime. The magnitude of harm from a failure was also significant: an undetected seal failure could allow mould and its toxins to develop over the shelf life of the product, capable of causing serious illness, as it in fact did to Farid, resulting in four days' hospitalisation. Both limbs of the probability/magnitude side of the Graham Barclay Oysters equation therefore point strongly toward the risk being one that called for a real response, not one so trivial or improbable that a reasonable manufacturer could simply proceed with existing precautions.

  Against that, the burden of avoiding the risk must be weighed. In Graham Barclay Oysters, the alleviating action argued for — eliminating the risk of contamination entirely through means beyond the existing depuration process — was found to be "difficult, expensive and inconvenient" relative to the low probability of occurrence, and this justified the absence of further precautions. The present facts are materially different. Roast & River's own decision was not a choice between some depuration and total elimination of a diffuse environmental risk; it was a deliberate choice to test only one in two hundred bottles, made with actual knowledge from its own trial data that this sampling rate would let some failed seals through undetected. Unlike Graham Barclay Oysters, where the residual risk could not be entirely eliminated even with more testing, here more frequent testing (or examining every batch more closely, or investigating why the new caps were failing at all before rolling them out) was a readily available, more protective alternative that Roast & River itself contemplated and consciously rejected on cost and speed grounds, despite knowing the consequence could be contaminated bottles reaching consumers. Where Graham Barclay Oysters involved a manufacturer already taking substantial (if imperfect) precautions against a risk that could not practically be eliminated, Roast & River made a documented, cost-driven decision to accept a known, quantified risk of exactly the kind of harm that eventuated, without taking a further step it acknowledged was available. This is a materially stronger case for breach than Graham Barclay Oysters, where the defendant's precautions failed only to reduce, rather than knowingly permit, a foreseeable risk.

  *Causation.* Although the question focuses on duty and breach, causation is not seriously in doubt on these facts: testing confirmed the mould toxins were consistent with a compromised seal developing over roughly ten days on the shelf, directly linking the type of defect Roast & River's own records anticipated to the injury Farid in fact suffered.

  **Conclusion**

  Roast & River owed Farid a duty of care as manufacturer, following the established category recognised in Donoghue v Stevenson and Grant v Australian Knitting Mills, since the sealed bottle gave Farid no reasonable opportunity for intermediate examination. On breach, applying Graham Barclay Oysters Pty Ltd v Ryan's weighing of probability and magnitude of harm against the burden of precautions, Roast & River is in a considerably weaker position than the defendant in that case: it had actual, documented knowledge from its own testing that its chosen sampling rate would let defective seals through, the magnitude of resulting harm (mould toxin illness) was serious, and more protective alternatives were known to be available and were consciously rejected for cost and speed reasons rather than being genuinely impractical. Farid should be advised that he has strong prospects of establishing both duty and breach, and, the causal link between the seal defect and his illness being clear on the testing evidence, a good claim in negligence against Roast & River overall.
