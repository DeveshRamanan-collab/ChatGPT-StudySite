  Explain the compensatory and deterrence functions of tort law, including one practical problem with each, and
  briefly contrast them with corrective justice.
model: |
  The compensatory function treats tort law as a mechanism to put an injured claimant back, so far as money
  can, into the position she would have been in had the wrong not occurred. In practice this faces problems
  such as miscalculating a claimant's loss of future earnings, the high cost of legal fees, and difficulties
  with lump-sum payments that must estimate future loss in a single award. The deterrence function instead
  argues that making a defendant (such as an employer) pay for an accident gives them, and others in a similar
  position, an incentive to avoid causing such accidents in future; law-and-economics scholars like Calabresi
  and Posner extend this into a systemic argument that tort law allocates the costs of accidents to produce an
  efficient outcome for society. A practical problem here is that it is far from certain tort law actually
  deters individuals — the lecture's drink-driving example shows it is unclear whether the threat of paying
  compensation deters more than licence cancellation or imprisonment — and insurance may further blunt any
  deterrent effect by absorbing the cost.

  Corrective justice, associated with Weinrib, differs from both by focusing on moral responsibility rather
  than outcomes: because the defendant wrongly harmed the plaintiff's rights, fairness requires the wrongdoer
  personally to correct that wrong through compensation, resting on the idea of free human agency. Unlike the
  compensatory function's focus on the victim's position or the deterrence function's focus on future
  behaviour, corrective justice is backward-looking and relational, tying payment specifically to the
  individual who caused the harm — though this can ignore the wider systems and other people often involved in
  an incident.
