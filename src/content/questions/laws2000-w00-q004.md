  **Issue**

  Three issues arise: (a) whether Australia can assert prescriptive jurisdiction over Ms Kade for the hospital attack and the counterfeiting; (b) whether the AFP may lawfully seize her in Tessaly; and (c) whether Dr Voss may be arrested in Sydney, both while he is foreign minister and after he leaves office.

  **Rule**

  Jurisdiction takes three forms: to prescribe (making law applicable to persons and conduct), to enforce (arrest, seizure and the like) and to adjudicate. In *SS Lotus* the PCIJ held that international law leaves states "a wide measure of discretion" to extend their laws beyond their territory, limited only by prohibitive rules; but, failing a permissive rule, a state "may not exercise its power in any form in the territory of another State". Israel's 1960 seizure of Eichmann in Argentina was unlawful, and the Security Council requested compensation.

  Where there is a foreign element, prescriptive jurisdiction must rest on a recognised ground. Territoriality is fundamental; objective territoriality covers an offence completed on the territory though elements occurred abroad. Active nationality allows a state to prosecute its nationals for acts abroad, and dual nationals can face both states; *Nottebohm* describes nationality as "a genuine connection of existence, interests and sentiments". Passive personality — jurisdiction because the victim is a national (*US v Yunis*) — remains controversial and is better treated as subsidiary. The protective principle covers acts abroad by non-citizens that genuinely threaten a vital security interest, including counterfeiting currency. Where jurisdictions concur there is no universally binding rule; treaties such as the Cybercrime Convention (art 22(5)) require consultation, and usually the state with the closest link is best placed.

  Immunities limit enforcement and adjudication. Personal immunity (ratione personae) attaches to the office: heads of state, heads of government and foreign ministers enjoy complete immunity, even for private acts, while in office (*Arrest Warrant*; *Djibouti v France*). The immunity belongs to the state and exists "to ensure the effective performance of their functions" (*Arrest Warrant*). Once the person leaves office, functional immunity (ratione materiae) continues for official acts done under the colour of authority, but private acts are no longer covered. The source is customary international law.

  **Application**

  *(a) Jurisdiction over Ms Kade — the hospital attack.* Greyfin launched the attack from Tessaly, but its effects — the shutdown of a Melbourne hospital and Mr Okafor's death — occurred in Australia. The offence was completed on Australian territory, so objective territoriality gives Australia the strongest ground. Tessaly, for its part, has subjective territoriality, because the attack was initiated and planned there.

  Australia also has active nationality jurisdiction: Kade is an Australian national. That she also holds Tessalian nationality does not defeat this, because dual nationals can face both states. Tessaly may argue that her genuine connection, in the *Nottebohm* sense, is now with Tessaly after ten years' residence, but states largely decide who their own nationals are, and Kade was born in Adelaide and holds Australian nationality. Finally, Mr Okafor was an Australian victim, so passive personality is available (*US v Yunis*), though as a controversial ground it should be relied on only as a supplement to territoriality.

  *The counterfeiting.* The counterfeit notes were designed and distributed from Tessaly. Some are circulating in Queensland, which gives a territorial link, but the clearest ground is protection: counterfeiting a state's currency is the classic example of conduct abroad that threatens a vital state interest. Active nationality is again available because Kade is Australian.

  *Concurrence.* Tessaly also has jurisdiction, on the grounds of territoriality (the conduct took place there) and nationality. There is no binding rule of priority. If both states are parties to the Cybercrime Convention, art 22(5) would require them to consult on the most appropriate jurisdiction; otherwise, the usual approach is that the state with the closest link is best placed. Australia's links — the death, the hospital, the currency — are strong, but the choice will be settled by consultation or extradition arrangements, not by unilateral action.

  *(b) The proposed operation in Tessaly.* Seizing Kade in Tessaly without its consent would be an exercise of enforcement jurisdiction on foreign territory. Under the *Lotus* restriction this is prohibited unless a permissive rule applies, and none does. Having prescriptive jurisdiction does not give Australia a right to enforce it in Tessaly. The operation would be unlawful, as the seizure of Eichmann was, and would expose Australia to a claim for reparation. Australia should instead seek Kade's extradition or her arrest if she enters Australia.

  *(c) Dr Voss while in office.* As a serving foreign minister, Dr Voss enjoys personal immunity from Australia's criminal jurisdiction. That immunity is complete: it covers both his official approval of the hospital attack and his private jet-ski crash (*Arrest Warrant*; *Djibouti v France*). In *Arrest Warrant*, even the issue and circulation of a warrant for a sitting foreign minister breached his immunity. Arresting him in Sydney while he is in office would therefore breach international law, whatever the seriousness of the allegations. The immunity belongs to Tessaly, which could waive it.

  *Dr Voss after office.* Once he leaves office, personal immunity ends and only functional immunity remains. His approval of the Greyfin attack was, on the facts, an official act done as part of a government operation, so functional immunity would continue to protect him for that act. The jet-ski crash, by contrast, occurred on a private holiday and was not done under the colour of authority. After he leaves office, Australia could exercise jurisdiction over that incident, which occurred on Australian territory; the victim's British nationality does not matter because territoriality alone suffices.

  **Conclusion**

  Australia may prescribe jurisdiction over Ms Kade for the hospital attack on the basis of objective territoriality, supported by active nationality and, as a subsidiary ground, passive personality; and for the counterfeiting on the protective principle and nationality. Tessaly's concurrent claim is also valid and should be resolved by consultation. The proposed seizure in Tessaly would be an unlawful exercise of enforcement jurisdiction (*Lotus*). Dr Voss cannot be arrested while he is foreign minister, because his personal immunity is complete (*Arrest Warrant*). After he leaves office, he could be prosecuted in Australia for the private jet-ski crash, but functional immunity would continue to cover his official approval of the attack.
