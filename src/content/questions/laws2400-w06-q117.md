  Explain how Tame v New South Wales; Annetts v Australian Stations changed the common law approach to
  psychiatric harm, and why the two cases reached opposite results despite applying the same test.
model: |
  Before Tame v New South Wales; Annetts v Australian Stations Pty Ltd, lower courts had treated three
  "control mechanisms" as preconditions for a duty of care in psychiatric harm cases: that the plaintiff be a
  person of normal fortitude, that the harm be caused by a sudden shock, and that the plaintiff directly
  perceive the distressing event or its immediate aftermath. Gummow and Kirby JJ held that none of these three
  mechanisms is in fact "a pre-condition to liability for negligently inflicted psychiatric harm." Instead, the
  central and only real inquiry is whether it was reasonably foreseeable that a person in the plaintiff's
  position would suffer a recognised psychiatric illness as a result of the defendant's conduct; normal
  fortitude, sudden shock and direct perception remain relevant to that foreseeability assessment, but do not
  operate as fixed gatekeeping requirements. The two cases, decided together, illustrate the test applied both
  ways rather than announcing a rule that always favours plaintiffs. In Tame, a police officer completing an
  accident report mistakenly recorded Mrs Tame's blood alcohol reading as 0.14 rather than nil; although the
  error was corrected within weeks and an apology given, her solicitor told her of it over a year later and
  she developed a psychotic depressive illness. The High Court held the officer owed her no duty of care,
  because it was not reasonably foreseeable that a person in her position would develop a recognised
  psychiatric illness from an error that had already been corrected and apologised for — her reaction was, in
  the Court's words, extreme and idiosyncratic. In Annetts, by contrast, the parents of a 16-year-old jackaroo
  had specifically sought and received an assurance from his employer that he would be constantly supervised;
  when he was instead sent to work alone, went missing, and died of dehydration in the desert, the Court held
  the employer did owe the parents a duty of care to avoid causing them psychiatric harm. Here, the
  pre-existing relationship between the parties — created by the parents' inquiries and the employer's
  assurance — made a psychiatric injury to the parents reasonably foreseeable if that promise were broken, and
  it also kept the class of possible plaintiffs determinate, addressing the indeterminate-liability concern
  that partly motivated the old control mechanisms. The difference in outcome therefore turns not on any
  difference in the legal test, but on the different facts bearing on foreseeability: an idiosyncratic reaction
  to a corrected clerical error in Tame, against a specific relationship and a broken promise of supervision in
  Annetts.
