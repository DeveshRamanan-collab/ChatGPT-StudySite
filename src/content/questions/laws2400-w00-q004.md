  **Issue**

  Whether Meridian Facilities Pty Ltd owed Farah a duty of care to repair the broken security gate, or otherwise to warn or protect her, given that her injury was directly caused not by Meridian's own conduct but by the independent criminal act of a third party who entered through the gate Meridian had failed to fix.

  **Rule**

  A case is "novel" when it falls outside an established category of relationship. Reasonable foreseeability alone is never enough for a novel duty; courts take an incremental approach, weighing the non-exhaustive "salient features" of the relationship between plaintiff and defendant (Caltex Refineries (Qld) Pty Ltd v Stavar (2009) 75 NSWLR 649). Relevant salient features include: foreseeability of harm; the defendant's degree and nature of control over the risk; the plaintiff's vulnerability and ability to self-protect; the plaintiff's reliance on the defendant; any assumption of responsibility by the defendant; the defendant's actual or constructive knowledge of the risk; and the risk of indeterminate liability and coherence with other legal principles.

  This case also engages the specific problem of omissions and third-party criminal conduct. Generally there is no duty to control the conduct of a third party who goes on to harm the plaintiff. Where physical harm is caused by an omission rather than a positive act, more than reasonable foreseeability is needed: Hargrave v Goldman (1963) 110 CLR 40 shows the extra ingredients are a special relationship, the defendant's knowledge of the danger, and their practical control over it. In Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre Pty Ltd v Anzil (2000) 205 CLR 254, the shopping centre controlled its car park lighting but had no control over the criminals who attacked Anzil there, so its duty as occupier — resting on "control over, and knowledge of, the state of the premises" — did not extend to their independent criminal conduct, and no duty was owed. Separately, courts remain cautious about recognising duties that risk indeterminate liability or sit incoherently with established principle (Sullivan v Moody (2001) 207 CLR 562).

  **Application**

  *Classifying the problem.* Farah's claim does not fall within any settled category: it concerns an omission (failing to fix the gate) causing harm through the intervening, independent criminal act of a stranger, rather than Meridian's own direct conduct. The salient features approach from Caltex Refineries therefore governs, and the case must be weighed on its own facts rather than resolved by simply reciting a rule.

  *Foreseeability and knowledge.* Unlike a case of merely generalised risk, Meridian had actual knowledge of the precise danger that materialised. At least six tenants had specifically complained that the gate's lock was broken, and one email expressly warned that "anyone could just walk in off the street," pointing to two recent break-ins in the same car park. This is a strong salient feature in favour of a duty: Meridian was not merely in a position where crime was a distant, general possibility, but had specific, repeated notice of a known security failure and a pattern of intrusions consistent with exactly the kind of harm that followed.

  *Control.* Applying Hargrave v Goldman's requirement of practical control over the source of danger, Meridian plainly had it: the broken lock was part of the building fabric Meridian was responsible for maintaining, and its manager had already promised a technician, showing Meridian itself recognised the gate was within its power and responsibility to fix. This distinguishes Meridian's position from a party with no ability to remove the danger at all.

  *Distinguishing Modbury Triangle.* Modbury is the obvious comparison, since both cases involve harm from a third party's independent crime in a car park the defendant partly controlled. But the facts are meaningfully different. In Modbury, the shopping centre controlled only the lighting, not the criminals, and there was nothing to suggest the centre had specific advance knowledge that an attack of that kind was likely; the risk of crime was a generalised background possibility. Here, by contrast, Meridian controlled the very means of entry the attacker used, had specific and repeated warnings that the broken gate created exactly this risk, and had already been told of two recent break-ins through the same vulnerability. Where Modbury's occupier had general control over premises but no particular reason to expect the specific harm, Meridian had both control over the risk's physical source and constructive (indeed actual) knowledge that a stranger could now simply walk in. This combination of control plus specific knowledge is precisely what was missing in Modbury and is capable of taking this case across the line that decision drew.

  *Vulnerability and reliance.* Farah, like other tenants, had no ability to fix the communal security gate herself; she depended entirely on Meridian, as managing agent, to maintain the building's security infrastructure she had no power over. The tenants' emails show they were actively relying on Meridian to respond, not merely hoping passively that nothing would go wrong — reinforcing both vulnerability and reliance as salient features supporting a duty.

  *Coherence and indeterminacy.* Recognising a duty here would not expose Meridian to indeterminate liability of the kind Sullivan v Moody cautions against: the class of potential claimants is confined and identifiable — the residents and lawful visitors of this one building — and the duty asserted (to act reasonably in response to known, specific security defects) is a modest, incremental extension consistent with an occupier's or managing agent's existing responsibility for the physical safety of premises it controls, not a general duty to prevent crime at large.

  **Conclusion**

  Weighing these salient features together — Meridian's actual and repeated knowledge of the precise defect, its practical control over the gate itself, Farah's vulnerability and reliance, and the absence of any indeterminate-liability concern — the balance favours a duty of care being owed by Meridian to Farah to take reasonable steps to repair the gate or otherwise warn tenants once it had specific notice of the danger. This case is distinguishable from Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre v Anzil, where the defendant had control but no specific knowledge of the very risk that eventuated; here Meridian had both. Meridian's argument that it owed no duty at all because it did not itself commit the assault should be rejected: the duty asserted is not a duty to prevent all crime, but a narrower, incremental duty grounded in Meridian's own control and specific knowledge, of the kind Caltex Refineries recognises can arise even for harm inflicted directly by a third party. Whether Meridian in fact breached that duty, and whether the breach caused Farah's loss, are separate questions to be addressed once duty is established.
