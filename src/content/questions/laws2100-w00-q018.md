model: |
  **Thesis**

  The first sentence of the statement is a fair summary of what outsider jurisprudence has found; the second does not follow from it. Feminist legal theory and critical race theory criticise a particular conception of rights — individual, formal, measured against a dominant benchmark and confined to the public sphere — while relying on rights as a means of naming and remedying injury. That is coherent, because the sceptical objections they draw on are objections to how rights have been defined and distributed, not to the idea that people have entitlements the law must respect. The charge of incoherence fits radical critical legal studies better than it fits outsider jurisprudence.

  **Argument**

  *The sceptical questions.* The week poses five questions against rights, and each targets something different. Is human nature a myth — is rights talk mere rhetoric, inflated until every entitlement is a right and the category loses its point? Are rights selfish, favouring the individual over the general interest, and neglecting duties? Are they an instrument of oppression, tied to a particular power structure? Are they a threat to community, centring individual autonomy? Are they ethnocentric, a Western creation? The first is a demand for discipline in using rights language; the second and fourth concern the individualism of rights; the third their relation to power; the fifth their origin.

  *Critical legal studies: rights as ideology.* Critical legal studies, drawing on realism, postmodernism and Marxism, attacks liberal legal values, including the idea of rights. Law is contradictory because it stems from political conflict, and it is an ideology: it promotes the interests of the powerful and legitimises injustice while presenting itself as neutral. On the Marxist account, the legal superstructure helps the dominant class maintain the relations of production; capitalism needs law that legitimises private ownership. Rights, on this view, are the alibi — formal guarantees that make domination look like freedom. This is the oppression objection in its strongest form.

  *Outsider jurisprudence: rights as targets.* Feminist legal theory and critical race theory share much of this critique. Both hold that law reflects and perpetuates a dominant perspective, and both question formal equality. Formal equality did not become substantive equality; it implicitly took male experience as the norm. Critical race theory says the same of colourblindness, meritocracy and equal opportunity, which can obscure structural racism, and of whiteness, which operates as a form of property conferring legal and social advantages. Feminist legal theory adds that challenging the (white) male norm means rethinking autonomy and individual rights, and 'the personal is the political' attacks the boundary that kept rights claims out of the family. Lacey's analysis of community brings in the communitarian worry: liberal theory treats individuals as autonomous agents, and the outsider asks whose autonomy, and whose community, the theory assumes.

  *Outsider jurisprudence: rights as tools.* Yet both families rely on rights. Critical race theory lists the importance of rights, especially for historically disadvantaged minorities, among its main themes. Naffine's account of feminist method — consciousness raising, naming and renaming the injuries done to women, and seeking their legal recognition — produced new legal categories: 'sexual harassment', 'date rape', 'domestic violence'. The practical achievements the lecture lists — the provocation defence tightened, the marital rape exception removed, the NSW consent reforms of 2021 — extend legal protection to people the law had left out.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the statement is that outsider jurisprudence wants the benefits of rights without paying the price of its own critique. If law upholds patriarchal institutions and racial hierarchies even when it appears neutral, new rights will be interpreted and enforced by the same institutions and absorbed by them. Moreton-Robinson shows the danger inside feminism itself: white feminists' rights demands left out Indigenous women's. Rights recognised in a text may remain paper rights. And the ethnocentrism objection applies to the outsider's own demands: if human rights are a Western creation, a strategy that relies on them extends the culture it criticises. On this view, an outsider who invokes rights has already accepted the insider's terms.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument identifies a real risk, but not an incoherence. Three points show why.

  First, criticising a conception is not rejecting a concept. The objections outsider jurisprudence uses — the benchmark, the public–private boundary, the exclusion of some groups' claims — are objections to who has counted as a rights-holder and what has counted as an injury. Moreton-Robinson's criticism is precisely that Indigenous women's rights were left out; it is a demand for more inclusive rights, not for fewer. The ethnocentrism worry meets the reply the lecture gave: we compare how different legal systems recognise rights, and that rights are expressed differently does not mean there is nothing common behind them. Even the community objection need not reject rights: a right to self-determination is a group right, and it is among the rights the human rights tradition already recognises.

  Secondly, outsider jurisprudence is openly normative. Critical race theory aims to transform law and society to achieve racial justice; feminist legal theory combines analysis with political engagement, theory with practice. A normative theory can consistently use the best tools available while criticising their limits, and it should be judged, as the lecture said, partly by whether it brought about change. On that test, the record of new categories and reforms counts in its favour.

  Thirdly, the incoherence charge bites harder on radical critical legal studies. If words have no stable meaning and the legal materials yield no coherent sense, then no rights claim is better than any other, and the critique of rights as ideology is as indeterminate as the rights it criticises. Outsider jurisprudence needs only the claim that law has been partial, not that it is meaningless. That is why it can say both that rights have served the insider and that they can be made to serve the outsider.

  So the first sentence of the statement should be accepted as a description of how rights have worked, and the second rejected. Outsider jurisprudence can coherently criticise liberal rights and rely on them, because what it criticises is whose rights and which injuries count, not whether rights matter.
