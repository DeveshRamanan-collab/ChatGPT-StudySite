model: |-
  Kelsen offers the most uncompromising version of the value-free ideal, but purity comes at a price that shows its
  limits. The pure theory is 'a theory of positive law; a general theory of law', which 'answers the question of what the
  law is, not what it ought to be. The latter question is one of politics, while the pure theory of law is science' (1941,
  44). It explains law as a coercive order that regulates its own creation, and validity as binding force traced through
  a chain of authorisation to the historically first constitution and a presupposed basic norm. Nothing moral enters: the
  tax officer differs from the gangster only by authorisation. The counterargument is that the theory depends on a
  presupposition the jurist must make — a 'juristic hypothesis', even a 'fiction' — and on choosing what counts as 'all the
  phenomena which go under the name of law'. And Hart avoided 'basic norm' partly to avoid Kelsen's view that law and
  morals cannot meaningfully conflict (CL 293). On balance Kelsen shows a theory can exclude moral evaluation of law, but
  not that it can avoid every theoretical choice.
