  For Hart, a rule is valid if it passes "all the tests provided by the rule of recognition" (CL 103), a social rule that exists because officials accept it as a common standard. In 'The Model of Rules I' Dworkin argued that this picture misses much of the law. When lawyers argue about legal rights and obligations they rely on principles, not only rules: standards observed "because [they are] a requirement of justice or fairness or some other dimension of morality". Principles have a dimension of weight; rules apply in an all-or-nothing fashion. And principles, Dworkin said, cannot be identified by their pedigree.

  His central example was *Riggs v Palmer*. Elmer Palmer murdered his grandfather to obtain the inheritance left to him under a valid will. The statutes on wills, literally construed, gave him the property; the majority of the New York Court of Appeals held that laws may be controlled in their operation by general, fundamental maxims of the common law, such as that no one shall profit by his own wrong.

  **Thesis**

  *Riggs v Palmer* shows that a rule of recognition understood as a pure pedigree test cannot identify all the law. But the second half of the statement is wrong. Inclusive positivism does not give up what made Hart's theory positivist, because on that view moral criteria count as law only where a social source — the accepted practice of officials — makes them count. The connection between law and morality stays contingent. What Hart does give up, or at least weaken, is the confident claim that judges in hard cases step outside the law and exercise strong discretion. Dworkin's argument hits the second of his three tenets harder than the first.

  **Argument**

  *Hart's picture.* Where a rule of recognition is accepted, officials and private persons have "authoritative criteria for identifying primary rules of obligation": an authoritative text, legislative enactment, customary practice, general declarations of specified persons, or past judicial decisions (CL 100). "To say that a given rule is valid is to recognize it as passing all the tests provided by the rule of recognition and so as a rule of the system" (CL 103). Validity is membership. The rule of recognition itself is neither valid nor invalid; it is "simply accepted as appropriate for use in this way" (CL 109).

  *Dworkin's three tenets.* Dworkin attributes to positivism three tenets: legal rules can be identified by their pedigree; in some cases judges must exercise discretion; and there is a legal obligation if and only if the case falls under a valid legal rule. Positivism is "a model of and for a system of rules", and its single fundamental test misses other standards (TRS 22).

  *Rules and principles.* A principle is a standard observed "because it is a requirement of justice or fairness or some other dimension of morality"; a policy sets out a goal. "The standard that automobile accidents are to be decreased is a policy, and the standard that no man may profit by his own wrong a principle" (TRS 22). The distinction is logical (TRS 24). A rule applies in an all-or-nothing fashion; a principle states a reason that argues in one direction and has a dimension of weight. Conflicting rules cannot both stand; conflicting principles are weighed.

  *Riggs.* The statutes, literally construed, gave Elmer the estate. The majority held that laws "may be controlled in their operation and effect by general, fundamental maxims of the common law"; the minority would have applied the statute, because the laws are silent and refusing Elmer would add a punishment. The majority treated the principle as binding, and it did so without asking who enacted it. That is Dworkin's point: principles cannot be identified by pedigree, so the principle's force here did not come from the kind of test a rule of recognition supplies. If such principles are law, the first tenet fails; discretion is only weak — judgment in applying standards — rather than strong, where the official "is simply not bound by standards" (TRS 32); and the third tenet fails, because an obligation "might be imposed by a constellation of principles as well as by an established rule" (TRS 44).

  *Hart's reply.* In the Postscript Hart restates his theory: "the existence and content of the law can be identified by reference to the social sources of law … without reference to morality except where the law thus identified has itself incorporated moral criteria for the identification of the law" (CL 269). On this inclusive or soft positivism, a legal system may make moral criteria necessary or sufficient for validity in that system only; a constitutional equality principle can invalidate a procedurally correct but discriminatory statute. Raz's exclusive or hard positivism denies that moral criteria can be either: once incorporated, they become legal criteria. Bix notes that this debate has been criticised as intramural, but it began because positivists recognised that Dworkin's criticisms had merit.

  **Counterargument**

  The statement's second half has real force. If morality can be a criterion of validity, then the answer to "what is the law?" can depend on a moral question, which is just what the separation thesis seemed to exclude. And the point of the rule of recognition, on Hart's own account, was to cure the uncertainty of a regime of primary rules. A rule of recognition that says "whatever justice requires" gives no more certainty than the regime it replaced. Inclusive positivism, the argument runs, keeps the positivist label while conceding Dworkin's substance.

  **Evaluation**

  The objection confuses a necessary connection with a contingent one. The separation thesis denies that it is a necessary truth that law satisfies the demands of morality (CL 185–186). Inclusive positivism holds that moral criteria bind judges in a particular system because, and only so far as, officials in that system accept them as criteria. That is still a social fact about the system. Hart could say without contradiction that another system, whose officials accept no such criteria, has law too. So the theory remains positivist in the sense that matters. The uncertainty objection is also weaker than it looks: Hart never claimed that the rule of recognition eliminates uncertainty, only that it supplies authoritative criteria, and a moral criterion that officials agree on still does that.

  Dworkin's argument does cost Hart something, but elsewhere. If the *Riggs* principle was already law, the judges were not filling a gap by legislating; they were applying existing standards. That weakens the claim that in hard cases judges exercise strong discretion. It also raises a harder question. The *Riggs* judges agreed on the statute and the facts yet disagreed about what the law required. If the rule of recognition exists because officials converge in accepting it, how can they disagree about its content? Inclusive positivism answers the pedigree objection; it does not obviously answer that one, which is why Dworkin's later work moves from principles to theoretical disagreement.

  The better conclusion is therefore that *Riggs* shows the limits of a pedigree test, that inclusive positivism is a legitimate positivist response rather than a surrender, and that the lasting damage of Dworkin's argument is to Hart's theory of discretion, not to the social sources thesis.
