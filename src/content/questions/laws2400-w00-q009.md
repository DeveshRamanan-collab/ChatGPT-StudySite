  **Issue**

  Whether DenRail owed a duty of care to James and, separately, to Mel, in respect of the psychiatric harm each suffered following the Denholm derailment, applying Part 3 of the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA").

  **Rule**

  Psychiatric harm was traditionally denied recovery; it is now compensable, but a plaintiff must show reasonable foreseeability that a person in their position would suffer a recognised psychiatric illness. Tame v New South Wales; Annetts v Australian Stations Pty Ltd (2002) 211 CLR 317 rejected rigid "control mechanisms" that lower courts had previously treated as preconditions — normal fortitude, "sudden shock," and "direct perception" of the event or its immediate aftermath — holding none is a precondition to liability, with reasonable foreseeability of a recognised psychiatric illness the only real test. In Tame, an idiosyncratic reaction to a corrected clerical error was not reasonably foreseeable; in Annetts, a pre-existing relationship (the parents' inquiries and the employer's assurance of supervision) made psychiatric harm to a jackaroo's parents reasonably foreseeable without sudden shock or direct perception.

  In New South Wales, Part 3 CLA has since modified this common law position. Section 27 divides "mental harm" into "pure" mental harm (standalone) and "consequential" mental harm (arising from another personal injury). Section 31 bars damages for pure mental harm unless it consists of a recognised psychiatric illness. Section 32 sets the duty test: a defendant does not owe a duty of care to avoid causing mental harm unless it ought to have foreseen that a person of normal fortitude might, in the circumstances, suffer a recognised psychiatric illness if reasonable care were not taken; s 32(2) directs attention to four circumstances, including whether the harm arose from sudden shock, whether the plaintiff witnessed a person being killed, injured or put in peril at the scene, the plaintiff's relationship to that person, and any pre-existing relationship between plaintiff and defendant. Section 32's "normal fortitude" standard is a fixed requirement, unlike Tame, where normal fortitude was treated only as one relevant consideration.

  Section 30 further limits recovery for pure mental harm arising from shock connected with another person (the "victim") being killed, injured or put in peril by the defendant's act or omission: the plaintiff may recover only if she witnessed, at the scene, the victim being killed, injured or put in peril, or is a close member of the victim's family. Wicks v State Rail Authority (NSW) (2010) 241 CLR 60 establishes that s 32 (duty) is applied first, with s 30(2) considered only once a duty is otherwise established, and reads "witnessed … being … put in peril" broadly: officers who spent hours assisting derailment victims were still "in peril" within s 30(2)(a), since not every such event begins and ends in an instant.

  **Application**

  *James.* James's PTSD is pure mental harm (not consequential on any physical injury of his own), and PTSD plainly satisfies s 31's recognised-illness requirement. Applying s 32, DenRail's admitted scheduling error made the derailment foreseeable, and it is readily foreseeable that a derailment of this severity, in a remote area forty minutes from help, would draw in a bystander who would witness passengers dying and injured at close range for hours — exactly the kind of event capable of causing psychiatric illness even to a person of normal fortitude. James witnessed, at the scene, passengers being killed, injured and put in peril, satisfying s 32(2)(b). Applying Wicks's broad reading of "witnessed … being … put in peril," his three-hour rescue effort does not fall outside the scene merely because it lasted hours rather than an instant — Wicks confirms an extended rescue can still qualify. A duty is owed under s 32.

  Following Wicks's ordering, s 30 is then considered: James satisfies s 30(2)(a) directly, having personally witnessed, at the scene, passengers being killed, injured and put in peril over that extended period. With duty, the s 31 illness threshold and the s 30 limitation all satisfied, James's claim in negligence should succeed, subject to breach and causation.

  *Mel.* Mel's position is considerably weaker and turns on a difficulty the materials leave genuinely open. Section 30 applies to harm arising from shock connected with a "victim" being killed, injured or put in peril by the defendant's conduct. Mel's harm did not arise from any passenger (the true victims of DenRail's error); it arose from imagining her brother James's ordeal, and James himself was an unharmed volunteer, not a person killed, injured or put in peril by DenRail. This casts real doubt on whether Mel's claim fits s 30's structure at all — she is not "a close member of the family" of any passenger-victim, and she did not witness any passenger being killed, injured or put in peril at the scene, arriving only after the site was made safe.

  Even putting s 30 aside, s 32 is harder to satisfy for Mel: she did not witness anyone imperilled at the scene, and had no pre-existing relationship with DenRail. Her illness developed gradually, from imagining events and media images, rather than sudden shock or direct exposure — factors s 32(2) weighs against foreseeability to a person of normal fortitude in her position. Annetts shows a pre-existing relationship can substitute for direct perception, but that relationship ran between the plaintiffs and the defendant itself; Mel had no relationship with DenRail at all, only with James, who was not himself a victim within the meaning s 30 and s 32 appear to require.

  **Conclusion**

  James has a strong claim: DenRail owed him a duty under s 32 given the foreseeable and severe nature of what he witnessed, and he satisfies s 30(2)(a) as a scene-witness applying Wicks's broad reading; with PTSD satisfying s 31, his claim should succeed subject to breach and causation. Mel's claim is likely to fail: she did not witness a passenger-victim imperilled at the scene, had no pre-existing relationship with DenRail, and her connection runs through James, who was not himself a "victim" of DenRail's negligence in the sense s 30 and s 32 require. This is the more genuinely contestable conclusion, given how far the materials' language could arguably be stretched to cover harm suffered in connection with a volunteer rescuer rather than a passenger directly imperilled — but on the words of ss 30 and 32, Mel's claim does not fit.
