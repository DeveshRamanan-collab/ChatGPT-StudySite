  The NSW Government, through its youth justice department ("the Department"), runs Ironbark Youth Centre, a secure residential facility for young offenders on the edge of the country town of Corran. Three weeks before the events below, a maintenance report recorded that the electronic lock on the exercise-yard gate "fails to engage about one time in five". A repair was booked for the following month. No padlock was fitted and no extra staff were rostered to watch the gate.

  One evening Riley and Jace, both 17 and detained for burglary offences, noticed that the gate had not locked and walked out. Staff noticed within twenty minutes and rang Corran police. The sergeant on duty had two officers on shift and a serious road crash to attend. She logged the escape and decided to send a car to the area once the crash had been dealt with, rather than search the town immediately.

  Within an hour of escaping, Riley and Jace smashed the front window of Ellie's hardware store, 400 metres from the centre, took tools and the keys to Ellie's ute, and drove the ute away. They abandoned it, badly damaged, on the highway an hour later. Ellie's store was closed for five days while the window was replaced, and she lost $7,000 in trade.

  Four days later and 300 kilometres away, Jace — still at large — broke into Nikhil's home in Dubbo and stole his laptop and car.

  Ellie and Nikhil each sue the State of NSW in negligence, both for the Department's conduct and for the police response. The State says that Riley and Jace, not the State, are responsible for what they chose to do.

  Advise Ellie and Nikhil.
model: |
  **Issue**

  Four questions arise: (1) whether the Department owed Ellie and Nikhil a duty of care in relation to the escapees' conduct, and how the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") Part 5 affects that; (2) whether any duty was breached; (3) whether the escapees' deliberate acts take the harm outside the scope of liability; and (4) whether the police owed either plaintiff a duty in responding to the escape.

  **Rule**

  A defendant may owe a duty for harm a third party causes the plaintiff only in certain circumstances — usually a pre-existing relationship or a situation of control. In Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co Ltd [1970] AC 1004 a duty was owed because damage on escape was foreseeable and the officers controlled the detainees, but for policy reasons (indeterminate liability) it was limited to damage in the vicinity immediately after the escape. In Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre v Anzil (2000) 205 CLR 254 there was no duty where the occupier had not created the risk of criminal attack and had no control over the attackers and no special relationship. For public authorities foreseeability is never enough; McHugh J in Crimmins v Stevedoring Industry Finance Committee (1999) 200 CLR 1 looked to control, knowledge, vulnerability, a specific class rather than the public at large, and whether "core policy making" was involved. CLA Part 5 then applies.

  Police owe no duty to individual members of the public in the investigation and suppression of crime (Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire [1989] AC 53), though this is not an immunity: operational conduct can attract a duty (Zalewski v Turcarolo [1994] Aust Torts Reports 81-280), and a power to intervene is not of itself a duty to exercise it (Stuart v Kirkland-Veenstra).

  Breach falls under s 5B. Under s 5D(1) causation needs factual causation and scope of liability; under s 5D(4) the court asks whether and why responsibility should be imposed, articulating the policy (Wallace v Kam) and the normative question (Ruddock v Taylor). A new intervening act ordinarily severs the chain if it is voluntary human action or an extremely unlikely coincidence (Haber v Walker); volitional conduct severed the chain in Rickards v Lothian, Yates v Jones and CES v Superclinics (Aust) Pty Ltd.

  **Application**

  *Duty to Ellie.* Unlike the occupier in Modbury, which had no control over the attackers, the Department controlled the third parties themselves — it was detaining them — which is the edge on which Dorset Yacht turned. Damage to nearby property by detainees held for burglary, if they escaped, was plainly foreseeable, and the Department knew from its own report that the gate failed one time in five. Ellie, a property owner 400 metres away, is within a specific class — those in the immediate vicinity of the centre — not the public at large, and she could do nothing to guard against an escape. The complaint concerns a known faulty lock, not core policy making. A duty is owed to Ellie.

  *Duty to Nikhil.* Dorset Yacht itself marks the limit: for reasons of indeterminate liability, no duty was owed for damage done while the escapees remained at large. Nikhil's loss occurred four days later and 300 kilometres away. Extending the duty to him would expose the State to liability for anything an escapee does anywhere for as long as he is free. No duty is owed to Nikhil.

  *CLA Part 5.* The Department is a public authority (s 41). Section 42(a) and (b) protect its general allocation of resources, including booking the full repair for next month; but a padlock or a staff member at one gate is a modest interim step. The State will argue that securing detainees is part of the exercise of a special statutory power — detaining young people is something persons generally cannot do without statutory authority — so that s 43A applies and liability needs conduct so unreasonable that no authority could properly consider it reasonable. Ellie will answer, as in RTA v Refrigerated Roadways, that the relevant precaution is not a special statutory power at all: anybody can fit a padlock to a gate. Even if s 43A applies, leaving the gate of a secure facility with a known one-in-five failure rate for a month, with no interim measure, is arguably conduct no reasonable authority could consider reasonable. Section 46 is no answer, because the duty rests on control and knowledge, not on the bare exercise of a function.

  *Breach.* Under s 5B the risk of escape through a gate known to fail one time in five was foreseeable and not insignificant. The probability of escape was real and recurring, the likely harm to nearby people and property serious, the burden of a padlock or a staff member slight, and the social utility of detention is served, not reduced, by securing the gate. A reasonable authority would have taken those precautions; the Department breached its duty.

  *Causation and scope — Ellie.* But for the faulty lock, Riley and Jace would not have walked out, so the breach was a necessary condition of Ellie's loss (s 5D(1)(a)). The State's real argument is scope of liability: the break-in was deliberate, voluntary human action, the kind of intervening act that ordinarily severs the chain (Haber v Walker; Rickards v Lothian; Yates v Jones; CES). Under s 5D(4), however, the court must articulate whether and why responsibility should be imposed (Wallace v Kam; Ruddock v Taylor). The duty exists precisely because of the risk that detainees will damage nearby property if they escape; it would make the duty empty if the very conduct it guards against took the harm outside its scope. Within the Dorset Yacht limits, responsibility should be imposed. That covers the window, the tools and the five days' lost trade, which is consequential on the damage to her property rather than pure economic loss. The State may say the ute, damaged on the highway an hour later, falls outside "the vicinity immediately after the escape", but it was taken from her store within the hour, as part of the same episode.

  *The police.* The sergeant's decision to send her two officers to a serious crash before searching for the escapees concerns the investigation and suppression of crime and the allocation of scarce resources — the core of Hill, whose reasons (conflicting responsibilities, resources, defensive policing) apply directly, reinforced by s 42. Zalewski concerned how police conducted an operation, not a decision whether and when to begin one. Stuart v Kirkland-Veenstra and s 46 confirm that the power to search did not create a duty to exercise it. The police owed no duty.

  **Conclusion**

  Ellie is likely to succeed against the State for the Department's negligence: as in Dorset Yacht, the Department controlled the escapees and knew of the faulty lock; Part 5 does not protect its failure to take a cheap interim precaution; and the escapees' deliberate acts, being the very risk the duty guarded against, do not take her nearby, immediate losses outside the scope of liability. Nikhil's claim fails: his loss, days later and far away, lies outside the Dorset Yacht limit. Neither plaintiff has a claim for the police response, which falls within Hill.
