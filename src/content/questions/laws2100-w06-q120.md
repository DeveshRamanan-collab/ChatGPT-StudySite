The charge, with the view that realism and positivism are incompatible, traces to Hart's chapter VII, which treated realism as a rival theory of law. On that reading realism looks absurd: if law is only what courts will do, no court could err, and without rules we could not identify a judge. Leiter replies that realism is a descriptive theory of adjudication. Judges react primarily to the underlying facts; decisions fall into discernible patterns (situation types) that permit prediction; since law is rationally indeterminate, rules cannot explain decisions, so we must find the real reasons. That is empirical rule scepticism, and Hart has no argument against it. Indeed the realists cannot be conceptual rule sceptics, since their arguments for indeterminacy presuppose a nonsceptical account of the criteria of legality close to hard or exclusive positivism (293). What is left is an empirical disagreement about how often rules in books play a causal role. A critic may still say the early realists' methodology was unsophisticated, but that is a reason for better empirical work, not a joke."
