model: |-
  Hart's account is better because it explains a difference the command model cannot see. Both theories start from the
  correct appreciation that where there is law, conduct is made non-optional (CL 82). But the gunman and the tax officer
  look the same factually: someone with power demands money and threatens consequences. Hart argues there is a
  difference between being obliged and having an obligation, and explains it through social rules. Unlike habits, rules
  attract criticism when broken, are treated as good reasons for that criticism, and have an internal aspect — a critical
  reflective attitude to a pattern as a common standard (CL 57). That is why a tax evader is still under an obligation.
  The strongest counterargument is Austin's: at the end of the day we stop at the light to avoid a fine, and the realists
  add that obligation is a prediction of consequences. But these accounts miss how participants use rules as reasons to
  criticise and to act. On balance Hart's is the better account on the command theory's own terms: it explains the
  normative force of law, not merely its power.
