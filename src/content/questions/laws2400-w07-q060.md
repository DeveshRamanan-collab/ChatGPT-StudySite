model: |-
  The issue is whether the council owed a duty of care to avoid pure economic loss caused by negligent words. The
  loss is PEL: the block lost value with no physical damage. Reasonable foreseeability is necessary but not sufficient;
  the cases require features showing a special relationship. Shaddock & Associates v Parramatta City Council is closely
  analogous: a council told an intending purchaser, by phone and then by certificate, that no road-widening proposal
  affected the land, and a duty was found. The duty covers information as well as advice, and there is no separate
  requirement that the speaker possess special skill. Here, as in Shaddock, the council was the only source of the
  information about its own proposals; it knew the inquiry came from a prospective purchaser, so it knew or ought to
  have known of the reliance; and the developer's reliance on a formal certificate was reasonable. The court in
  Shaddock suggested the phone call alone might not have been enough; here, as there, the certificate confirms it.
  This is unlike Tepko, where a reluctant ball-park estimate was not reasonably relied on, and unlike San Sebastian,
  where reliance on a statement of intention was unreasonable — here the statement was one of existing fact. Being a
  public authority does not prevent a duty. Conclusion: the council very likely owed Harbourside a duty of care.
