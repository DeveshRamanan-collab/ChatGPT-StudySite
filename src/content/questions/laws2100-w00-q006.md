  *Lex iniusta non est lex* — an unjust law is not a law — is the slogan most often associated with natural law theory. Hart read natural law as the contention that there is a connection between law and morality which is in some sense necessary, and in its Thomist version as the claim that man-made laws conflicting with principles of true morality or justice are not valid law. Against it he set the positivist view that a rule's violating morality does not show that it is not law, and he wanted to be able to say that a law may be valid and yet too iniquitous to be obeyed.

  Finnis argued that this misunderstands the tradition. 'The obligation to obey the law' has several senses: liability to sanction, legal obligation in the intra-systemic sense, legal obligation in the moral sense, and moral obligation that does not derive from the law at all. Unjust laws, on Finnis's view, are not laws in the central case or focal meaning of the term — as an unfaithful friend is not properly a friend — but they are not for that reason legally invalid. Others, such as Radbruch, have held that only extremely unjust "laws" are not legally valid.

  Critically evaluate the following statement:

  "'An unjust law is not a law' is either false or trivial: false if it means that unjust laws are invalid, trivial if it means only that they are defective."

  **Thesis**

  The first horn of the dilemma holds; the second does not. Read as a claim that every unjust law is legally invalid, *lex iniusta non est lex* is false, or at least carries costs that Hart identifies. But Finnis's weak thesis is not trivial. It says that the concept of law should be understood through its central case, which is law that gives its subjects genuine reasons to comply; and that is a substantive claim about legal theory which a positivist rejects. The dilemma therefore fails, because its second horn misdescribes what the weak thesis claims.

  **Argument**

  *The strong thesis.* The strong natural law thesis says that moral soundness is a necessary element of legal validity, so an unjust "law" is not legally valid. It is a claim about each and every unjust law. Hart's objection starts from his account of validity: "To say that a given rule is valid is to recognize it as passing all the tests provided by the rule of recognition and so as a rule of the system" (CL 103). Whether a rule passes those tests is a question of social fact. The utilitarian tradition asserted "two simple things": a rule's violating morality does not show it is not law, and its being morally desirable does not show it is law (599). And Hart chose "rule of recognition" over "basic norm" partly "to avoid any commitment to Kelsen's view of the conflict between law and morals" (CL 293). For Kelsen, a legal and a moral norm cannot both be valid at once, so no meaningful conflict is possible. The strong thesis ends up in the same place from the other side: if unjust laws are not law, there can be no conflict between law and morality either. Hart wants a vocabulary in which one can say "this is law, but it is too iniquitous to obey". The strong thesis removes that sentence, and with it an honest description of what officials and citizens of wicked regimes faced. So the first horn holds.

  *Radbruch's limited version.* A narrower strong thesis says only extremely unjust laws are invalid. It is less implausible, but it inherits the same cost at the extreme, and it needs a line between very unjust and extremely unjust that the thesis itself does not supply.

  *The weak thesis is not a platitude.* Finnis does not say unjust laws are invalid. He distinguishes four senses of the obligation to obey the law: empirical liability to sanction; legal obligation in the intra-systemic sense; legal obligation in the moral sense; and moral obligation that does not derive from legality. An unjust law may create an obligation in the first two senses and fail in the third. His claim is that such a law is not law in the central case or focal meaning, as an unfaithful friend is not properly a friend and a fallacious argument is not properly an argument. We still use the word, but we measure the instance against the central case, and the more unjust a system is, the more peripheral it is to that case. In the central case, law's authority "depends … on its justice or at least its ability to secure justice" (NLNR 260).

  Why is this not trivial? Because it is a claim about how a theory of law should be built. Finnis's point in *Natural Law and Natural Rights* is that no theorist can describe social facts "without also participating in the work of evaluation" (NLNR 3). Hart himself uses a central case — the union of primary and secondary rules — but treats the internal point of view as an amalgam of viewpoints, including "calculations of long-term interest" and "the mere wish to do as others do" (CL 203). Finnis says the central case of the internal point of view is the practically reasonable person. That is a disagreement about which case is central, and it changes what counts as the paradigm of law.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest reply is that the weak thesis, once separated from invalidity, collapses into something a positivist already says. Hart accepts that laws may be morally defective, that morality provides grounds for criticising law, and that there may be moral reasons to disobey. His separation thesis denies only that it is a necessary truth that law satisfies morality (CL 185–186). If Finnis agrees that an unjust law is legally valid, then saying it is "not law in the focal sense" is a stipulation about the word "law", adding a label to a conclusion both sides share. Fuller's demand that Hart adopt a definition of law "that will make meaningful the obligation to fidelity to law" (635) looks, on this reading, like the same move: a proposal about how to use the word, not a discovery about law.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument would succeed if the weak thesis were only about labels. It is not. Finnis's four senses of obligation show what is at stake: the positivist answers the question "is it law?" at the level of intra-systemic validity and treats the moral question as separate; Finnis says the moral sense is the one that explains why law matters, and so belongs in the account of what law is. That is the real disagreement between the methodological sense of positivism — legal theory as a morally neutral, descriptive enterprise — and natural law. A thesis that picks out the ground of a whole methodological dispute is not trivial.

  Fuller's distinction "between order and good order" (644) strengthens the point. The weak thesis tells us that a regime of order without good order is a degenerate instance of law, and gives us terms in which to say how and why it is degenerate. The positivist can criticise such a regime, but only from outside the concept of law.

  So the statement's dilemma should be rejected. The strong thesis is false, or at least too costly, for the reasons Hart gives. The weak thesis is true or false, but it is not trivial: it is a substantive claim about the central case of law and about whether a theory of law can be value-free. Attacking the strong thesis does not touch Finnis.
