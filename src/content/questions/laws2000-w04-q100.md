model: |
  Acquisition of territory is a state gaining sovereign title over land. There are five modes. Cession is a
  transfer from one state to another, often by purchase treaty (Alaska from Russia, 1867); the acquirer cannot
  obtain more rights than the ceding state had and must respect third states' and indigenous rights. Accretion is
  new land formed gradually by nature (the 1986 volcanic island in Japan's territorial sea). Only naturally formed
  land counts: UNCLOS art 121(1) defines an island as "a naturally formed area of land, surrounded by water, which
  is above water at high tide", and art 60(8) denies artificial islands island status, as the 2016 South China Sea
  arbitration confirmed. Occupation of terra nullius requires effective control and an intention to claim title
  over land never under any state's sovereignty; it is barely applicable today. Prescription passes title over
  another state's territory through peaceful control, acting as sovereign, on implied consent — sustained protest
  defeats it.

  Conquest was once accepted, but since the UN Charter 1945 territory cannot be acquired by force: art 2(4)
  prohibits force against territorial integrity. Occupation by force now triggers the belligerent occupation
  regime; the occupier's presence is temporary and sovereignty does not transfer (Iraq/Kuwait 1990, Crimea 2014).
