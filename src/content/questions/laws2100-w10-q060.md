model: "The offence principle, properly limited, does not hand the state too much power, because Feinberg's two tests filter out most unpopular conduct; the risk lies in how loosely they are applied. Feinberg holds that it is \"always a good reason\" for a criminal prohibition that it would probably be an effective and necessary way of preventing serious offence to persons other than the actor. It is a supplement to Mill, answering the criticism that harm does not reach far enough. The worry is real: offence has a greater reach than harm, and unpopular conduct offends many people. But two tests must be met. Universality requires a reaction expected from almost anyone chosen at random, regardless of sex, age or race, so group-specific or merely majority disgust fails. Reasonable avoidability requires that the offended person cannot reasonably avoid the conduct, so private or signed-off conduct fails. A critic may reply that \"almost anyone\" and \"inconvenient\" are elastic. On balance the principle is defensible because it targets unavoidable public encounters, not the moral status of conduct, which keeps it distinct from Devlin's moralism."
