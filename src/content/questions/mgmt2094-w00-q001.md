model: |
  **Thesis**

  The board member is right about where the value lies and wrong about what follows from it. For a company, creativity pays only when it is turned into innovation: the economic value sits in introducing something new or improved, not in imagining it. But creativity that is not commercialised is not simply "wasted effort", and the proposal to fund only implementation misunderstands the order of the process. Creativity precedes innovation; an organisation that stops funding ideas will soon have nothing worth implementing. A year in which hundreds of ideas produced one product points to a conversion problem, not a creativity surplus.

  **Argument**

  *Three terms, kept apart.* Levitt's distinction is the starting point: "Creativity is thinking up new things. Innovation is doing new things." The Week 7 chart adds that creativity is the use of imagination or original ideas, cannot be measured and precedes innovation; innovation is the introduction of new or improved goods, services or processes, is related to implementation, can be measured and follows creativity. Invention sits between them: a novel product, device, process or concept, such as the printing press or the telephone. Only innovation is tied to the market — a newer and better solution that meets new requirements or existing market needs, like the introduction of the iPhone, the tablet or the flat-screen TV. Week 8 compresses this into a formula: innovation = invention + commercialisation. If you cannot commercialise it, it is creativity, not innovation.

  *What the statement gets right.* On these definitions the board member is right that ideas alone create no economic value for the firm. Dow Corning shows the point at scale. It spent 10% of revenue on R&D and had analytical capabilities among the best in the world, yet its New Product Market Share — the percentage of revenue from newly commercialised products — fell from around 25% in the 1970s and 80s to 13% by 1995. The bottleneck was commercialisation. The three circles make the same point: the jetpack has long been desirable, but an idea must also be possible with technology and viable in the marketplace before it is an innovation.

  *Why ideas must still be funded.* The statement's second sentence does not follow, because creativity is the input that innovation selects from. Creative problem solving runs from identifying an interesting problem, through divergent thinking (many solutions), to convergent thinking (the solution). The diverging pattern asks a team to defer judgement and look for lots of ideas, and Brown and Wyatt quote Linus Pauling: "To have a good idea you must first have lots of ideas." Hundreds of ideas for one product is therefore not proof of waste; a funnel is supposed to narrow. Disney shows both columns working together. Imagineering's Dish, a virtual reality chamber for testing future rides, is "blue sky" experimentation whose technology is still too expensive and cumbersome for the parks: thought up, not yet done. MagicBands, RFID wristbands for park entry, hotel rooms and payment, were rolled out at Disney World through a gradual, iterative process, with more than 9 million given out: done. Disney funds both, and the semester's own path — vision, imagination, creativity, innovation — treats them as stages of one process.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the board member is that unconverted ideas have real costs, and that only innovation can be measured. Disney's interactive division was in the red for five years and lost more than $1 billion. Lego is investing about 1 billion kroner and has tried around 200 alternatives to its plastic, most of which have fallen short, and its chief executive said it was difficult to know the financial implications. A board that can measure innovation but not creativity might reasonably fund only what it can count.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument shows that creativity must be managed, not that it should be starved. Iger's verdict on the interactive division was "we learned from that", alongside his rule that "if you're going to win big, you have to get in early". Lego's search serves a clear goal of innovation, reducing environmental damage while keeping the brick the same, and its experiments are the creative stage of that project. What the firm in the scenario lacks is the conversion stage. Convergent thinking — identifying benefits and risks, keeping the best and eliminating wrong answers — has to follow divergence. Dow Corning's answer was a commercialisation process, not less science: Design for Six Sigma, whose pilot, the ArmorAll Deck Protector, produced over 4,500 tonnes and US$40 million of revenue in 12 months.

  So the statement should be qualified. Creativity that never becomes innovation earns a company nothing, and a board that counts ideas is counting the wrong thing. But creativity precedes innovation, and most of it is meant to be discarded on the way. The board should fund implementation more — and keep funding the ideas that implementation depends on.
