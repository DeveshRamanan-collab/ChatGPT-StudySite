  What does it mean to be a subject of international law? Explain, with reference to Reparation for Injuries,
  how the legal personality of an international organisation differs from that of a state.
model: |
  A subject of international law is "an entity possessing international rights and obligations and having the
  capacity (1) to maintain its rights by bringing international claims; and (2) to be responsible for its
  breaches of obligation by being subjected to such claims" (Crawford). Asking who is a subject is asking who has
  international legal personality. Capacities ascribed to subjecthood include claiming rights, entering diplomatic
  relations, bearing obligations imposed directly by international law, creating law (e.g. binding treaties) and
  enjoying immunities from other states' jurisdiction.

  States are the only subjects with full legal capacity. Other subjects have partial capacity. In Reparation for
  Injuries (1949) the ICJ held that the UN, although its Charter is silent, is an international person, because
  it was intended to exercise functions and rights that can only be explained by a large measure of
  international personality. But the Court stressed this "is not the same thing as saying that it is a State" or
  that its rights and duties are the same as a state's: subjects "are not necessarily identical in their nature
  or in the extent of their rights". An IO's capacity is therefore functional — limited to what its members gave
  it — and it has no general competence, whereas a state's capacity is comprehensive.
