  Advise Mrs Nguyen whether Bayside breached its duty of care. Do not consider causation, damages or defences.

model: |
  **Issue**

  Duty and Bayside's responsibility for Jett are admitted. The only question is breach: whether, in restocking the top racks during trading hours with the aisle open, and in Jett continuing to lift while Mrs Nguyen was in the aisle, Bayside failed to take precautions required by the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") s 5B, read with s 5C.

  **Rule**

  The duty is a duty to take reasonable care, not an absolute duty to avoid injury: the measure is reasonableness, not elimination of risk (Swain v Waverley Municipal Council (2005) 220 CLR 517). The standard is objective — a reasonable person in the defendant's position.

  Under s 5B(1), a person is not negligent in failing to take precautions against a risk of harm unless (a) the risk was foreseeable (one the person knew or ought to have known of), (b) the risk was not insignificant, and (c) in the circumstances a reasonable person in the person's position would have taken those precautions. The elements are joined by "and" and each must be separately addressed (Benic v NSW [2010] NSWSC 1039). First, the risk of harm must be identified: it is sufficient to describe it "as a class of injury, as distinct from the particular injury actually suffered by the plaintiff" (Benic), but it must be specific enough to correspond to the precautions in issue. In RTA v Dederer (2007) 234 CLR 330 Gummow J said the Court of Appeal's framing "obscured the true source of potential injury".

  A risk that is not far-fetched or fanciful is foreseeable (Wyong Shire Council v Shirt (1980) 146 CLR 40). "Not insignificant" indicates a higher probability than that (Ipp Report), depending on obviousness, likelihood of occurrence and seriousness of consequence (Rail Corporation NSW v Donald [2018] NSWCA 82). Under s 5B(2) the court considers, amongst other things, the probability of harm, the likely seriousness of the harm, the burden of precautions and the social utility of the activity. Section 5C adds that (a) the burden includes precautions against similar risks; (b) the fact that a risk could have been avoided by doing something in a different way does not of itself give rise to liability; and (c) later action that would have avoided the risk does not of itself give rise to liability or constitute an admission.

  **Application**

  *The risk of harm.* The risk is of customers being struck by heavy goods falling from the top racks while they are restocked by forklift over an aisle open to the public. That is a class of injury tied to its source. Framing it as "a fractured shoulder from a cement bag" would be too narrow; framing it as "injury in a hardware store" would be so broad that it covers scenarios needing quite different responses, the error identified in Dederer. Framed correctly, it points straight to the precaution in issue: keeping customers out of the aisle while the lifting happens.

  *Section 5B(1)(a) — foreseeable.* Bayside knew of this risk. Its own manual identifies it and prescribes the precaution, and its incident log records two bags slipping off pallets in six months. A risk the defendant has written down, and has seen happen twice, is far from far-fetched or fanciful (Wyong).

  *Section 5B(1)(b) — not insignificant.* This needs its own analysis, because the threshold is higher. On the Donald factors, the risk is obvious: twenty-kilogram bags are handled four metres above an open aisle. It is reasonably likely: two slips in six months show that bags do fall during trading-hours restocking. And the consequences are serious: a falling bag can cause head and spinal injuries. Bayside's first argument, that no one was hurt in five years, does not help it. Probability is judged prospectively, from the position before the accident (Dederer). Most of those five years came before the change to trading-hours restocking, and the two slips since show the risk was real, not remote. It was not insignificant.

  *Section 5B(1)(c) and (2) — the reasonable response.* The precaution Mrs Nguyen relies on is simple: restock before opening, or close both ends of the aisle while lifting — and, on the day, stop lifting once a customer came in. Weighing the s 5B(2) considerations together:

  - Probability: with the aisle open on a busy Saturday, a customer walking under a lift was likely, and slips were already happening.
  - Seriousness: a heavy bag falling from four metres risks grave injury, and the standard rises with known vulnerability. As in Paris v Stepney Borough Council, where the employer knew its worker had only one good eye, Jett saw that Mrs Nguyen was elderly and slow on a walking frame and could not quickly get clear.
  - Burden: low. Restocking before opening costs some overtime; closing the aisle costs minutes; stopping the lift costs nothing. This is not Woods v Multi-Sport Holdings, where suitable helmets did not exist — the precaution is Bayside's own written procedure.
  - Social utility: selling hardware is lawful and useful, but none of that utility depends on lifting pallets over shoppers. Unlike E v Australian Red Cross Society, where testing would have cost about 5% of all blood donations, the precaution here takes nothing from the activity.

  *Section 5C(a) and the $250,000.* Bayside is right that the burden includes precautions against similar risks across its business. But the precaution need not be gates in every aisle of every store: restocking before opening, or closing an aisle with what staff already have, deals with the similar risks too, at modest cost. Even if gates were needed, $250,000 across 30 stores is not out of proportion to the risk of serious injury to customers.

  *Section 5C(b) and lower shelving.* The fact that the risk could have been avoided by storing bags lower does not of itself give rise to liability, so Mrs Nguyen should not rest her case on that alternative. She does not need to. Her case is that, having chosen to store heavy bags high, Bayside failed to take the precautions for lifting them that a reasonable operator — and its own manual — required.

  *Section 5C(c) and the new gates.* Buying gates after the accident does not of itself give rise to liability and is not of itself an admission. Mrs Nguyen must prove breach without relying on it, which, on the analysis above, she can.

  *Jett's inexperience.* The standard is objective and impersonal. A forklift operator in his second week owes the same standard as an experienced one, just as a learner driver owes the same standard as a licensed driver (Imbree v McNeilly). That Jett was doing his best is no answer: a reasonable operator, seeing a frail customer enter the aisle, would have stopped lifting.

  **Conclusion**

  Bayside was not obliged to eliminate every risk in its store (Swain). But the risk of goods falling on customers during trading-hours restocking was foreseeable and not insignificant, and a reasonable store operator would have restocked before opening or closed the aisle, and at the least would have stopped lifting once Mrs Nguyen came in. None of Bayside's arguments changes that. Bayside breached its duty of care to Mrs Nguyen.
