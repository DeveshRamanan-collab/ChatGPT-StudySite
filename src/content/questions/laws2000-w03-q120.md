  Explain the difference between relative and absolute invalidity under the VCLT, and why an internal-law
  violation is only rarely a successful ground for invalidity.
model: |
  The VCLT splits invalidity into two categories. Relative invalidity concerns defects in a state's consent:
  error (art 48) — a material factual mistake, like an erroneous map, important to the decision to join;
  fraud (art 49) — deliberately misleading a representative into signing; and corruption of a representative
  (art 50) — an improper advantage inducing a representative to sign who otherwise would not have. Absolute
  invalidity is more serious and makes a treaty void ab initio: coercion of a representative (art 51) —
  economic pressure, blackmail or a threat to their life — and, more seriously still, threat or use of force
  against the state itself (art 52), as well as conflict with a peremptory norm of general international law
  (jus cogens, art 53), such as the prohibitions on torture or genocide, which must exist at the time the
  treaty was concluded. Violating a state's own internal law on treaty-making competence is deliberately not
  a ground for invalidity in the ordinary case: art 46, applied in *Cameroon v Nigeria*, says a state cannot
  invoke this unless the violation was "manifest and concerned a rule of its internal law of fundamental
  importance". This protects the other party's reasonable reliance on an apparently validly concluded treaty
  — a mere change of government disagreeing with its predecessor's choices will not meet that high bar.
