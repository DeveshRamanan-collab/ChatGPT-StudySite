prompt: Did the judges in Riggs v Palmer exercise strong discretion? Answer using Dworkin's account of discretion.
model: No, on Dworkin's account they exercised at most weak discretion. Discretion, like the hole in a doughnut, exists only as an area left open by a surrounding belt of restriction. Weak discretion means the standards demand judgment, or that an official has final authority; strong discretion means the official is simply not bound by standards on the issue. In Riggs the statutes, literally construed, gave the property to the murderer, but the majority relied on a canon of construction and on fundamental maxims of the common law — no one shall profit by his own wrong. These principles were found within positive law, so the judges were bound by standards and needed judgment in weighing them. The positivist counterargument is that the maxims are extra-legal, so the judges reached beyond the law and made a new rule, creating an obligation that did not exist before. On balance the judgment's own language — maxims of the common law — supports Dworkin, although critics say he cherry-picks such cases.
