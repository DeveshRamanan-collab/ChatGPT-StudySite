  **Issue**

  Whether Tegan can establish that Coastal Shire Council, as occupier of the Sundown Point reserve, owed and breached a duty of care to fence the unstable cliff edge or to provide a more specific warning than the sign it displayed, given that the danger of loose, crumbling ground at the very edge of an unfenced cliff was visually apparent to anyone who approached it.

  **Rule**

  Negligence requires the plaintiff to prove three elements: that the defendant owed a duty of care; that the defendant breached that duty; and that the breach caused compensable harm. An occupier of land owes entrants a duty to take reasonable care against foreseeable risks of injury, but Romeo v Conservation Commission (NT) (1998) 192 CLR 431 shows this duty is "not owed in the abstract in practice" — the critical question in most occupiers' cases is not whether a duty exists at all, but its scope. In Romeo, the plaintiff fell from an unfenced cliff in a reserve the Commission managed; the High Court held the duty did not extend to fencing the coastline, because the danger of an unfenced cliff edge was assessed "according to its obviousness and the care ordinarily exercised by the public," and there is no duty to warn of, or guard against, an obvious risk — only a concealed one.

  The Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") now codifies this approach. Section 5F defines an "obvious risk" objectively: a risk that would have been obvious to a reasonable person in the position of the plaintiff, including risks that are patent or a matter of common knowledge, and a risk can be obvious even though it has a low probability of occurring or is not prominent, conspicuous or physically observable. Section 5G presumes that a plaintiff who suffers harm from an obvious risk was aware of that risk, unless she proves on the balance of probabilities that she was not — and a person is taken to be aware of a risk if aware of its type or kind, even without knowing its precise nature or extent. Section 5H then removes any proactive duty to warn of an obvious risk altogether, subject to three exceptions: the plaintiff requested advice or information about the risk, a written law required a warning, or the defendant is a professional and the risk is one of death or personal injury arising from a professional service.

  **Application**

  *Duty and its scope.* Coastal Shire Council, as occupier of the reserve, owed entrants including Tegan a general duty to take reasonable care against foreseeable risks of injury; that much is uncontroversial and follows directly from its position as occupier. The real question, as Romeo emphasises, is the scope of that duty — specifically, whether it extended to fencing the cliff edge or providing a more specific warning about unstable ground. The facts here are strikingly close to Romeo itself: an unfenced natural cliff edge in a reserve managed by a public authority, where the plaintiff was injured after leaving a formed area and approaching the edge directly. Romeo's reasoning — that the danger of an unfenced clifftop drop is assessed according to its obviousness rather than treated as requiring universal fencing — applies with equal force here.

  *Obvious risk under s 5F.* The danger Tegan encountered — loose gravel and crumbling limestone at the very edge of an unfenced forty-metre cliff — is a paradigm obvious risk. Section 5F asks whether the risk would have been obvious to a reasonable person in Tegan's position; a reasonable adult approaching the unfenced edge of a high cliff, where visible loose gravel and crumbling rock could be seen along the last few metres before the drop, would appreciate that stepping onto that ground carries an obvious risk of losing footing or having the ground give way. Section 5F(4) makes clear a risk can be obvious even if it is "not prominent, conspicuous or physically observable" in every particular — so even if Tegan says she did not personally notice or fully appreciate the instability, the test is objective, not dependent on her own perception or her limited prior experience with cliff-top locations. The general danger of an unfenced cliff edge with visibly loose ground is exactly the kind of risk s 5F treats as obvious, regardless of the precise mechanism (gravel giving way rather than, say, a sudden gust of wind) by which the fall actually occurred.

  *The s 5G presumption.* Once the risk is characterised as obvious, s 5G presumes Tegan was aware of it unless she proves otherwise on the balance of probabilities. Her evidence that she had never visited a cliff-top lookout before and did not personally appreciate how unstable the very edge could be goes to her subjective appreciation, but s 5G(2) only requires awareness of the type or kind of risk, not its precise nature or extent — and the type of risk (that ground can give way at an unfenced, unstable cliff edge) is a matter of common knowledge within the meaning of s 5F(2). Tegan's unfamiliarity with this specific location does not, without more, rebut the statutory presumption that she was aware of this general and widely understood category of risk.

  *No duty to warn under s 5H.* Even if Tegan could rebut the s 5G presumption, s 5H independently removes any duty to warn of an obvious risk, subject to three exceptions, none of which applies here: Tegan did not request advice or information about the risk from the council before approaching the edge; no written law is identified requiring the specific warning Tegan says should have been given; and the council is a public land manager providing a passive recreational reserve, not a professional providing a "professional service" of the kind s 5H(2)(c) contemplates. The existing sign — general as it was — was not required at all once the risk is properly classified as obvious, so its perceived inadequacy does not itself found breach; the council was under no proactive duty to do more.

  **Conclusion**

  Applying Romeo v Conservation Commission (NT) and its statutory codification in ss 5F–5H CLA, Coastal Shire Council owed Tegan the general duty an occupier owes any entrant, but that duty did not extend to fencing the cliff edge or providing a more specific warning, because the danger of loose, unstable ground at an unfenced clifftop is an obvious risk within s 5F. Tegan is presumed under s 5G to have been aware of that general risk, and s 5H removes any duty to warn of it in any event, since none of the three statutory exceptions is engaged on these facts. The council should be advised that Tegan's claim is likely to fail at the duty stage — or, even if a duty were found, the obvious-risk analysis would mean no breach arises from the absence of fencing or a more specific sign — and that Romeo remains the leading authority defeating claims of this kind against public occupiers of natural, unfenced landscape features.
