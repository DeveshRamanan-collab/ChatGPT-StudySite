  Using Rootes v Shelton and Agar v Hyde, explain how courts distinguish the "inherent risk" of a sport from a
  separate failure of duty of care, and how ss 5L–5M of the Civil Liability Act interact with that distinction.
model: |
  Rootes v Shelton establishes that participants in a sport accept the risks inherent in it, but this does not
  eliminate all duty of care between participants. As Barwick CJ put it, participants "may be held to have
  accepted risks which are inherent in that sport or pastime … but this does not eliminate all duty of care of
  the one participant to the other," and whether a duty arises, and its extent, depends on the circumstances
  of the case. In Rootes itself, an experienced water-skier was briefly blinded by spray during a "crossover"
  manoeuvre and collided with a stationary boat; the High Court held the boat driver owed, and had breached, a
  duty of care because the towing boat's observer failed to warn skiers of the stationary obstacle. This was
  not simply an inherent risk of water-skiing but a distinct failure to take a reasonable precaution (a
  warning) that was independent of the ordinary risks of the sport. Agar v Hyde shows the same inherent-risk
  reasoning defeating a claim entirely: two rugby players injured in a scrum argued the International Rugby
  Football Board owed a duty to monitor and change the rules to reduce "unnecessary risk," but the High Court
  found no such duty. Gleeson CJ explained that rugby "is based on activities … which, by the standards of
  most members of the community, are obviously dangerous," making it very fact-specific to say what counts as
  an unnecessary, rather than inherent, risk — and here the risk of injury in a scrum was treated as inherent
  to the game rather than a discrete failure of care by a remote regulatory body. The Civil Liability Act
  reinforces this distinction with its own statutory rules for dangerous recreational activities. Section 5L
  removes liability entirely for harm from the materialisation of an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational
  activity, regardless of the plaintiff's actual awareness of it, effectively giving legislative force to the
  inherent-risk idea in Rootes and Agar. Section 5M goes further, removing the duty of care over a
  recreational risk once it has been the subject of a general or specific risk warning, whether given orally
  or in writing, and whether or not the plaintiff actually understood it. Together, the common law and the
  statute point the same way: a plaintiff cannot recover for the ordinary, inherent risks of a sport or
  recreational activity, but a distinct and separate failure of care — a failure to warn, a departure from
  safety rules, or similar conduct beyond the sport's own inherent dangers — can still found liability.
