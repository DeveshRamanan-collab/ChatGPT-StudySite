model: |
  The statement should be rejected as stated. Article 38(1) does distinguish two groups of sources: paragraphs
  (a)-(c) — treaties, customary international law, and general principles of law — are primary sources, the
  real sources of binding obligations by states, while paragraph (d) — judicial decisions and the teachings of
  the most highly qualified publicists — is secondary, a subsidiary means for determining what the law already
  is rather than a law-creating source in its own right. That distinction is real, but it is not the "clear
  hierarchy" the statement asserts between treaty, custom and general principles themselves.

  First, Article 38(1) was not drafted to rank sources against each other; its purpose was simply to tell the
  Court what it could apply when deciding disputes. Second, a rule of international law can exist as both a
  treaty rule and a customary rule at the same time (as with the right of self-defence, found in both custom
  and UN Charter art 51), so treaty and custom are not mutually exclusive or ranked — a state not party to the
  treaty can still be bound by the parallel customary rule, and the two rules can even carry different
  specifications. Third, general principles operate as a gap-filler rather than a subordinate source in a
  formal hierarchy: they are reached for only once a dispute cannot be effectively resolved on the basis of
  treaty or custom, which is a functional ordering of convenience rather than a ranking of legal authority.

  A better description is that Article 38(1) lists a primary/secondary distinction (law-creating versus
  law-interpreting), within which the three primary sources coexist and interact (a treaty may codify,
  crystallise, or generate a customary rule), rather than a strict hierarchy in which a "higher" source
  automatically defeats a "lower" one.
