  The Week 6 slides quote a line attributed to John Dewey: "We do not learn from experience… we learn from reflecting on experience." Moon (1999) describes reflection as "a form of mental processing with a purpose".   Critically evaluate the following statement:

  "In a fast-moving market, innovators learn by doing, not by reflecting. Time spent reflecting is time lost."

model: |
  **Thesis**

  The statement sets up a false choice. The fast-moving methods the unit teaches do not replace reflection; they build it into the cycle and shorten it. Build–measure–learn ends in a documented learning step, scrum teams review their progress daily and their process after every cycle, and the Week 8 innovation process includes learning lessons for improvement. What fast markets punish is slow reflection and unexamined experience, not reflection itself. Doing without reflecting produces activity, and sometimes the same mistake twice; reflecting without doing produces nothing to learn from. Innovators need both, in short cycles.

  **Argument**

  *Why reflect.* The Week 6 slides say it is not sufficient merely to have experience in order to learn: without reflecting, an experience may be quickly forgotten or its learning potential lost. Moon calls reflection "a form of mental processing with a purpose", and the slides list five purposes: considering the process of our own learning, critically reviewing behaviour, building theory from observation, personal development, and making decisions or resolving uncertainty. The last is exactly what a fast-moving market demands. The lecture's image is Dumbledore's Pensieve: excess thoughts siphoned into a basin so that it becomes easier to spot patterns and links. The lecturer added that, constantly distracted by phones and social media, we have many experiences but rarely sit down to reflect on them, and so do not really learn from them.

  *Reflection inside the fast methods.* The lean start-up's loop runs build, measure, learn, and the Learn stage is reflective. The innovation accounting report records the background, a falsifiable hypothesis, the build, the measured results, the learning (validated, invalidated or inconclusive) and the next action. Validated learning begins by asking "What do we need to learn?" before anything is built. At Learn the venture decides whether to pivot or persevere — a decision made by reviewing evidence, not by doing more. Scrum teams hold brief daily stand-ups to review progress and identify roadblocks, and after each cycle they brainstorm ways to improve future cycles. Week 8's innovation process includes learning lessons for improvement and developing the organisation, and Week 12 names continuous learning among the challenges of managing innovation and the learning organisation — knowledge capture and dissemination — among the ways to overcome its barriers.

  *Examples.* Instagram's founders did not simply keep building Burbn: they noticed that users posted a lot of pictures and refocused on being really good at one thing. Collis argues that strategy frames how results are read: damage reports on Spitfires pointed to the wrong areas until someone saw that planes hit elsewhere never came back. Dow Corning documented lessons learned from the Deck Protector, including that poor risk management by a customer can sink a successful project, and the project became the basis for all its new commercialisation projects. Mark, after more than 30 years in furniture, advises keeping a diary and records, and making mistakes but not repeating them.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the statement is that speed is itself the source of learning. The build–measure–learn loop's one instruction is to minimise the total time through it. Agile values working prototypes over excessive documentation and resolves arguments with experiments rather than endless debates or appeals to authority. Brown and Wyatt accept failure as long as it comes early and teaches something, and Blank says the facts live outside the building. On this view, an hour spent writing a reflective paper is an hour not spent testing with customers.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument is fair against long, detached reflection, but each of its sources keeps a reflective step. Minimising time through the loop includes learning faster — through customer development and the "five whys" — not skipping the learning. Experiments only resolve arguments if someone states a falsifiable hypothesis in advance and reads the result honestly, which is what the innovation accounting report is for. Early failure is a source of learning only if someone stops to ask what failed: Iger's verdict on the interactive division's losses of more than $1 billion was "we learned from that". Week 5 adds a further reason to protect reflective time: in Wallas' model, Incubation needs time away from conscious work, and more difficult creative problems need mental relaxation, not just distraction.

  So the statement should be rejected. In a fast-moving market reflection should be quicker, more frequent and closer to the evidence — not abandoned. The unit's own definition makes the point: reflection is mental processing with a purpose, and in innovation the purpose is to decide what to build next.
