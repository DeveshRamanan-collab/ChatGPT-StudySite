  **Thesis**

  The statement is half right. In the divergent step of creative problem solving the number of ideas does matter: quantity breaks the habit of settling for the first idea, and many of the unit's techniques are built to raise it. But "simply" is wrong. Creativity starts before divergence, with identifying an interesting problem, and finishes after it, with convergence and verification. A team that produces the most ideas about the wrong problem, or cannot choose among them, is busy rather than creative. The most creative teams produce many ideas on a well-defined problem and then select well.

  **Argument**

  *Where quantity belongs.* Week 2's creative problem solving runs in three steps: identify an interesting problem, divergent thinking (find solutions), and convergent thinking (find "the" solution, or a good solution). Divergent thinking is where numbers count. The quota method asks every team member for at least three ideas, because once three good ideas have been found many more generally follow, and because linear thinking tempts us to settle for the first idea. Mattimore's brainwriting turns 12 participants and five rounds into 60 ideas, and gets round the introvert/extrovert problem because everyone "speaks" with their pen. Brown and Wyatt's ideation space defers judgement and quotes Pauling. On this much, the statement is right.

  *Problem first.* But the first step is not about numbers. The Week 2 slide attributes to Einstein the claim that he would spend 59 minutes of an hour defining the problem; identifying meaningful problems and asking the right questions is "a key characteristic of creative people". The slide's examples are questions, not answers: "Why is the speed of light the same everywhere?" led to relativity; "How to use colours differently?" to impressionism; "How to make bus stop shelters viable?" to the worldwide success of Decaux. Markman explains why: the statement of the problem is the cue to memory. Thinking about vacuum cleaner bags reminds you of bags; describing the problem as separating the dirt from the air drops the bag and brings industrial cyclones to mind, which is how Dyson's bagless vacuum came about. A radically new solution requires a new problem statement, and the most consistently creative groups find many descriptions of the problem — not "think outside the box", but find more descriptions of the box. Kara Pecknold, asked to build a website for Rwandan weavers who had little access to computers, made progress by reframing the brief to ask what services could improve their livelihoods, not by producing more website ideas.

  *Selection and verification.* After divergence comes convergence: identifying benefits and risks, keeping the best and eliminating wrong answers. Week 5's creative problem-solving process puts objective, fact and problem finding before idea finding, and solution and acceptance finding after it. Wallas' model ends with Verification, which the lecture stresses for people who generate lots of ideas, because the problem is often matching and fitting the solutions to the problem; Poincaré warned that an inspiration felt with certainty can still fail "the test of verification".

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest defence of the statement is empirical. Carmeli, Gelbard and Reiter-Palmon measure creative performance in two dimensions, fluency (the number of ideas) and originality (highly original ideas, mentioned by fewer than 1% of the sample), and found creative problem solving related to both. Large numbers also raise the chance of an original idea: the Floor Company Exercise demands 100 ideas in 10 minutes precisely so that teams get past adding an adjective like "soft" or "blue". If more ideas tend to bring more original ideas, counting ideas is a fair proxy for creativity.

  **Evaluation**

  The evidence supports quantity as one dimension of creative performance, not as the measure of it. Carmeli et al. treat fluency and originality separately because they are different, and in their second study creative problem solving fully mediated only the link between internal knowledge sharing and originality. The Floor Company Exercise itself ends by asking teams to pick their top three ideas and report one: diverging, then converging. Week 2 adds that there is no standard test for the ability to formulate interesting problems — exactly the ability the statement leaves out.

  So the statement should be qualified. Producing many ideas is a sign of a creative team at one stage of the process. The most creative teams also spend time finding and redescribing the problem, and have the discipline to converge, verify and choose. A count of ideas measures the middle of the process; creativity is the whole of it.
