  Discuss the importance of the judgments of international courts and tribunals as a source of international
  law, notwithstanding Article 59 of the ICJ Statute.
model: |
  As a matter of doctrine, judicial decisions are not, in and of themselves, binding international law. Article
  59 ICJ Statute provides that "the decision of the Court has no binding force except between the parties and
  in respect of that particular case." Unlike a common law system, where case law is a primary source through
  the doctrine of precedent, an ICJ decision does not bind the Court, or any state, in a later case, even
  between the same parties on similar facts. This respects the consent-based, non-compulsory nature of the
  Court's jurisdiction: a state not before the Court in a given dispute has not consented to be bound by its
  outcome.

  Despite this formal limitation, judicial decisions carry substantial practical importance. International
  judges and legal practitioners constantly refer to, and base their reasoning on, previous decisions, and the
  ICJ rarely departs from its own jurisprudence. This consistency is maintained for identifiable reasons:
  legitimacy, since inconsistent jurisprudence would undermine confidence in the international adjudicatory
  system; predictability, since states would be reluctant to bring disputes to international courts if
  outcomes were unpredictable; and the peaceful settlement of disputes, since consistent case law helps
  maintain international peace and stability. States also invoke ICJ decisions outside formal litigation,
  for example as reference points or bargaining chips in bilateral negotiations.

  Judicial decisions are therefore best understood as a secondary source with real, if indirect, importance:
  they do not themselves create binding obligations, but they carry heavy interpretative weight, guide how
  primary sources (treaties, custom and general principles) are understood and applied, and function much like
  persuasive, rather than binding, authority. An exam answer should treat a relevant ICJ decision as strong
  support for how a rule should be interpreted, while being careful never to present it as the binding source
  of the obligation itself.
