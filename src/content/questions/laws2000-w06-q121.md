  Explain the basis and scope of the immunities of the United Nations, and assess the accountability problems they

model: |
  International organisations enjoy immunity so they can carry out their functions without interference from host
  states and be treated alike across countries. Unlike state immunity, their immunities rest on treaty law granted by
  states. UN Charter art 105 gives the UN the privileges and immunities necessary for the fulfilment of its purposes,
  and member representatives and officials those necessary for the independent exercise of their functions. The 1946
  Convention on the Privileges and Immunities of the UN defines them: the UN, its property and assets are immune from
  every form of legal process unless expressly waived, and no waiver extends to execution (art II s 2); its premises
  and archives are inviolable (ss 3–4). Representatives (art IV), officials (art V s 18) and experts on missions (art
  VI) have functional immunity only.

  Accountability is the problem. The UN must provide alternative modes of settling private claims, and the
  Secretary-General can waive officials' immunity. But after the 2010 Haiti cholera outbreak, which killed nearly
  10,000 people, the UN apologised without accepting legal responsibility and US federal courts upheld its immunity
  in 2016. In Mothers of Srebrenica the Dutch Supreme Court (2012) and the ECtHR (2013) recognised UN immunity, so
  victims could proceed only against the Netherlands.
