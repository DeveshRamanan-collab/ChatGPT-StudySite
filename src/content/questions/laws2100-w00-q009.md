  Law schools promise to teach students to "think like a lawyer". But is there a distinctive way of thinking to learn? The American realists doubted it: judges, they said, respond to the underlying facts of cases and then find doctrine to support a result reached on other grounds. Critical legal scholars went further and called legal reasoning a myth, since the legal materials are so indeterminate that they cannot guide or constrain a decision.

  Within analytical jurisprudence the answers are surprising. Raz, a legal positivist, rejected "the strong thesis of the autonomy of legal reasoning": "Legal reasoning is an instance of moral reasoning." Finnis, a natural law theorist, said the opposite: "Legal reasoning, indeed, is technical reasoning, at least in large part not moral reasoning." Schauer, in *Thinking Like a Lawyer* (2009), suggests that legal reasoning may be special not because its methods are unique — we reason by analogy and precedent in sport and daily life too — but because certain methods are "particularly concentrated and dominant in legal argument and decision-making". He adds that the belief in legal reasoning is "in the final analysis an empirical claim", and that "in a society governed by the wise and the good, legal reasoning is likely simply to get in the way".

  Critically evaluate the following statement:

  "There is no such thing as legal reasoning — only reasoning that lawyers happen to do."

  In your essay, separate the empirical question (do judges in fact reason differently?) from the conceptual one (could legal reasoning be different in kind?), and say how, if at all, the answer depends on a general theory of law. Draw on material from more than one week of the unit, engaging with at least three of Schauer, Raz, Finnis, Dworkin, Hart and the realists. Take a clear position, support it with reasons, and deal with the strongest counterargument to your view.
model: |
  **Thesis**

  The statement is false, but for a modest reason. Legal reasoning is not a different kind of reasoning from the practical reasoning we use elsewhere: it uses deduction, analogy, induction, purposive reasoning and the weighing of principles, none of which is unique to law. But it is not merely "reasoning that lawyers happen to do" either. Legal reasoning is reasoning constrained by authoritative sources and by the generality of rules, and those constraints make certain methods, above all precedent and analogy, concentrated and dominant in law. Schauer's claim of distinctiveness by concentration is the right one, and it survives the realist and critical challenges.

  **Argument**

  *Not different in kind.* The main types of legal reasoning — deductive, inductive, analogical, abductive, purposive, and reasoning by principle — are all found outside law. We apply rules deductively when we check whether a document is a valid will, but we reason the same way about the rules of a game; we reason by analogy and precedent in sport and in daily life. Dworkin's principles are weighed, not applied all-or-nothing (TRS 24), but weighing reasons is the stuff of all practical deliberation. So if "legal reasoning" means a method found only in law, the statement is right.

  *Distinct by concentration and constraint.* Schauer's answer does not depend on uniqueness. If we identify "methods of reasoning that are found everywhere but that are particularly concentrated and dominant in legal argument and decision-making, then the claim that there is something called legal reasoning will turn out to be justified" (p 8). The concentration is not an accident of who lawyers are. Legal reasoning goes with the inherent generality of law: a legal system communicates general standards through legislation and precedent (CL 124–126), and a judge must give weight to the rule or the earlier decision even when she would have decided differently on the merits. That is why, as Schauer observes, "in a society governed by the wise and the good, legal reasoning is likely simply to get in the way" (p 11). The remark shows what legal reasoning is: a practice of deciding by authority rather than by all-things-considered judgment. It would be superfluous among the wise and the good precisely because it is different from what they would do.

  *Finnis and Raz, read together.* The apparent reversal — the positivist calls legal reasoning moral; the natural lawyer calls it technical — supports this view. Raz holds that courts ought to follow the principles of their system, "which are moral principles, though they may be mistaken moral principles", so legal reasoning is moral reasoning that is sometimes morally incorrect. Finnis holds that academic theory "greatly exaggerates the extent to which reason can settle what is greater good or lesser evil, and minimizes the need for authoritative sources". Both agree that the legal reasoner is bound by sources in a way the unconstrained moral reasoner is not. They differ over how to characterise that constraint. The real question, as the unit puts it, is whether legal reasoning is different in kind from other practical reasoning geared towards action; both answers imply that it is at least different in its conditions.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest counterargument comes from realism and critical legal studies. Empirical rule-scepticism holds that judges react primarily to the facts of the case; there are so many potentially relevant rules and so many ways of reading statutes and precedents that the materials support both sides, and doctrine is chosen to justify results reached on other grounds — the judge's personality (Frank), the shared background of judges (Llewellyn), or policy (Cohen). CLS adds radical indeterminacy: law is contradictory because it stems from political conflict, and "legal reasoning" is a legitimating ideology. If that is right, the constraints I have relied on are illusory, and what remains really is only reasoning that lawyers happen to do, dressed up in the language of authority.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument makes an empirical claim, and Schauer is right that the question is empirical. But the evidence the realists rely on is drawn from contested, litigated cases, where by definition the materials pointed in more than one direction. Hart's account of open texture already concedes indeterminacy in the penumbra; it denies that every case lies there. Rules individuals can apply to themselves without fresh official guidance (CL 130) are the everyday evidence that sources constrain. Leiter's reading of realism is also telling: realist indeterminacy arguments presuppose a positivist account of what the legal materials are (293). One cannot say the legal reasons run out without knowing which reasons are legal — and that is already to accept a distinction between legal and non-legal reasoning.

  The CLS version is stronger in scope but weaker in support. That judges share a commitment to the status quo would explain predictability, but it would not show that sources have no effect; and the claim that words have no stable meaning, if taken seriously, would undermine the critics' own arguments as much as the law's. Schauer's work on rules and standards points to a better description: interpreters do push rules towards standards and standards towards rules, but that is movement within a practice of reasoning from authoritative texts, not evidence that no such practice exists.

  Finally, the answer does not depend on a general theory of law. The Raz and Finnis reversal shows that specific claims about legal reasoning are separable, though not disconnected, from an author's view of the nature of law. A positivist and a natural lawyer can agree that legal reasoning is distinctive by concentration and constraint.

  The statement should therefore be rejected. There is no special logic of law, but there is legal reasoning: practical reasoning disciplined by the generality of law and the authority of its sources.
