model: |
  **Issue**

  Four issues arise: (1) whether Article 5 of the Convention has direct effect in Australian law; (2) whether s 20 of the Border Detention Act can be read, with the Convention's help, as requiring review by an independent court; (3) whether the principle of legality affects s 12; and (4) whether DFAT's draft reply to Veloria correctly states Australia's position in international law.

  **Rule**

  Australia is primarily a dualist system. Treaties do not have direct effect: in *Dietrich v The Queen*, ratification of the ICCPR "has no direct legal effect upon domestic law" until specific legislation implements it. A treaty may be transformed by new legislation, amendment of existing legislation, scheduling, reliance on existing legislation already giving effect to it, or giving it the force of law; s 51(xxix) of the Constitution supports such legislation. International law is not "a back door means of importing an unincorporated convention" (Mason CJ and Deane J, *Teoh*).

  International law nevertheless shapes interpretation. Under s 15AB(2)(d) of the *Acts Interpretation Act 1901* (Cth), a treaty may assist interpretation only if Australia actually or contemplatedly participated in it before the Act was enacted, and only to confirm the ordinary meaning, or where the provision is ambiguous or obscure or would otherwise produce a manifestly absurd or unreasonable result. A treaty cannot modify words that are otherwise clear (*Yager*, Mason J). At common law, in a case of ambiguity, courts favour a construction that accords with international law (*Jumbunna*), but this presumption does not apply to clear contrary legislation (*Polites*). Under the principle of legality, courts will not impute to Parliament an intention to curtail fundamental rights and freedoms unless it is clearly manifested by unambiguous language (*Coco*). Parliament may legislate in breach of international law, "taking the risk of international complications".

  At the international level, a party "may not invoke the provisions of its internal law as justification for its failure to perform a treaty" (VCLT art 27). Under ARSIWA art 3, mostly recognised as custom, whether an act is internationally wrongful "is governed by international law" and "is not affected by the characterization of the same act as lawful by internal law".

  **Application**

  *(1) Direct effect.* Australia ratified the Convention in 2019, but no legislation implements it. The National Interest Analysis said the Convention would be implemented through existing legislation, which is one recognised method of transformation, but only if existing legislation actually gives effect to it. Nothing in Australian law in 2019 required court review of immigration detention, and the Border Detention Act, passed later, does not do so either. Under *Dietrich*, Article 5 therefore has no direct legal effect, and Ms Farah cannot rely on it as a free-standing source of a right to release or to review. *Teoh* confirms that she cannot use international law as a back door to import it.

  *(2) Section 20.* Section 20 requires that detention "shall be reviewed every six months" but does not say by whom. That is a genuine ambiguity. The s 15AB(2)(d) conditions are met: Australia ratified the Convention in 2019, before the Act was enacted in 2021, so it participated in the treaty beforehand. Because s 20 is ambiguous on the identity of the reviewer, the Convention may be used as extrinsic material. The *Jumbunna* presumption points the same way: where a provision is ambiguous, the court should prefer the construction consistent with Australia's obligations — here, Article 5's requirement of review by an independent court after twelve months. Ms Farah has a respectable argument that, at least once detention exceeds twelve months, s 20 should be read as requiring review by a court. The Commonwealth will respond that the Act's silence, and its practice of departmental review, show Parliament did not intend court review; but practice does not resolve textual ambiguity.

  *(3) Section 12.* Section 12 is different. It says that an unlawful non-citizen "must be kept in detention until he or she is removed from Australia or granted a visa". Ms Farah will invoke the principle of legality: liberty is a fundamental right, and Parliament must use unambiguous language to curtail it (*Coco*). But s 12's words appear clear and mandatory. Where legislation is clear, neither the *Jumbunna* presumption (*Polites*) nor a treaty (*Yager*) can modify it, and Parliament may legislate in breach of international law. The principle of legality is also satisfied by clearly manifested language. Her argument on s 12 is therefore weak: clear words will prevail. Her best prospect is the procedural argument on s 20, not release under s 12.

  *(4) DFAT's draft reply.* The draft reply is wrong in international law. That Ms Farah's detention is lawful under the Border Detention Act says nothing about whether Australia has complied with Article 5. Under art 27 VCLT, Australia may not invoke its internal law as a justification for failing to perform the Convention, and under ARSIWA art 3 the characterisation of the detention as lawful by Australian law does not affect whether it is internationally wrongful. Domestic legality and international legality are separate questions. If Ms Farah has been detained for more than twelve months without review by an independent court, the conduct of Australian organs (the department, applying the Act) breaches Article 5 — a treaty obligation in force for Australia since 2019. Parliament's entitlement to pass the Act is a domestic point; at the international level, Australia took the risk of international complications. As a party, Veloria can invoke Australia's responsibility, and DFAT's reply should be redrafted to address the Convention on its own terms.

  **Conclusion**

  Article 5 has no direct effect in Australian law, so Ms Farah cannot rely on it directly (*Dietrich*; *Teoh*). She has a real argument that s 20 is ambiguous and should be read consistently with the Convention as requiring review by an independent court, since the s 15AB conditions are met and the *Jumbunna* presumption applies. Her argument that s 12 does not authorise continued detention is weak, because its words are clear (*Polites*; *Yager*), even allowing for the principle of legality (*Coco*). DFAT's draft reply is incorrect: under art 27 VCLT and ARSIWA art 3, Australia cannot rely on the Border Detention Act to justify non-performance of the Convention, and should address the substance of Veloria's complaint.
