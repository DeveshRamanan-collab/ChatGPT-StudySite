  **Thesis**

  The statement is partly right. Proportionality does give up the idea that every right is a trump: a right that yields whenever a limit is suitable, necessary and on balance beneficial is not a card that wins whatever the hand. But the statement goes too far in two ways. First, balancing is not utilitarian aggregation: proportionality asks whether a particular limit on a particular right is justified, and the burden of justification lies on the limit. Secondly, not every right is balanced. The non-derogable rights in art 15(2) of the European Convention keep a core that works as a trump, and Waldron's indirect utilitarianism shows that there can be good reasons, even utilitarian ones, for officials not to weigh rights against welfare case by case.

  **Argument**

  *Rights against utility.* Human rights are said to have priority over the general interest. Act-utilitarianism, a form of consequentialism, judges each action by its consequences, measured by the principle of utility. The ticking bomb shows the clash: an act-utilitarian aggregating happiness may justify torturing one suspect to save hundreds of lives; someone truly committed to human rights says it is not acceptable. The minority religion makes the same point in ordinary politics: if a majority is disturbed that a minority embraces a different religion, purely utilitarian reasoning may justify forcing the minority to stop, and defending the minority needs reasons that value its rights. Whether rights are "trumps", the high-priority card in a game (the lecture's Dworkin), or "shields" that protect individuals from interference by other claims (the lecture's Schauer), they disrupt this kind of reasoning.

  *Limitation in practice.* Constitutional texts nonetheless provide for limits. The Canadian Charter guarantees rights "subject only to such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society" (s 1). The Victorian Charter adopts the formula, "based on human dignity, equality and freedom", and lists the relevant factors: the nature of the right, the importance of the purpose of the limitation, the nature and extent of the limitation, the relationship between the limitation and its purpose, and any less restrictive means reasonably available (s 7(2)). Alexy gives the practice a theory. Rules are definitive commands; principles are optimisation requirements, to be realised to the greatest extent possible given legal and factual constraints. Because rights are principles, proportionality is necessarily connected to their adjudication: a limit must be suitable to a legitimate aim, necessary in that no less restrictive means would achieve it, and proportionate in the narrow sense, its benefits outweighing the detriment to the right.

  *The resemblance to utility.* The third stage looks utilitarian. Benefits are weighed against detriments, and Alexy's weight formula tries to formalise the comparison through the intensity of the interference, the importance of the competing principle and the certainty about both. There is a parallel in week 5. Dworkin's principles, unlike rules, have a dimension of weight (TRS 22–24): conflicting rules cannot both stand, but conflicting principles are weighed. If rights are principles in this sense, a rights claim can be outweighed.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the statement is that the difference between balancing and utilitarian calculation is only one of degree. Whatever the formula, a right that loses at the balancing stage gives way because the competing interest is weightier, which is exactly what the trump metaphor denied. The lecture made the point with defeasibility from week 7: keep finding exceptions to a rule and at some point the rule has been chipped away; find enough limitations to a right and one may ask what it even means to have it. A general reasonable-limits clause invites that erosion. On this view, rights in a system with a limitation clause are at most shields, and a shield can be overcome by enough force.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument shows that most constitutional rights are not trumps. It does not show that they have become one more weight on a utilitarian scale, for three reasons.

  First, the structure is different. Utilitarianism adds up consequences for everyone and chooses the greatest total. Proportionality starts from the right and asks whether a specific measure can be "demonstrably justified": the measure must serve a legitimate aim, and the necessity stage rules out any measure for which a less restrictive means exists, however useful it might be. The question is not what maximises happiness, but whether this interference with this right is justified. Hart's answer to Llewellyn's dilemma applies here: "A rule that ends with the word 'unless…' is still a rule" (CL 139). A right subject to justified limits is still a right; it shifts the burden of argument to whoever would limit it.

  Secondly, some rights are not balanced at all. Article 15(2) allows no derogation, even "in time of war or other public emergency threatening the life of the nation", from the prohibition of torture, from the prohibition of slavery or from the rule against punishment without law. The ticking bomb is precisely such an emergency, and the Convention gives the rights theorist's answer, not the act-utilitarian's. Whether these rights are absolute is debated, and the right to life carries its own exception; but the structure of art 15 shows that a legal system can balance most rights while treating a core as a trump.

  Thirdly, Waldron's indirect utilitarianism suggests that the two ideas need not be opposed. For utilitarian reasons it can make sense to create structures in which officials are asked to refrain from utilitarian reasoning and to respect rights. Entrenched rights tested by structured proportionality are such a structure: officials do not ask, case by case, what maximises welfare, but whether a limit on a right can be justified.

  So the statement should be qualified. Proportionality abandons the idea that every right is a trump, but it does not turn rights into utilitarian weights: it keeps the burden of justification on the limit, and it coexists with rights that are not balanced at all.
