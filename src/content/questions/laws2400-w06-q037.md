  "There is generally no duty to rescue or to control the conduct of others." Using Hargrave v Goldman and
  Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre v Anzil, explain what extra ingredients can turn this general rule around,
  and why they were present in one case but not the other.
model: |
  The general common law position is that there is no duty to rescue and no duty to control a third party's
  conduct — foreseeability of harm is not, by itself, enough to found a duty based on an omission. Hargrave v
  Goldman identifies the extra ingredients that can displace this rule: reasonable foreseeability of the risk,
  a special relationship between the parties (there, occupier and neighbour), the defendant's knowledge of
  the danger, and their practical control over it. On the facts of Hargrave, the landowner knew a fire he had
  only partially extinguished remained on his land and could still be controlled by him, which combined with
  the occupier-neighbour relationship to found a duty. Modbury Triangle Shopping Centre v Anzil shows the
  limits of this reasoning: although the shopping centre, as occupier, had control over and knowledge of the
  state of its own premises (the car park lighting), it had no control over the independent criminal conduct
  of the men who attacked Anzil. Gleeson CJ held that control over premises is not the same as control over
  the people who might commit crimes there, and neither reliance nor an assumption of responsibility (the
  centre's practice of lighting the car park) extended to a responsibility to prevent criminal attacks. The
  difference between the two cases is therefore that in Hargrave the defendant controlled the actual source
  of the danger, while in Modbury the defendant controlled only the premises, not the third party whose
  independent choice caused the harm — though Gleeson CJ left open that a duty might arise if criminal
  conduct had a high degree of foreseeability and predictability, which was not shown on Modbury's facts.
