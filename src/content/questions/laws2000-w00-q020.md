  **Issue**

  The issues are: (a) whether the ICC has jurisdiction over the highlands killings, and whether cases against Rask and Hale are admissible despite Ondara's trials and amnesty; (b) whether domestic law, superior orders or Hale's office are any answer before the ICC; and (c) whether Hale can be prosecuted in Australia now, or after she leaves office.

  **Rule**

  The ICC has jurisdiction over genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and aggression (art 5), committed after the Statute entered into force for the relevant state (art 11). The Prosecutor may act proprio motu (arts 13(c), 15), in which case the territorial state or the accused's state of nationality must be a party (art 12(2)). Genocide means acts such as killing members of a national, ethnical, racial or religious group "with intent to destroy, in whole or in part" the group as such (Genocide Convention art II).

  The Court is "complementary to national criminal jurisdictions" (art 1). A case is inadmissible under art 17 where a state with jurisdiction is investigating or prosecuting it, or has investigated and decided not to prosecute, unless the state is unwilling or unable genuinely to do so; where the person has already been tried for the conduct; or where the case lacks sufficient gravity. Unwillingness includes proceedings or decisions made "for the purpose of shielding the person concerned from criminal responsibility". Under art 21 the Court applies the Statute first, and national laws only as a source of general principles consistent with it.

  The Nuremberg Principles (GA Res 95(I)) deny three defences: internal law that imposes no penalty (II), official position (III), and superior orders where "a moral choice was in fact possible" (IV). Immunity is procedural and "does not mean … impunity" (*Arrest Warrant*). Personal immunity attaches to the office of a head of state, head of government or foreign minister, covers all acts, lasts only while in office, and admits no exception for international crimes in foreign national courts; but prosecution remains possible in the person's own state, after waiver, by other states once the person leaves office for acts before or after office and private acts during it, and before international criminal courts (*Arrest Warrant*). Functional immunity attaches to official acts and survives office. In *Pinochet (No 3)* a former head of state had no immunity for torture subject to a form of universal jurisdiction under the Convention against Torture; ILC draft article 7 (2017) would exclude immunity for genocide and crimes against humanity, but whether it reflects state practice is debated. Universal jurisdiction extends to genocide and crimes against humanity; states usually require the accused's presence.

  **Application**

  *(a) Jurisdiction.* Killing about 900 Vey villagers, under orders that "no Vey family is to remain in the west", is capable of amounting to genocide: the Vey are an ethnic group, and the order is evidence of intent to destroy the group in part, in the western highlands. In the alternative it is capable of amounting to crimes against humanity (art 7). The conduct in 2024 post-dates Ondara's ratification in 2005 (art 11). The Prosecutor acted proprio motu, with authorisation, and both the territorial state and the accused's state of nationality — Ondara — are parties (art 12(2)). The Court has jurisdiction.

  *Admissibility.* Admissibility is assessed case by case. Ondara tried three privates for "excessive use of a firearm"; it has not investigated or prosecuted Rask or Hale, and those trials did not concern their conduct. Nothing in art 17(1)(a)–(c) therefore makes a case against Rask or Hale inadmissible. Ondara might argue that the amnesty is a national decision not to prosecute (art 17(1)(b)). If so, it fails the genuineness test: an amnesty for acts done "in good faith in restoring public order", followed by Rask's promotion, bears the marks of a decision made to shield those responsible. Ondara's justice system functions normally, so this is unwillingness, not inability. The killing of about 900 people is plainly of sufficient gravity.

  Ondara's argument (1) misreads complementarity: national courts come first only when their proceedings are genuine, and art 17 contains no exception for amnesties adopted for reconciliation. Under art 21 the Court applies the Statute first; a national statute such as the Reconciliation Act could enter only as a source of general principles, and only if consistent with the Statute and international law.

  *(b) Domestic law, orders and office before the ICC.* Rask's reliance on the Reconciliation Act fails under Principle II: that internal law imposes no penalty for an act that is a crime under international law does not relieve him of responsibility. His plea of superior orders fails under Principle IV, because a colonel ordering that no Vey village be left standing plainly had a moral choice. Hale's office is no answer before the ICC either. Principle III denies any shield based on official position, and *Arrest Warrant* recognises that immunity does not bar prosecution before international criminal courts. Ondara, as a State Party, must also cooperate fully with the Court (art 86).

  *(c) Prosecution in Australia now.* Australia has no territorial or nationality link to the crimes, so it would need to rely on universal jurisdiction, which extends to genocide and crimes against humanity. Hale's presence in Brisbane would meet the usual requirement that the accused be present. But Hale is a serving foreign minister, so she holds personal immunity under customary international law. In *Arrest Warrant* a warrant against a serving foreign minister breached that immunity, and state practice supports no exception for international crimes. Unless Ondara waives her immunity — which is unlikely — Australia cannot arrest or prosecute her during the visit.

  *After she leaves office.* Personal immunity then ends, and only functional immunity remains, for official acts. Planning a military operation as Defence Minister is an official act, so Hale would argue that it is covered. The answer is uncertain. *Pinochet (No 3)* denied a former head of state immunity for torture subject to universal jurisdiction under the Convention against Torture, to which the states concerned were parties; ILC draft article 7 would exclude immunity for genocide and crimes against humanity, but whether it reflects state practice is debated. A prosecution in Australia after she leaves office is therefore possible but contestable, while proceedings before the ICC face no immunity bar at any time.

  **Conclusion**

  The ICC has jurisdiction over the highlands killings, which may amount to genocide or crimes against humanity committed on the territory, and by nationals, of a State Party. Cases against Rask and Hale are admissible: Ondara has not proceeded against them, and its amnesty and promotion of Rask show unwillingness (art 17). The Reconciliation Act and superior orders are no defence (Nuremberg Principles II and IV), and Hale's office is no bar before the ICC (Principle III; *Arrest Warrant*). Australia cannot prosecute Hale while she is Foreign Minister, because of her personal immunity (*Arrest Warrant*); after she leaves office, prosecution for her official acts depends on the debated exception for international crimes (*Pinochet (No 3)*; ILC draft art 7). Immunity does not mean impunity: the ICC route remains open throughout.
