  **Issue**

  Two issues arise. First, whether Deacon's conduct in blocking Anzac Lane for three weeks amounts to public nuisance actionable by Mariana. Second, whether BuildRight Pty Ltd is vicariously liable for Deacon's conduct, given that he acted directly against the project manager's explicit written instruction.

  **Rule**

  Public nuisance is an unlawful act or omission which either endangers the lives, safety, property or comfort of the public, or obstructs the enjoyment of a right common to all. Because it is ordinarily the community's or the state's responsibility to address, a private individual may bring a tort action only if they have suffered special or particular damage — damage different from that suffered by the public generally — in addition to establishing interference with a public right that is substantial and unreasonable. Walsh v Ervin [1952] VLR 361 illustrates how special damage can be proved: a farmer ploughed and planted crops on the highway and erected gates blocking the plaintiff's vehicle access to his land; the court held that special damage is not limited to pecuniary loss, but substantial delay and inconvenience, different in kind from what the rest of the public experienced, was enough to give the plaintiff title to sue.

  Vicarious liability makes an employer liable for a tort committed by an employee acting in the course of their employment. Professor Fleming's policy rationale explains the doctrine: an employer who engages others to advance its own economic interests should fairly bear a corresponding liability for losses in the course of that enterprise; the employer is a more promising source of recompense than an employee, often "a man of straw without insurance"; the rule spreads tort losses widely because the employer can pass them on through insurance and higher prices; and it has an admonitory value in accident prevention, because deterrent pressure works best on employers, who are in a strategic position to organise and supervise staff to reduce wrongdoing, and holding the employer liable gives it an incentive to discipline staff who cause harm.

  **Application**

  *Public nuisance.* Deacon's conduct in placing scaffolding materials and a delivery truck across the full width of Anzac Lane, every day for three weeks during trading hours, obstructs the enjoyment of a public right — the right of the public, including customers and suppliers, to pass along a public laneway. This is an unlawful obstruction: nothing suggests BuildRight had any council permit or other lawful authority to occupy the laneway in this way, and doing so without approval, and against an internal instruction that the lane remain clear, points against any lawful justification. The interference is also substantial and unreasonable: it was total (blocking the "full width" of the lane), continuous (every day, during business hours, for three weeks), and calculated to affect exactly the commercial traffic the laneway exists to carry — this is not a fleeting or trivial inconvenience of the kind the law tolerates.

  The remaining requirement is title to sue through special damage. The other shopkeepers suffered only the kind of minor, shared inconvenience — walking around a truck — that the general public (or at least the general class of laneway users) experienced; that is not special damage, and those shopkeepers could not sue. Mariana's position is materially different, and closely analogous to Walsh v Ervin. Just as the farmer's crops and gates caused the plaintiff in Walsh substantial delay and inconvenience distinct from what other road users suffered, the blockage here caused Mariana specific and substantial commercial harm — the daily cost of an alternative hand-cart delivery method and repeated spoilage of perishable stock — that no other shopkeeper on the lane experienced. Her loss is different not merely in degree but in kind from the general public's shared, minor inconvenience, because her business's particular dependence on vehicle-based refrigerated deliveries exposed her to a type of harm the general inconvenience did not inflict on anyone else. Mariana therefore has title to sue, and all three elements of public nuisance are established against Deacon (and, through him, potentially BuildRight).

  *Vicarious liability.* The remaining question is whether BuildRight is liable for Deacon's tort even though he was expressly told to keep the laneway clear. Vicarious liability does not require the employer to have authorised, or even to have been aware of, the specific wrongful conduct; it is enough that the tort was committed in the course of employment. Deacon was performing exactly the kind of task he was employed to do — managing the day-to-day logistics of the building site, including the storage of scaffolding materials and the use of the company's delivery truck — when he blocked the lane. Storing materials and parking the truck in a location convenient for the site, even an unauthorised and instructed-against location, is best understood as an unauthorised way of doing an authorised task (managing site logistics), rather than an entirely independent frolic unconnected to his job. Applying Fleming's policy rationale supports this conclusion: BuildRight, as the entity carrying out the renovation for its own commercial benefit, is far better placed than Deacon personally to bear and insure against this kind of loss; BuildRight controlled the overall project and had the practical ability to supervise Deacon's conduct on site (for example, by following up after Mariana's two complaints to head office, which went unaddressed); and holding BuildRight liable serves the admonitory function Fleming identifies, giving employers like BuildRight a real incentive to enforce their own site instructions rather than merely issue them and take no further action once shopkeepers began complaining directly to head office. The fact that BuildRight did have a policy is not a shield to liability where, on the facts, it did nothing to enforce that policy once put on notice by Mariana's own two complaints.

  **Conclusion**

  Mariana has an actionable claim in public nuisance: Deacon's continuous, substantial and unauthorised blocking of Anzac Lane obstructed a public right, and — applying Walsh v Ervin — Mariana suffered special damage, different in kind from the minor inconvenience felt by the other shopkeepers, giving her title to sue. BuildRight is vicariously liable for Deacon's tort notwithstanding his express instruction to keep the lane clear, because he was still acting in the course of his employment in managing site logistics, and Fleming's policy rationale — control of the enterprise, capacity to insure and spread the loss, and the value of holding the more supervisory party liable — supports imposing liability on BuildRight rather than leaving Mariana to pursue Deacon alone. Mariana should be advised she is likely to recover both the $180-a-day hand-cart costs and the roughly $950 in spoiled stock as damages flowing from the nuisance.
