prompt: |
  Every theory of law must say why the rules of a legal system are valid and what makes them one system. Austin's answer was the sovereign: a determinate superior habitually obeyed by the bulk of a society and not in the habit of obeying anyone else. Kelsen traced validity up a chain — a regulation is valid because a statute authorised it, the statute because the constitution did, and so back to the historically first constitution — and then said we must presuppose a basic norm: you ought to obey the historically first constitution. For Kelsen the basic norm is a "juristic hypothesis", and validity means binding force.

  Hart replaced both with the rule of recognition: a social rule, manifest in the practice of officials, that supplies authoritative criteria for identifying the rules of the system. For Hart validity is membership, and the rule of recognition itself is neither valid nor invalid but simply accepted, like the standard metre bar in Paris. Courts have reasoned with both ideas: the Privy Council in *Madzimbamuto v Lardner-Burke* considered the legality of Rhodesia's 1965 declaration of independence in light of Kelsen's theory, and the UK Supreme Court in *Miller* referred to the rule of recognition as "the rule which identifies the sources of law in our legal system".

  "Hart's rule of recognition and Kelsen's basic norm do the same job. The only real difference is that one is a fact and the other a presupposition, and that difference favours Kelsen."

model: |
  **Thesis**

  The first sentence of the statement is broadly right and the second is wrong. The basic norm and the rule of recognition perform the same function: each explains legal validity and the unity of a legal order. But fact versus presupposition is not the only difference. They also differ on whether the ultimate rule is itself valid, on whether its content varies between systems, and on whether law and morals can conflict. Those differences all flow from two conceptions of validity — binding force for Kelsen, membership for Hart — and on each of them Hart's account fits the facts of legal systems better. The difference favours Hart.

  **Argument**

  *Why not Austin's sovereign?* Austin explained a legal system by a determinate superior habitually obeyed by the bulk of a society (PJD 165). Both Kelsen and Hart reject this, for related reasons. Hart's objection is that "habits are not 'normative': they cannot confer rights or authority on anyone" (CL 60), and a habit of obedience cannot explain the continuity and persistence of law. Kelsen's is that a command, as such, has only a subjective meaning: the gangster's command and the tax officer's look alike, but "the command of the tax officer is authorized by an objectively valid legal norm, whereas the command of the gangster is not" (p 6). Each needs a norm, not a fact about power, at the foundation.

  *Four dimensions of comparison.* First, fact or presupposition. For Hart, whether a rule of recognition exists is a complex, empirical question of fact: it exists if officials accept it, and it is manifest in the way they identify primary rules. For Kelsen the basic norm is a "juristic hypothesis", a "postulated ultimate rule", presupposed by the jurist rather than part of the legal order.

  Second, validity of the ultimate rule. Kelsen's jurist must presuppose that the basic norm is valid. Hart says of the rule of recognition that "no such question can arise … it can neither be valid nor invalid but is simply accepted as appropriate for use in this way" (CL 109) — like the standard metre bar in Paris.

  Third, content. Kelsen's basic norm always has the same content: whoever laid down the historically first constitution ought to be obeyed; only the first constitution differs. Hart's rules of recognition may have different content in different jurisdictions.

  Fourth, law and morals. Hart chose his term partly "to avoid any commitment to Kelsen's view of the conflict between law and morals" (CL 293). For Kelsen a legal norm and a moral norm cannot both be valid at once; for Hart they can conflict.

  *Hart fits the facts better.* The third and fourth dimensions tell against Kelsen. Legal systems do differ in their ultimate criteria. *Miller* shows judges treating the content of the UK's rule of recognition as a live question — the Court would not accept "that the so-called fundamental rule of recognition … has been varied by the 1972 Act or would be varied by its repeal" [60]. That is a question about what officials actually accept, which only Hart's account frames as a question of fact. And it is a plain fact of legal experience that people face valid laws they judge immoral; a theory in which that conflict is meaningless has lost something important.

  **Counterargument**

  The statement's second sentence draws on the strongest argument for Kelsen. Validity is binding force: if a norm is valid, it is binding, and validity cannot be a fact. An ought cannot be derived from an is. Hart's rule of recognition exists because officials accept it, but the fact that officials accept a rule cannot make it binding on anyone. Kelsen at least sees that at the top of the chain something normative must be presupposed if we are to speak of legal obligation at all. On this view Hart does not solve the problem; he describes the practice and leaves the ought unexplained. *Madzimbamuto*, where the Privy Council considered the legality of a revolutionary declaration of independence in light of Kelsen's theory, shows courts reaching for exactly this idea when the chain of validity is broken.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument shows that the two theorists are answering slightly different questions. Kelsen asks how legal norms can be binding; Hart asks how we identify which rules belong to a system and when a system exists. Hart's minimum conditions for the existence of a legal system are that valid rules are generally obeyed and that the secondary rules are "effectively accepted as common public standards of official behaviour by its officials" (CL 109). Both conditions concern existence, not legitimacy. Hart does not claim that acceptance makes a rule morally binding; acceptance from the internal point of view may rest on many grounds.

  So the charge that Hart "leaves the ought unexplained" is true but not a defect by his lights: his is a descriptive theory of a social practice, and the internal point of view explains why participants treat the rules as reasons without the theorist endorsing them. Kelsen's presupposition, by contrast, buys binding force at the price of making the ultimate norm a fiction with fixed content that can never conflict with morality. For the job both ideas share — explaining validity and the unity of a system — that is a poor exchange.

  The statement should therefore be rejected. The two ideas do the same job, but they differ on at least four dimensions, and the difference that matters, validity as membership rather than binding force, favours Hart.
