  Mill was a utilitarian, and the lecture traced the utilitarian reasoning behind his defence of the harm principle. Following the harm principle, Mill argues, will bring maximum happiness in the long run: experimentation with different kinds of lifestyles is valuable for the whole society, even though the majority may find these experiments in living unsavoury or offensive, and enjoying and exercising personal freedom is "one of the principal ingredients of human happiness". But the slides give a second justification as well: the value of personal autonomy and the presumptive case for liberty, the idea that whatever we seem to think about freedom, we agree that it is of intrinsic value for both the individual and society.

  Week 9 asked whether rights can survive utilitarian reasoning. Act-utilitarianism judges each action by its consequences under the principle of utility, the greatest happiness for the greatest number. A majority disturbed by a minority's religion could, in purely utilitarian terms, justify banning it; defending the minority needs reasons that value its rights. On the lecture's account of Waldron's chapter, act, rule and indirect utilitarianism differ, and indirect utilitarianism leaves room for structures in which officials are asked, for utilitarian reasons, to refrain from utilitarian reasoning and respect rights. Mill's harm, meanwhile, is not merely affecting another's interests but infringing interests we are under a duty not to infringe, and the lecture asked students to remember the link between interests, entitlements and rights.

  Critically evaluate the following statement:

  "The harm principle is only as strong as the utilitarian argument behind it. If liberty is protected because it maximises happiness, a majority sufficiently disturbed by a minority's way of life has a utilitarian case for suppressing it — and Devlin's popular moralism is the result."

  In your essay, say which of Mill's two justifications each argument relies on, and which form of utilitarianism. Draw on material from week 9 as well as week 10. Take a clear position, support it with reasons, and deal with the strongest counterargument to your view.
model: |
  **Thesis**

  The statement is wrong on both counts. It is wrong that the harm principle is only as strong as the utilitarian argument behind it, because Mill gives the principle two foundations, and the lecture placed the intrinsic value of liberty at the bottom with the utilitarian argument built on top. And it is wrong that a utilitarian case for suppression would give us Devlin, because Devlin's argument is not utilitarian at all: it appeals to social cohesion and to feelings of intolerance, indignation and disgust, not to a sum of happiness. What the statement does expose is that Mill's utilitarian argument, taken alone, holds against a disturbed majority only if it is read as a long-run, indirect argument rather than an act-utilitarian one.

  **Argument**

  *Two foundations.* Mill's principle allows power to be exercised over an adult against their will only "to prevent harm to others"; our own good, physical or moral, is not enough. The slides give two justifications. The first is the value of personal autonomy and the presumptive case for liberty: whatever we think about freedom, we agree it is of intrinsic value for the individual and for society. The second is utilitarian: following the harm principle brings maximum happiness in the long run. The lecture described the structure precisely. An intrinsic, non-instrumental value of liberty sits at the foundation, and a utilitarian argument about long-run consequences is built on top of it. On that reading the harm principle does not stand or fall with the utilitarian argument. If the long-run calculation failed in some case, the presumptive case for liberty would still demand a justification for coercion, and the majority's distress would have to supply it.

  *The utilitarian argument is not act-utilitarian.* Act-utilitarianism judges each action by its consequences. Mill's argument judges a principle by its consequences over time. Experiments in living are valuable to the whole society even though the majority may find them unsavoury or offensive: the argument anticipates the disturbed majority and treats its present distress as a cost worth paying for the options that experimentation produces. This is close to the structure that Waldron, on the lecture's account, calls indirect utilitarianism: for utilitarian reasons, we create structures in which officials are asked to refrain from utilitarian reasoning and to respect rights. The harm principle is such a structure. An official applying it does not ask whether suppressing this minority would increase happiness today; they ask whether the minority harms others.

  *Harm is defined by duties, not by distress.* Mill's harm is not merely affecting another's interests. It is infringing interests we are under a duty not to infringe: the violation of a specific duty to the public, or perceptible hurt to a specific individual. The lecture tied this to the link between interests, entitlements and rights, and on the interest theory a right protects an important interest (week 9). The majority's distress at a minority's way of life affects their interests, but no one is under a duty not to cause that distress by living differently. So the harm principle filters the majority's distress out before any calculation begins. Feinberg's offence principle confirms the point from the other side. Even where offence counts, it supports coercion only if it is a reaction expected from almost anyone chosen at random and cannot reasonably be avoided. A majority's disgust at a minority's private conduct fails both standards.

  *Devlin is not a utilitarian.* Devlin's case is that shared moral beliefs are necessary to society, so that without a shared morality the social order would break down; that the content of that morality is found in the intolerance, indignation and disgust of the man in the jury box; and that there are no more limits to legislation against immorality than to legislation against treason and sedition. None of this is a calculation of happiness. The statement's last step, from a utilitarian case for suppression to Devlin, therefore does not follow. Devlin and the disturbed majority may reach the same conclusion, but by different routes.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest objection is that the statement is right about the utilitarian argument even if it is wrong about Mill's case as a whole. The minority-religion example from week 9 shows that, in purely utilitarian terms, a sufficiently disturbed majority can justify banning a minority's practice. Mill's long-run argument is a prediction, and predictions can fail: if experiments in living produced little and distressed many, the utilitarian half of the case would turn against the minority. Indirect utilitarianism does not escape this, since a structure justified on utilitarian grounds is justified only so long as it pays. And the critics' objections to the harm principle, that it conceives of harm too narrowly, that no person's affairs affect only that person, and that it ignores indirect harms and kinds of offence that could become harm, suggest that the filter which keeps the majority's distress out is less secure than it looks.

  **Evaluation**

  The objection is correct about the utilitarian half, and it shows why Mill needed the other half. A purely utilitarian harm principle protects liberty contingently, and the week 9 test cases show what that contingency costs. That is why, on the week 9 account, a true commitment to human rights is incompatible with a certain type of utilitarian reasoning, and why the presumptive case for liberty matters. Once liberty has intrinsic value, the majority's distress is not simply weighed against the minority's happiness; it must justify an interference with something valuable in itself, and Mill's narrow conception of harm says what kind of reason can do that.

  The critics' objections are better read as demands to widen that conception than as a route to Devlin. Counting indirect harms, or offence that could become harm, still asks what the conduct does to others; it does not license enforcing morality as such. Hart's two questions to Devlin, whether the conduct is harmful independently of its repercussion on the general moral code, and whether failing to criminalise it would jeopardise the whole fabric of morality and society, are questions a careful utilitarian would also ask, and both point away from suppression.

  So the statement fails. The harm principle is stronger than its utilitarian argument because it does not rest on that argument alone; the utilitarian argument is stronger than the statement allows because it is indirect; and Devlin's popular moralism comes from a different premise altogether.
