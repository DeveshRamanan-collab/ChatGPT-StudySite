  In Orvania's south, Kallen Mining Ltd, a private company, employs a security firm that has beaten and shot at villagers protesting against the company's mine. Orvanian police were present on two occasions but did not intervene, and no one has been investigated.

  **Issue**

  The issues are: (1) whether the emergency decree is a valid derogation under ICCPR art 4, and whether it can justify each measure; (2) whether the ICCPR applies to the detention camp in Belka; (3) whether Orvania is responsible in relation to the security firm's violence; and (4) what weight the Human Rights Committee's views carry.

  **Rule**

  Under ICCPR art 4(1), in a public emergency which threatens the life of the nation and is officially proclaimed, a state may derogate "to the extent strictly required by the exigencies of the situation", provided the measures are consistent with its other international obligations and "do not involve discrimination solely on the ground of race, colour, sex, language, religion or social origin". Art 4(2) bars any derogation from arts 6 (life), 7 (prohibition on torture), 8(1)–(2), 11, 15, 16 and 18. A derogation must meet four requirements: necessity and proportionality (nature, duration, impact); consistency with other international obligations, especially jus cogens; no effect on non-derogable rights; and notification. In *Aksoy v Turkey* (para 78) holding a suspect for fourteen days without judicial intervention was "exceptionally long", left him vulnerable to torture, and was not shown to be necessary. Outside an emergency, a restriction must be provided by law, serve a legitimate aim, and be necessary in a democratic society.

  States must respect the rights of people within their territory and subject to their jurisdiction, irrespective of nationality (art 2(1)). Jurisdiction is primarily territorial but may be exercised abroad, and the drafters "did not intend to allow States to escape from their obligations when they exercise jurisdiction outside their national territory" (*Wall*, para 109); human rights instruments apply to acts done in the exercise of jurisdiction abroad, particularly in occupied territory (*Armed Activities*, para 216). States have horizontal obligations: their positive obligations "will only be fully discharged if individuals are protected by the State, not just against violations of Covenant rights by its agents, but also against acts committed by private persons or entities" (General Comment 31, para 8). Treaty body decisions are not binding but carry significant weight: the ICJ, though "in no way obliged" to follow the Committee, ascribes "great weight" to its interpretation (*Diallo*, para 66).

  **Application**

  *(1) The derogation.* Some conditions are met. A bombing campaign that has killed 40 people in the capital may be a public emergency threatening the life of the nation, and the decree was officially proclaimed in the Gazette. But Orvania has not notified any derogation, so it fails one of the four requirements, and the Attorney-General cannot treat the decree as a valid derogation. Even if notification were cured, each measure must be tested separately.

  *(a) 21-day detention.* Liberty and security (art 9) and rights in detention (art 10) are not listed in art 4(2), so they may be derogated from, but only to the extent strictly required. *Aksoy* treated fourteen days without judicial intervention as exceptionally long and not necessary; twenty-one days is longer still and, as in *Aksoy*, leaves detainees exposed to ill-treatment — a real risk given measure (c). Unless Orvania can give detailed reasons why judicial supervision is impracticable, the measure fails necessity and proportionality.

  *(b) The curfew.* Freedom of movement (art 12) is derogable, and a curfew may respond to night-time bombings. But this curfew applies only in districts identified by the language most residents speak. Art 4(1) forbids measures that discriminate solely on the ground of language. Orvania may argue that the districts were chosen because the TLA operates there, not because of language; it would need to show a security basis independent of the districts' Tarnic-speaking character. On the facts, the measure appears to target a linguistic minority, and it is unlikely to be strictly required when the bombings have struck government buildings, not those districts.

  *(c) "Enhanced pressure techniques".* Sleep deprivation for up to 96 hours and mock executions are designed to inflict severe suffering to extract information, and are very likely to amount to torture. Art 7 is non-derogable under art 4(2), so no emergency can justify them. The prohibition of torture is also customary international law and considered jus cogens, so these measures would also fail the requirement of consistency with Orvania's other international obligations. The first argument fails.

  *(2) Belka.* Orvania occupies the strip and runs the camp, so the villagers are subject to its jurisdiction even though they are outside its territory and are not its nationals (art 2(1)). Following *Wall* (para 109) and *Armed Activities* (para 216), the Covenant applies to acts done in the exercise of jurisdiction abroad, particularly in occupied territory. Denying detainees all contact with lawyers or family engages arts 9 and 10, and no derogation has been notified. The second argument fails.

  *(3) The security firm.* The firm is not a state organ, and nothing suggests it is empowered by law to exercise governmental authority (art 5 ARSIWA), so its conduct is probably not attributable to Orvania. But the Attorney-General's argument misses Orvania's own obligation. Under General Comment 31 (para 8) Orvania must protect people against private persons and entities, not only against its agents. Its police were present on two occasions and did not intervene, and no one has been investigated. The police are state organs, and their failure to act is an omission by Orvania itself. Orvania is responsible for failing to protect the protesters' rights to life (art 6) and to liberty and security (art 9), whatever the firm's own status.

  *(4) The Committee's views.* Orvania has accepted the individual complaints procedure, so detainees and protesters can bring written complaints, which the Committee decides in proceedings modelled on judicial practice and concludes with "views". The Attorney-General is right that views are not binding. But that is not the end of the matter: Orvania's obligations under the Covenant itself are binding, and the Committee is the body established to supervise the treaty, so its interpretation carries "great weight", as the ICJ held in *Diallo*. Ignoring the views would not make Orvania's conduct lawful. The organisation could also use Charter-based mechanisms: submissions to Orvania's Universal Periodic Review, and complaints to the relevant special procedures, although country visits require Orvania's consent.

  **Conclusion**

  The decree is not a valid derogation because Orvania has not notified it, and in any event the 21-day detention fails necessity and proportionality (*Aksoy*), the curfew appears to discriminate solely on the ground of language, and the interrogation techniques breach the non-derogable, jus cogens prohibition of torture. The Covenant applies in the occupied strip of Belka (*Wall*; *Armed Activities*). Orvania is responsible for its police's failure to protect the protesters (General Comment 31), whether or not the firm's conduct is attributable to it. The Committee's views are not binding, but they carry great weight (*Diallo*) and rest on obligations that do bind Orvania.
