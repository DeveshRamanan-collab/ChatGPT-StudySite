prompt: |
  Critical legal studies (CLS) offers a radical critique of law as a coherent and neutral system of rules, of legal institutions, and of liberal legal values such as the rule of law and the idea of rights. Drawing on legal realism, postmodernism and Marxism, its writers argue that the legal materials are radically indeterminate and yield no coherent sense; that law is contradictory because it stems from political conflict; that decisions are nonetheless predictable because judges share a commitment to the status quo; and that law is an ideology which promotes the interests of the powerful while presenting itself as neutral.

  Earlier theorists had already challenged the picture of law as a closed logical system. Holmes said that "the life of the law has not been logic; it has been experience", and the American realists argued that general rules cannot decide concrete cases. Hart accepted that general language is open-textured and that judges must choose in the penumbra, but called formalism and rule-scepticism the Scylla and Charybdis of juristic theory. Dworkin argued that even hard cases have right answers, because the principles of a community's political morality give the law its unity.

  Critically evaluate the following statement:

  "Law is politics by other means. The critical legal scholars simply finished the argument the realists started."

  In your essay, identify which CLS claims go beyond realism and which are shared with it, and test each against the replies available from Hart or Dworkin. Distinguish claims about what law is from claims about how judges decide and claims about what law does in society. Draw on material from more than one week of the unit. Take a clear position, support it with reasons, and deal with the strongest counterargument to your view.
model: |
  **Thesis**

  The statement is wrong on both counts. CLS did not simply finish the realists' argument: it changed it, from an empirical claim about how judges decide into a claim about the nature and social function of law, and it borrowed from postmodernism a theory of meaning the realists did not hold. And "law is politics by other means" is true only in a weak sense that Hart and Dworkin already accept — that political and moral choices enter the law, especially in hard cases. In the strong sense, that the legal materials neither guide nor constrain decisions, the claim is not established and undermines itself.

  **Argument**

  *What CLS shares with realism.* The realists attacked formalism: law as a self-contained system of rules from which results follow deductively, independent of moral, political, economic and social considerations. Their claims — that law in books differs from law in action, that law is indeterminate and general rules cannot decide concrete cases, that judges do or should decide by policy, that legal concepts are not neutral — are shared by CLS. Holmes' dictum that "the life of the law has not been logic; it has been experience" is common ground. So far the statement is right: CLS, like realism, holds that law is indeterminate and that legal reasoning does not constrain judges.

  *What goes beyond realism.* CLS goes further in three ways. First, its indeterminacy is more radical. The realists thought the materials often underdetermined results; CLS, drawing on postmodernism, says words do not have stable meaning and the materials yield no coherent sense at all. Secondly, its interest shifts from adjudication to ideology: law mirrors political visions, promotes the interests of the powerful and legitimises injustice, and — on the Marxist account — the legal superstructure helps the dominant class maintain the relations of production while presenting itself as neutral. Thirdly, CLS rejects the is/ought separation outright: the question of what law is cannot be separated from the question of whose interests it serves. The realists, on Leiter's reading, offered a descriptive theory of adjudication whose indeterminacy arguments presuppose a positivist account of the criteria of legality (293). CLS rejects that presupposition. It did not finish the realist argument; it replaced its foundations.

  *The weak sense is already conceded.* Hart accepts that general language is open-textured: "uncertainty at the borderline is the price to be paid for the use of general classifying terms" (CL 128), and in the penumbra judges make a choice between open alternatives. Dworkin goes further: judges in hard cases must engage with the principles of the community's political morality, and a theory of law must be normative as well as conceptual. If "law is politics" means that moral and political judgment enter adjudication, both would agree. The disagreement concerns the strong sense.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the statement is that Hart's and Dworkin's concessions prove more than they admit. If judges choose in the penumbra, and the penumbra expands whenever a litigant has resources to contest the core, then the line between easy and hard cases is itself drawn by those with power. Schauer's convergence thesis supports this: interpreters push rules towards standards by finding exceptions, and standards towards rules by concretising them, so the choice between specific and vague directives makes less difference than is assumed. *Church of the Holy Trinity v United States*, where a statute banning the importation of aliens to perform labour "of any kind" was read as not covering an English priest, shows that even clear words give way. And the predictability CLS concedes is exactly what one would expect if judges share a commitment to the status quo. On this view, the appearance of legal constraint is the ideology.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument shows that the boundary between core and penumbra is contestable and that political commitments shape legal outcomes. It does not show that the materials have no effect. Hart's point is that systems compromise between rules individuals can apply to themselves without fresh official guidance and issues left open for official choice (CL 130). The vast majority of legal transactions never reach a court because the rules are clear enough to be followed; the litigated cases from which realism and CLS draw their evidence are not a random sample. Schauer's convergence is also movement within a practice of reasoning from texts, and "a rule that ends with the word 'unless…' is still a rule" (CL 139). Implicit exceptions are defeasibility, not the absence of rules.

  The radical version also undermines itself. If words have no stable meaning, the CLS claims about domination and ideology have no stable meaning either. And the claim that judges share a commitment to the status quo is an empirical hypothesis about judicial behaviour; it needs the kind of evidence the realists sought, not a theory of meaning.

  Dworkin's integrity offers the sharpest reply to the ideological claim. It concedes that law is a matter of political morality, but insists that the principles judges apply must fit the community's past decisions and justify them. That is politics constrained by fidelity: arguments the losing side can test and criticise. Whether that ideal is realised is a fair question — CLS's critique of liberal legal values has force against a system that claims neutrality it does not possess — but it is a question about how well the legal system lives up to its standards, not proof that it has none.

  So the statement should be rejected. CLS changed the realist argument rather than finishing it, and "law is politics by other means" is either a platitude both Hart and Dworkin accept or a radical thesis that the evidence does not support.
