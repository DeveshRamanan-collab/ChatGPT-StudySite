model: |-
  The issue is whether there is a third exception to art 2(4) for humanitarian intervention. Art 2(4) prohibits the
  threat or use of force, and the Charter provides only two exceptions: Security Council authorisation (art 42) and
  self-defence against an armed attack (art 51). Force to protect people in another state from gross and systematic
  human rights violations is not addressed in the Charter and appears to conflict with art 2(4). The debate came to the
  fore after the failures in Rwanda (1994) and Srebrenica (1995). In Kosovo (1999) NATO acted without authorisation; the
  UK argued legality "as an exceptional measure on grounds of overwhelming humanitarian necessity", but a draft
  resolution called the force a flagrant violation of arts 2(4), 24 and 53, and the Independent Commission called it
  "illegal, yet legitimate". R2P (ICISS 2001; World Summit 2005) reframed the issue as a responsibility to protect
  populations from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity, passing to the international
  community when a state is unwilling or unable — but its third pillar is a response through UN mechanisms, so it works
  within the Charter. It does not solve Security Council paralysis, is selectively applied and is open to abuse, as
  Libya (2011) showed. Conclusion: no clear right exists; unilateral humanitarian intervention remains a "legal grey
  area" (ILA 2018), and lawful force still needs Security Council authorisation.
