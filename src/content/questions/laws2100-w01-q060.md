model: |
  The claim is overstated: jurisprudence asks at least six kinds of questions, and only one is normative. Doctrinal
  questions ask what the law is on a specific issue; normative questions, drawing on the is/ought distinction, ask
  what ought to be and are either justificatory or critical; empirical questions make explanatory/causal or effects
  claims; metatheoretical questions ask for a theory's success criteria; methodological questions ask how research
  should be conducted; and conceptual questions ask what law, contract or justice is. Hart claims a theory that is
  descriptive — morally neutral, with no justificatory aims — which would be impossible if every question were
  normative. The best counterargument is that choosing criteria to judge theories, or deciding which features of law
  matter, may involve evaluation; Finnis thinks legal judgments cannot be assessed without premises about true human
  goods. But evaluation in metatheory is not the same as a normative claim about how law ought to be. On balance the
  kinds of questions remain distinct, though they interact, and an essay must say which one it is answering.
