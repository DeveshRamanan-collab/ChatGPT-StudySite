  **Issue**

  Swift Parcels accepts that it is liable for Daniel's negligence in the collision, so duty and breach are not in issue. The questions are how far that liability extends: (1) whether Swift is liable for the full hip fracture and income loss, given Ines's brittle bones and high earnings; (2) whether Dr Hale's missed diagnosis, Ines's decision to go without crutches, or the falling tree takes any later harm outside the scope of its liability; and (3) whether Swift can recover a contribution from Dr Hale.

  **Rule**

  Under the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") s 5D(1), causation has two elements: (a) factual causation — the negligence was a necessary condition of the harm — and (b) scope of liability — it is appropriate for the negligent person's liability to extend to the harm so caused. Under s 5D(4) the court considers whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed on the negligent party. This embraces policy considerations the court must articulate (Wallace v Kam) and normative questions about whether the defendant ought to be held liable (Ruddock v Taylor). Scope of liability raises two concepts: remoteness and new intervening acts.

  Remoteness asks whether the kind of damage was reasonably foreseeable (The Wagon Mound (No 1)) — a "real risk" that a reasonable person would not brush aside as far-fetched (The Wagon Mound (No 2)). The extent of the harm and the precise manner of its occurrence need not be foreseeable (Hughes v Lord Advocate). Under the egg-shell skull rule the defendant must take the plaintiff "with all his weaknesses, beliefs and reactions as well as his capacities and attributes, physical, social and economic" (Nader v Urban Transit Authority of NSW).

  A new intervening act breaks the chain if it is "ultroneous, something unwarrantable, a new cause which disturbs the sequence of events", something "unreasonable or extraneous or extrinsic" (The Oropesa). Ordinarily it must be voluntary, volitional human action, or a causally independent coincidence that is extremely unlikely (Haber v Walker). Volitional conduct severed the chain in Rickards v Lothian, Yates v Jones and CES v Superclinics (Aust) Pty Ltd. Where negligence is followed by later negligence, whether the chain is severed is a matter of "fact and degree" (Mahony v J Kruschich (Demolitions) Pty Ltd); later negligence by a third party or the plaintiff does not necessarily sever it (Burns v Pearce), especially where the defendant's conduct generated the very risk and the injury occurred in the ordinary course of things (March v Stramare).

  Where tortfeasors are jointly and severally liable, the plaintiff may recover the whole amount from either; the one who pays may seek contribution from the other (Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1946 (NSW) s 5(1)(c)), and the risk of the other's insolvency lies with the tortfeasors, not the plaintiff.

  **Application**

  *Factual causation throughout.* But for Daniel's negligence Ines would not have been injured, treated, put on crutches or admitted to hospital, so each later harm satisfies s 5D(1)(a). Every question below is one of scope of liability under s 5D(1)(b) and (4).

  *The hip fracture and income loss.* Physical injury to a cyclist is the obvious kind of harm risked by reversing blind into a bicycle lane. That Ines's fracture was worse than an average rider's goes only to extent, which need not be foreseeable (Hughes v Lord Advocate). The egg-shell skull rule is decisive: Swift takes Ines with her brittle bones and with her capacities and attributes, including economic ones, so it is liable for the whole fracture and for her high lost earnings (Nader).

  *The missed pelvic fracture.* Dr Hale was negligent, but later negligence is weighed, not treated as an automatic break (Mahony). Daniel's negligence created the very situation in which Ines needed hospital diagnosis, and a tired registrar's imperfect diagnosis is not "extraneous or extrinsic" to that situation (The Oropesa); the displacement then happened in the ordinary course of things as she walked at home (March v Stramare). It was neither a deliberate act nor an extremely unlikely coincidence. Under s 5D(4), responsibility for the displacement, the second operation and the extra four months should be imposed on Swift. Dr Hale is also liable for that additional harm.

  *The wedding fall.* This is the hardest point. On one view it is the plaintiff's own carelessness, generated by her injury — later negligence that does not necessarily sever the chain (Burns v Pearce), better dealt with as contributory negligence under CLA s 5R, judged by the standard of a reasonable person in Ines's position on what she knew. On the better view, though, it was not mere carelessness: Ines made a deliberate, informed choice to disobey clear, written medical instructions for a trivial reason. That is volitional conduct of the kind that severed the chain in the Rickards v Lothian, Yates v Jones and CES group, and it is "unreasonable" in the Oropesa sense. Articulating the policy, as s 5D(4) and Wallace v Kam require, it is hard to say why Swift ought to answer for a re-fracture that Ines chose to risk against advice (Ruddock v Taylor). The re-fracture is probably outside the scope of Swift's liability; at the least, any damages for it would be heavily reduced for contributory negligence.

  *The collarbone.* A tree falling through a hospital roof in a freak storm is causally independent of the collision and an extremely unlikely coincidence (Haber v Walker). Daniel's negligence explains only why Ines was in that bed, not why the tree fell. The chain is severed, and the collarbone is outside the scope of Swift's liability.

  *Contribution from Dr Hale.* For the displaced fracture, Swift and Dr Hale are concurrent tortfeasors liable for the same damage. Ines may recover the whole of that harm from Swift, which may then seek contribution from Dr Hale under the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1946 (NSW) s 5(1)(c) and s 5(2). That is a matter between the tortfeasors alone: it does not reduce what Ines recovers, and if Dr Hale could not pay, the loss would fall on Swift, not Ines. Swift has no basis for contribution towards the original hip fracture, which Dr Hale did not cause.

  **Conclusion**

  Swift Parcels is liable for the hip fracture in full, including Ines's high lost earnings, because it takes her as it finds her (Nader). It is also liable for the displaced pelvic fracture and the second operation: Dr Hale's later negligence arose in the ordinary course from the situation Daniel created and does not sever the chain (Mahony; March v Stramare), though Swift may claim contribution from Dr Hale for that harm. It is probably not liable for the wedding re-fracture, which followed Ines's deliberate, informed decision to ignore medical advice — or, if a court treats it as mere carelessness, her damages for it would be substantially reduced. It is not liable for the collarbone, caused by an extremely unlikely, causally independent coincidence (Haber v Walker).
