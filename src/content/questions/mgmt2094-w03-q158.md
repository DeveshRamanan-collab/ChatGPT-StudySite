model: |-
  Human-centred design rests on the premise that a deep understanding of users' unarticulated needs, gained through
  empathy, inspires innovation — and unarticulated needs are exactly what asking misses. Henry Ford's line makes the
  point: asked what they wanted, people would have said faster horses.

  The reading distinguishes affective empathy — mirroring a family's distress — from cognitive empathy, perspective
  taking: understanding their worldview, fears and experiences. The hospital needs the second. Empathetic design means
  observing families in the waiting room through the night, closer to anthropology than to a satisfaction survey. Temple
  Grandin shows the power of this: watching animals without assumptions, she saw that straight paths caused panic and
  designed curved corrals.

  The team must also watch for the four roadblocks: idealism (seeing what they expect), egoism (what they want), realism
  (assuming they see things as they are) and circumstantialism (thinking only about what is in front of them — for
  example, ignoring what families went through before arriving).

  So the statement is too simple. Asking is a start, not the method. Verganti goes further, warning against engaging
  customers too much because they rarely see far-future possibilities; the hospital should combine observation and
  perspective taking with its own judgement.
