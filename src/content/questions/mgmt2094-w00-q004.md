  **Thesis**

  The statement mistakes one part of the leader's job for the whole of it. The unit's materials treat innovation as something that can, and must, be managed: managing innovation "is all about creating firm-specific routines", and the innovative leader is defined by active traits, not by absence. What leaders should get out of the way of is the work of the people who innovate — their ideas, their experiments and the decisions teams are better placed to make. They cannot get out of the way of building the culture, setting direction and removing barriers, because no one else will do those things.

  **Argument**

  *Culture has to be built.* An innovation culture shows organisation-wide commitment to the business and to innovation, experiments proactively, manages risk, can implement ideas quickly, and produces and cultivates innovators. Each characteristic has a matching trait in the leader: passionate about innovation, willing to experiment, realistic and informed, capable of rapid project execution, and able to attract and nurture innovators. The slides contrast a culture that cultivates innovators — managers allow ideas to be heard, innovators have space to develop and test, innovation is rewarded — with one that does not, where failure is discouraged or punished, employees are reluctant to bring up ideas, and innovation is top-down. Getting out of the way removes the top-down control, but allowing ideas to be heard and rewarding innovation are things managers do. Shelley Simpson expects her team to "lean in", and rewards those who do.

  *Managing innovation is managing routines.* The seven challenges of managing innovation include building an innovation culture — creating firm-specific routines, repeated and reinforced patterns of behaviour — and continuous learning, because firms must keep developing those routines as the environment changes. The components for overcoming barriers begin with shared vision, leadership and the will to innovate: a clearly articulated purpose, stretching strategic intent and top management commitment. They also include key individuals such as champions, effective team working, a creative climate supported by motivation systems, and a learning organisation. The slides' conclusion asks enterprises for "planning and controlling systems with a high degree of flexibility", respect for individual initiative, and tolerance for mistakes. Flexible control is still control.

  *Leaders build creative capacity.* Carmeli, Gelbard and Reiter-Palmon found that leader supportive behaviours — modelling collaboration and knowledge sharing, and encouraging information exchange and openness — were related to employees' creative problem-solving capacity, directly and through knowledge sharing. Robert Buckman, chief executive of Buckman Laboratories, modelled this himself in lengthy online discussions with salespeople about compensation. That is leadership by involvement, not withdrawal.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest support for the statement is that intrusive leaders kill innovation. Iger shifted decision-making from a central strategic planning division to Disney's business units and gave each its own chief technology officer, so they could "experiment and try even if they fail … without a corporate watchdog looking over their shoulder all the time". Rigby, Sutherland and Takeuchi found that agile stalls when executives launch countless initiatives, become overly involved in the work of teams, talk more than listen, overturn team decisions and add review layers. Week 1's team competencies point the same way: without psychological safety, people hold back their ideas for fear of being seen as stupid or incompetent.

  **Evaluation**

  These examples show leaders stepping back from the work, not from management. Iger also set Disney's three pillars, made big and early bets such as the iTunes deal, and created a CTO council that runs hackathons and a "Best of Disney" symposium — designing the structure within which others could experiment. Rigby's six practices ask leaders to learn how agile works, to practise it in their own teams and to destroy the barriers to it; at Mission Bell Winery the executive agile team ranked an enterprise backlog and protected the organisation from pet projects. Dow Corning shows the cost of under-managing change: a consultant-designed commercialisation process failed because poor change management practices led to poor take-up. And at Gilbert + Tobin, Sam Nickless judged that the incentives and culture for a low-cost offshoot would be very hard to create beside a traditional legal model, so the firm bought a stake in LegalVision instead — a managed decision about where innovation could live.

  So the statement should be rejected, though its second sentence contains a truth. Leaders should get out of the way of ideas and of the teams that develop them. But innovation can be managed, and has to be: through a shared vision, routines that keep learning, a culture that rewards experiment and tolerates failure, and the removal of barriers that only leaders can remove.
