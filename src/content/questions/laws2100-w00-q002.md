  In 'The Path of the Law' (1897) Holmes told lawyers to look at the law through the eyes of the bad man, who cares only about the practical consequences of breaking the rules: "The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law." The American realists who followed him attacked formalism — the picture of judges deducing results from a self-contained system of rules — and argued that judges respond to the underlying facts of cases, reaching results on other grounds and then finding doctrine to support them.

  **Thesis**

  The statement is correct in its first sentence and overstated in its second. Once realism is read, as Leiter reads it, as a descriptive theory of adjudication — a claim about why judges decide as they do — it no longer competes with legal positivism, which is a general theory of what law is. Hart's arguments in chapter VII defeat a conceptual rule-scepticism that the best realists need not hold. But the realist answer to the question of adjudication is not more convincing than Hart allowed. Hart's own account of open texture already concedes that judges choose in the penumbra; what remains between the two is an empirical question about how often legal rules decide cases, and on that question the realists exaggerate.

  **Argument**

  *Two scepticisms, two questions.* Conceptual rule-scepticism is a claim about the nature of law: the real law is not the "paper rules" but what courts will do, so we are under a legal duty not to do X if and only if doing X is likely to result in an adverse court ruling. Empirical rule-scepticism is a claim about adjudication: judges respond to the underlying facts, there is support in the materials for both sides, and doctrine is cherry-picked to reach results reached on other grounds — Frank's idiosyncratic judge, Llewellyn's shared background of judges as a class, Cohen's policy consequences. One can be an empirical rule-sceptic without embracing conceptual rule-scepticism: rules may exist, but judges do not use them to decide cases.

  *Hart defeats the conceptual claim.* Holmes' formula — "The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law" (460–461) — is a conceptual claim, and Hart's objections to it are strong. It misses the internal point of view of participants who treat rules as common standards. It makes it impossible to say that a court has made a mistake about the law, since whatever the court does is the law. It privileges the bad man over the puzzled person who wants to arrange their affairs according to law. And if there are no legal rules, we cannot say who a judge is: only secondary rules of adjudication make someone's decision a court's decision.

  *Leiter: realism is a theory of adjudication.* Leiter's reply is that Hart construed realism wrongly as a rival theory of law. The realists' core claims are that judges react primarily to the facts of the case, that decisions fall into patterns of situation types which allow prediction, and that law is rationally indeterminate, so the question is what the real, non-legal reasons are. Those are empirical claims. More strikingly, "the Realists … cannot be conceptual rule skeptics, because their arguments for the indeterminacy of law presuppose a nonskeptical account of the criteria of legality, one that has the most obvious affinities with that developed by hard or exclusive Positivists" (293). To say that the legal materials fail to determine a result, one must first know which materials count as legal. Realism needs something like a rule of recognition. On this reading, the first sentence of the statement is right: positivism says what law is; realism says how much it does.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest counterargument is that Leiter's reading is too charitable. Holmes did not say that the law has little causal effect; he said that the law *is* a prophecy of what courts will do. That is a theory of law, and a rival to Hart's. Hart's lecture on American jurisprudence adds that the realists presented judicial law-making "not simply as a feature of difficult adjudication" but as an essential feature of law and adjudication — the Nightmare that the image of the judge applying existing law is an illusion. The Scandinavian branch went further: Ross treated "ownership" in 'Tû-Tû' as having no semantic reference at all. If the realists themselves made conceptual claims, the rivalry is not Hart's invention.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument shows that some realists made conceptual claims; it does not show that realism needs them. Realism is an umbrella term for a family of claims, and the best case for realism is the one that keeps the empirical claims and drops the conceptual one, since only the empirical claims survive Hart's objections. So the first sentence of the statement stands, provided we say which realism we mean.

  The second sentence is weaker. Leiter himself puts the remaining disagreement as a question of degree: both think law is indeterminate, with different sources and scope, and they disagree over how often legal rules matter in decisions — whether rules in books play a causal role. Hart's answer is careful. Language has a core and a penumbra: "If anything is a vehicle a motor-car is one", but bicycles, aeroplanes and roller skates are unclear (CL 126). "Uncertainty at the borderline is the price to be paid for the use of general classifying terms" (CL 128), and in the penumbra judges must choose. But not every case is in the penumbra. Legal systems compromise between rules individuals can apply to themselves and issues left open for official choice (CL 130). That is why "formalism and rule-scepticism are the Scylla and Charybdis of juristic theory; they are great exaggerations, salutary where they correct each other, and the truth lies between them" (CL 147).

  The realists' own evidence comes from litigated, often appellate, cases — Hart notes American jurisprudence's concentration "almost to the point of obsession" on the judicial process, explained partly by the extraordinary role of US courts. Litigated cases are exactly those in which the rules ran out or were contested. The countless transactions ordered by rules that never reach court are invisible from that vantage point. The puzzled person is not merely a moral alternative to the bad man; she is evidence that rules guide conduct most of the time.

  The better conclusion, then, is that realism and positivism are compatible, and that each corrects the other at the margin. Realism is a valuable reminder that the penumbra is larger in some fields, such as constitutional adjudication, than formalists admitted. It is not a more convincing account of adjudication in general than Hart's middle way.
