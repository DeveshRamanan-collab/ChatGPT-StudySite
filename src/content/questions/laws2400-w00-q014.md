The Council's written playground policy requires a safety inspection of every playground each quarter.

The sheet went to the Council's works depot, where repairs were scheduled in the order requests arrived. The depot manager says the year's playground maintenance budget had been spent by May. No warning sign was put up and no barrier tape was placed around the frame, although either would have taken a worker a few minutes and cost very little.

  Two years earlier, the Council had adopted a ten-year plan to replace all 46 of its playgrounds built before 2000, starting with the busiest parks. Wattle Park was scheduled for year seven.

  In August, Rosa took her six-year-old son, Leo, to Wattle Park. She sat on a bench about fifteen metres from the frame, answering work emails on her phone. Leo climbed to the top platform. As he stepped onto it, the corroded bolt sheared, the platform tipped, and Leo fell onto the matting, fracturing his wrist and elbow. Nothing about the bolt was visible from the ground.

  Leo sues the Council in negligence. The Council says that it owed Leo no duty; that the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) protects it because it had no money left for repairs and a sensible replacement plan; and that in any event Rosa, who was "glued to her phone", should contribute to any damages it has to pay.

  Advise Leo on his claim against the Council, and advise the Council on its claim against Rosa.

model: |
  **Issue**

  Three questions arise: (1) whether Greenvale Shire Council, a public authority, owed Leo a duty of care in respect of the climbing frame; (2) whether the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") — Part 5, and ss 5B and 5D — defeats or limits his claim; and (3) whether the Council can obtain contribution from Rosa.

  **Rule**

  Public authorities have no Crown immunity and can be sued in negligence, but they differ from private defendants because they act under statute and because courts weigh resources and indeterminate liability; reasonable foreseeability is always needed but never enough. In Sutherland Shire Council v Heyman (1985) 157 CLR 424 the High Court considered the distinction between policy and operations: high-level policy is largely off limits, while negligence in carrying policy out is more arguable. Control is "of fundamental importance" (Gummow and Hayne JJ in Graham Barclay Oysters v Ryan (2002) 211 CLR 540), and it includes control of any situation containing a risk of harm to others (Amaca v NSW [2004] NSWCA 124). An occupier — whoever controls the land — owes a duty to lawful entrants.

  CLA Part 5 modifies the position. Under s 42, an authority's functions are limited by the resources reasonably available; the general allocation of those resources is not open to challenge; its functions are judged by the broad range of its activities; and it may rely on compliance with general procedures. Sections 43 and 43A impose a "so unreasonable" threshold for breach of statutory duty and for special statutory powers; s 44 limits liability for failing to regulate an activity; and s 46 says exercising a function does not of itself create a duty to exercise it.

  Breach is decided under s 5B: a foreseeable, not insignificant risk, and precautions a reasonable person in the defendant's position would have taken, considering probability, seriousness, burden and social utility (s 5B(2)). Causation requires factual causation and scope of liability (s 5D(1)).

  A tortfeasor held liable for the whole damage may seek contribution from another tortfeasor (Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1946 (NSW) s 5(1)(c)). A parent or carer owes no general duty to protect a child from every danger, but may be liable if the child is led into danger by their actions (Hahn v Conley (1971) 126 CLR 276).

  **Application**

  *Duty.* The Council owns, manages and inspects the playground: it controls the very structure that created the risk. Children using a public playground are lawful entrants who cannot inspect a bolt hidden under a platform, so they are vulnerable, and the Council knew of the specific danger from its own inspection sheet. The complaint must be framed carefully. If Leo argued that the Council should have replaced the frame sooner or budgeted more for repairs, he would be attacking policy — the ten-year plan and the annual allocation of funds — which is largely off limits. His real complaint is operational: having found an "URGENT" defect, the Council left the frame open with no sign or barrier. On that framing a duty is owed.

  *CLA Part 5.* The Council is a public authority (s 41). It is right that s 42(a) and (b) protect its budget and its replacement plan: a court cannot tell it how to allocate limited funds, just as the argument in Romeo v Conservation Commission (NT) (1998) 192 CLR 431 that a coastline should have been fenced failed partly for that reason. Section 42(d) also lets it point to its quarterly inspections. But none of this answers the claim as framed. Taping off a frame or putting up a sign costs almost nothing and was possible within any budget; and the inspection procedure worked — it found the danger — while the failure lay in doing nothing with the result. Section 43 does not apply, because Leo sues in negligence, not for breach of statutory duty. Nor does s 43A: as with the protective screens in RTA v Refrigerated Roadways [2009] NSWCA 263, closing off a frame is not a special statutory power — anybody can do it. Section 44 concerns failures to regulate an activity, which this is not, and s 46 is no answer because the duty rests on the Council's control and knowledge as occupier, not on the bare fact that it inspects.

  *Breach.* Under s 5B(1)(a) the risk of a child falling from an unstable platform was not merely one the Council ought to have known of — it actually knew. The risk was not insignificant (s 5B(1)(b)): a bolt recorded as "badly corroded" under a platform that "moves under load" presents a real risk (Bolton v Stone), and is certainly not far-fetched or fanciful (Wyong Shire Council v Shirt). Under s 5B(2), the probability of harm was high given daily use by children (para (a)); the likely seriousness of a two-metre fall included fractures or a head injury (para (b)); the burden of a sign or tape was a few minutes' work (para (c)); and closing one frame for a while barely reduced the social utility of the playground (para (d)). A reasonable authority in the Council's position would have taken those precautions (s 5B(1)(c)). The Council breached its duty.

  *Causation.* Under s 5D(1)(a), the failure was a necessary condition of Leo's injuries: had the frame been closed off, he would not have been on the platform when the bolt sheared. As to scope of liability (s 5D(1)(b), (4)), a child falling from that platform is precisely the harm the inspector's warning foresaw, so responsibility should be imposed.

  *Contribution from Rosa.* Contribution is a matter between tortfeasors, so the Council must show that Rosa was herself liable to Leo for the same damage. On Hahn v Conley, a parent owes no general duty to protect a child from every danger, though she may be liable if her actions lead the child into danger. Rosa took a six-year-old to a public playground built for children; she did not lead him into danger, and the danger — a hidden, corroded bolt — was one she could not see. Even if she owed and breached a duty by not watching, the but-for test defeats the claim: watching from the bench, or standing at the foot of the frame, would not have stopped the bolt failing. Hahn v Conley is a close parallel: there the defendant driver's claim to an indemnity from the grandfather depended on the child having a cause of action against him, and she did not. In any event, contribution would never affect Leo, who can recover his whole damages from the Council.

  **Conclusion**

  The Council owed Leo a duty as the authority controlling the playground. Framed as an operational failure to take cheap interim precautions, not an attack on the budget or replacement plan, his claim survives s 42 and the rest of Part 5. The Council breached s 5B and caused Leo's injuries under s 5D, so he should recover his damages in full. The Council's contribution claim against Rosa should fail: on Hahn v Conley she owed no duty to protect Leo from a hidden defect she did not create, and her inattention made no causal difference.
