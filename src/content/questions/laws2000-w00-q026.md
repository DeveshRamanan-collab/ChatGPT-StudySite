prompt: Explain the difference between the declaratory theory and the constitutive theory of state recognition in international law. Which approach is more consistent with contemporary international law, and why? In your answer, refer to at least one example of contested statehood to illustrate the practical significance of this debate.
model: |-
  Recognition is the act by which a state officially acknowledges the existence of another state. Under the declaratory theory, recognition only acknowledges a pre-existing statehood: an entity meeting the Montevideo criteria — population, territory, effective government and capacity for relations — is a state whether or not others recognise it, so recognition is symbolic. Under the constitutive theory, recognition creates the state and confers legal personality: no recognition, no existence.

  The declaratory theory is the most widely held today and fits contemporary law better. Recognition mixes politics and law: states recognise entities that fail the criteria and refuse ones that meet them. If recognition created states, statehood would depend on politics, and an entity could be a state for some states but not for others.

  Recognition still matters. Non-recognition may prevent statehood where the criteria were achieved illegally (Friendly Relations Declaration 1970; *Kosovo* Advisory Opinion). The Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus meets the criteria, but Security Council resolutions 541 and 550 called its declaration "legally invalid" after Turkey's 1974 invasion, and only Turkey recognises it. Kosovo and Palestine remain contested.
