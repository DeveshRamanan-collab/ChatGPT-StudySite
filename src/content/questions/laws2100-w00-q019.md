  In 1957 the Wolfenden report recommended that "homosexual behaviour between consenting adults in private should no longer be a criminal offence". In his 1959 Maccabean Lecture, 'Morals and the Criminal Law', Lord Devlin disagreed. If behaviour is immoral by common standard, he argued, the law-maker has a prima facie right to prohibit it, even if it causes no harm or offence to the actor or to others. Shared moral beliefs are necessary to society: without a shared morality the social order would break down. The content of that morality is found by asking the man in the jury box, whose moral judgments are a matter of common sense and feelings of intolerance, indignation and disgust; and there are no limits to legislation against immorality, as there are no limits to legislation against treason and sedition.

  Hart replied in 'Immorality and Treason' (1959) and *Law, Liberty and Morality* (1963). "The most remarkable feature of Sir Patrick's lecture", he wrote, "is his view of the nature of morality – the morality which the criminal law may enforce". He asked whether conduct that offends moral feelings is harmful, independently of its repercussion on the general moral code, and whether it is true that failing to criminalise it would jeopardise the whole fabric of morality and society. Robert George later agreed with Devlin that the law should prohibit vice even if the conduct is harmless, but held that the standards it enforces should come from critical morality, since the majority's moral beliefs may not be sound.

  "Hart's reply shows only that Devlin chose the wrong morality. Replace popular morality with critical morality, as George does, and the case for enforcing morality through the criminal law stands."

  **Thesis**

  The statement is half right. A large part of Hart's reply is aimed at Devlin's account of what morality is: a compound of intolerance, indignation and disgust, read off the imagined reactions of the man in the jury box. George's critical legal moralism is built to avoid exactly that objection, because it enforces true or objective morality rather than the majority's beliefs. But the statement is wrong that nothing else remains. Hart's two questions and his deeper criticism are aimed at a second step, which Devlin and George share: the step from "this conduct is immoral" to "this conduct may be punished by the criminal law". Changing the morality does not repair that step. The case for enforcing morality through the criminal law therefore does not stand merely by moving from popular to critical morality.

  **Argument**

  *Devlin at his strongest.* Devlin was answering the Wolfenden report's recommendation that "homosexual behaviour between consenting adults in private should no longer be a criminal offence". His central claim is that if conduct is immoral by common standard, the law-maker has a prima facie right to prohibit it, even if it causes no harm or offence to the actor or to others. He reaches it through three questions. Has society the right to pass judgement on moral matters? If so, may it use the weapon of the law to enforce its judgements? And should it do so in all cases, or only in some? He answers the first two with an argument about social cohesion: shared moral beliefs are necessary to society, and without a shared morality the social order would break down. The third is practical. Adultery and bigamy are both wrong on his view, but only bigamy, because marriage is regulated and recorded, can feasibly be enforced. Devlin adds that the criminal law as it stands already punishes harmless conduct; the prohibition of voluntary euthanasia, which protects the sanctity of human life, is his example. Hence his analogy: there are no limits to legislation against immorality, just as there are none to legislation against treason and sedition.

  *Two questions, not one.* Every legal moralist must answer two different questions: which morality the law enforces, and whether immorality is in itself a sufficient reason to criminalise. Devlin and George give the same answer to the second, yes, and differ only on the first. Devlin enforces popular morality, what a society conventionally regards as right; George enforces critical morality, what is objectively right. The statement is therefore correct only if Hart's reply is directed at the first question alone.

  *The part of Hart's reply that George escapes.* Hart's first move is not to restate the harm principle. Devlin rejects that principle, so starting there would only set one premise against its opposite. Instead Hart turns to Devlin's concept of morality: "The most remarkable feature of Sir Patrick's lecture is his view of the nature of morality – the morality which the criminal law may enforce". Moral judgments sit between rationality and feelings, and Hart doubts that morality is a compound of intolerance, indignation and disgust, even when it takes the form of "a real feeling of reprobation". His deeper criticism makes the same point: it must be possible to assess rationally, with reason, sympathetic understanding and critical intelligence, the claim that immoral conduct should be punished, and to ask whether the general morality rests on ignorance, superstition or misunderstanding. George accepts the force of this. Because the majority's beliefs may not be sound, he looks to critical morality instead. On this part of the reply, the statement is right.

  *The part George does not escape.* Hart also asks two questions of any proposal to criminalise conduct that offends moral feelings. Is the conduct harmful, independently of its repercussion on the general moral code? And is it true that failing to criminalise it would jeopardise the whole fabric of morality and society? These questions are not about the source of moral judgments; they are about whether immorality alone justifies coercion. Hart answers the second by denying that morality is a seamless web and by rejecting the comparison between suppressing sexual immorality and suppressing treason. His minimum content of natural law (week 4) shows how little shared morality a society needs to hold together: rules restricting violence and protecting property and promises are "a natural necessity" (CL 199), but that is a very thin morality compared with the rich one Devlin assumes. And Hart's last question, whether the misery and evil consequences of criminal punishment are well understood, applies whatever morality is enforced. A true moral standard enforced by imprisonment still imposes the suffering of imprisonment on the people it catches.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest objection is that George does not need Devlin's social-cohesion argument at all. Devlin defends enforcement because society must protect its own existence; George defends it because the law should protect our moral character by prohibiting vice, even if the conduct is harmless. If that is the ground, Hart's second question (would society collapse?) misses the target, and his first question (is the conduct harmful?) assumes the liberal answer to the very question in dispute. The legal moralist can add that the liberal is not neutral either. Mill's harm principle rests on the value of personal autonomy and the presumptive case for liberty, the claim that freedom is of intrinsic value. That too is a claim of critical morality. On this view the real disagreement is between two critical moralities, and Hart has not shown that his should prevail.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument is right that Hart's social-cohesion objections fall away against George. But it also shows what is left once they do. The question is no longer which morality to enforce but whether criminal punishment is a justified means of making people good, and here Hart's question about the misery of punishment, and his insistence that the claim be open to rational assessment, still apply. The rationale for legal paternalism makes the same demand when it asks whether there is a less risky way of reaching the goal: show that measures short of public coercion would not do. Raz shows that the demand can be met without giving up George's concern with the good. Raz accepts that the state should promote intrinsically worthy ways of life and discourage unworthy ones, yet denies that it should use the criminal law to pursue perfectionist ideals; it may promote virtue by non-coercive means, such as taxing activities of which the majority disapproves. So one can share George's premise about the good and still reject his conclusion about the criminal law.

  The statement is therefore half right. Replacing popular with critical morality answers Hart's objection to Devlin's account of how we know what morality requires, but it leaves untouched the objection that immorality alone does not justify the criminal law. The case for legal moralism does not stand; it has only been moved to the ground on which Hart and the liberal tradition are strongest.
