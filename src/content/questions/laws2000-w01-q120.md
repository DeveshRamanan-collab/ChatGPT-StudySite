prompt: Louis Henkin wrote that 'almost all nations observe almost all principles of international law and almost all of their obligations almost all of the time'. Using this week's compliance material, explain why this is true despite international law having no legislature, executive or police.
model: |
  Henkin's observation is true precisely because international law does not need a domestic-style
  legislature, executive or police to secure compliance — it relies on different mechanisms. First,
  compliance rests on something more basic than enforcement: as Hoffman (1987) put it, 'the basis of
  obligation is the same in every legal order: a consciousness among the subjects that this order is
  needed if one is to reach a common end', and enforcement is 'irrelevant to the binding quality of
  international law as a system of law' in the first place. Second, where a more concrete incentive is
  needed, reputation supplies it: Chayes & Chayes (1995) argue that compliance comes 'from fear not of
  sanction but "loss of reputation"', because other states will exclude a habitually non-complying state
  from future cooperation — from trade partnerships to alliances. Third, there is quasi-enforcement:
  continued participation in international organisations is conditional on trust, so Arts 5-6 of the UN
  Charter allow suspension or expulsion from membership, and bodies like the IMF and World Bank can apply
  economic leverage. Taken together, these three mechanisms — shared necessity, reputational cost, and
  quasi-enforcement — explain widespread compliance without any domestic-style enforcer, while also
  explaining why the occasional, well-publicised violation (use of force, occupation, disrespect of
  treaties) does not disprove Henkin's claim: just as domestic crime does not destroy a domestic legal
  system, these violations sit alongside, rather than replace, the underlying pattern of compliance.
