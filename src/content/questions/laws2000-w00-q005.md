Brenmark, whose own nationals are not affected, wishes to bring a claim against Oldavia.

  **Issue**

  Five issues arise: (1) whether Oldavia's "Interpretative Declaration" is in substance a reservation; (2) whether it is permissible, and how it affects Oldavia's relations with Brenmark and Marisol; (3) the meaning of "taking" in Article 4 and the use of the negotiating records; (4) whether Oldavia's conduct breaches Article 4 and is attributable to it; and (5) whether Brenmark can invoke Oldavia's responsibility.

  **Rule**

  Under art 2(1)(d) VCLT, a reservation is a unilateral statement, "however phrased or named", made on ratification, that "purports to exclude or to modify the legal effect of certain provisions of the treaty in their application to that State". An interpretative declaration merely explains a state's understanding without conditioning its consent. Article 19 bars a reservation that the treaty prohibits, that falls outside a list of specified permitted reservations, or that is incompatible with the treaty's object and purpose. Only a permissible reservation engages art 20, under which acceptance makes the reserving and accepting states parties on the modified terms, and objection means the treaty does not enter into force between the reserving and objecting states. An impermissible reservation is void from the start, and three competing solutions exist for its effect: the state does not become a party at all; the reservation is severed and the state is fully bound; or the state is presumed bound without the reservation's benefit unless it expressly conditioned its consent on acceptance.

  Treaty interpretation follows arts 31–32 VCLT, recognised as customary: good-faith ordinary meaning in context and in the light of object and purpose, with context including the preamble and annexes. Supplementary means, including preparatory work, are used to confirm that meaning, or only where art 31 leaves the meaning ambiguous or obscure or produces a manifestly absurd or unreasonable result. In the *South China Sea Arbitration* the tribunal applied both steps to the term "island" in art 121 UNCLOS.

  Under ARSIWA, an internationally wrongful act is conduct attributable to a state that breaches an international obligation (art 2), whatever its source (art 12). Conduct of any state organ is attributable (art 4). A non-injured state may invoke responsibility if the obligation breached is owed to a group of states including it and is established for the protection of a collective interest of the group (art 48(1)(a)).

  **Application**

  *(1) Declaration or reservation?* The label "Interpretative Declaration" is not decisive, because art 2(1)(d) applies to a statement "however phrased or named". Oldavia's statement does more than explain its view: it says Oldavia "will not apply Article 4" to traditional egg collection. That purports to exclude the legal effect of Article 4 for a category of conduct in its application to Oldavia. It was made on ratification. It is therefore a reservation.

  *(2) Permissibility and relationships.* Article 22 of the Convention prohibits reservations to Article 4, so under art 19 the reservation is impermissible. It is also arguably incompatible with the object and purpose recorded in the preamble — halting the decline of breeding colonies — since egg collection strikes directly at breeding. Because the reservation is impermissible, the art 20 accept/object machinery does not operate: Marisol's acceptance cannot validate it, and Brenmark's objection does not have its usual art 20 effect of preventing the treaty entering into force between the two states. The reservation is void from the start. Its consequence for Oldavia's status is unresolved. On the first view Oldavia is not a party at all; on the second, the reservation is severed and Oldavia is bound by Article 4 in full; on the third, Oldavia is bound without the reservation's benefit unless it expressly conditioned its consent on acceptance. Oldavia's statement does not say its ratification depended on acceptance, which supports the view that it is bound by Article 4 in full as against all parties, including Marisol and Brenmark.

  *(3) The meaning of "taking".* Starting with art 31, the ordinary meaning of "taking" in Article 4 is informed by its immediate context — Article 4 itself expressly includes "its eggs" — and by Annex I, which defines "taking" as including "collecting". The preamble's object of halting the decline of breeding colonies confirms a broad reading. On this analysis the meaning is clear: collecting eggs is "taking". Oldavia's appeal to the negotiating records fails at the threshold: preparatory work is a supplementary means, available to confirm an art 31 meaning or where that meaning is ambiguous, obscure, absurd or unreasonable. None of those conditions is met, and one delegation's raising of traditional collection during drafting cannot rewrite clear treaty text.

  *(4) Breach and attribution.* If Oldavia is bound by Article 4, it must "prohibit" the taking of listed species. Rather than prohibiting egg collection, Oldavia's Ministry of Fisheries licenses it, up to 50,000 eggs a season, and Oldavia's Coast Guard escorts the collectors and keeps scientists away. Oldavia's argument that the villagers are private misses the point. The obligation in Article 4 is Oldavia's own obligation to prohibit; the breach lies in the licensing — an act of a state organ — and in the failure to prohibit, an omission (art 2). Both the Ministry and the Coast Guard are organs of the state under art 4. The breach is therefore attributable to Oldavia regardless of whether the villagers' own conduct is attributable. It also appears to be a continuing breach, repeated each season since 2021, while the Convention was in force for Oldavia.

  *(5) Invocation by Brenmark.* Brenmark is not specially affected, so it is not an injured state under art 42 in the ordinary sense. But the Convention is a multilateral conservation treaty whose obligations are owed to all 34 parties, and it is established for the protection of a collective interest — the conservation of southern seabirds. Brenmark, as a party, falls within art 48(1)(a) and may invoke Oldavia's responsibility, demanding cessation and assurances of non-repetition.

  **Conclusion**

  Oldavia's statement is a reservation, whatever its label, because it purports to exclude the application of Article 4 to egg collection. It is impermissible under art 19, because Article 22 prohibits reservations to Article 4, and it is probably also incompatible with the Convention's object and purpose. Marisol's acceptance and Brenmark's objection therefore do not operate under art 20. The most persuasive view is that the reservation is severed and Oldavia is fully bound, because it did not condition its consent on acceptance, though the law is unresolved. "Taking" clearly includes egg collection under art 31, so the negotiating records cannot be used to narrow it. By licensing collection and escorting the collectors, Oldavia's organs have breached Article 4 (arts 2, 4 and 12 ARSIWA). As a party to a treaty protecting a collective interest, Brenmark can invoke Oldavia's responsibility under art 48(1)(a).
