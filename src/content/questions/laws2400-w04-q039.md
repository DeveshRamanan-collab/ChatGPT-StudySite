model: |-
  This statement is incorrect, and confuses bare agreement to some act with valid consent to the specific
  act that actually occurred. Consent is only a defence where it relates to the act complained of: a
  person who agrees to one thing has not thereby agreed to something materially different or more
  extensive. In McNamara v Duncan, the plaintiff agreed to play Australian Rules football, and consented to
  forcible bodily contact within the rules and usages of the game. He did not, by taking part, consent to
  a deliberate blow that was a serious infringement of the rules and caused a fractured skull; the court
  held that consent to a sport does not extend to deliberately inflicted injury, even though some foul play
  is known to occur. Similarly, in Dean v Phung, the plaintiff consented to treatment for minor chipping of
  his front teeth. He did not, by giving that limited consent, agree to more than 53 consultations of
  unnecessary procedures costing $73,640; the court found this consent was not validly given because the
  treatment actually carried out went far beyond what had been agreed. In both cases, the defendant's
  conduct exceeded the scope of the plaintiff's actual consent, so the defence of consent failed. The
  correct analysis is therefore not "they agreed to something, so anything follows", but "did the specific
  conduct complained of fall within the scope of what was actually agreed to" — and in both cases it did
  not.
