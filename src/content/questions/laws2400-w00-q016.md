    Pure economic loss (PEL) is economic loss that does not result from physical injury to the person or property. Consequential economic loss — loss flowing from physical injury or property damage — has never been a problem: reasonable foreseeability is typically enough. PEL is recoverable only in certain situations, because of indeterminate liability, the relationship with the law of contract, and loss linked to lawful commercial activity. CLA s 5 defines "harm" to include economic loss.

    For PEL caused by an act, Australian courts may find a duty by weighing salient features, incrementally. In Perre v Apand Pty Ltd (1999) 198 CLR 180 a duty was owed to potato growers who suffered PEL when diseased seed supplied to a neighbouring farm 3 km away triggered WA's ban on potatoes grown within 20 km of the disease. McHugh J asked whether the loss was reasonably foreseeable; whether a duty would impose indeterminate liability (no — the 20 km rule defined the class); whether it would unreasonably burden the defendant's autonomy; whether the plaintiffs were vulnerable; and whether the defendant knew its conduct could harm people like them. In Caltex Oil v Dredge 'Willemstad' (1976) 136 CLR 529 a dredge that negligently damaged an oil pipeline owed Caltex a duty for the cost of transporting oil by road.

    Breach is decided under CLA s 5B, and causation under s 5D.

    Under the Design and Building Practitioners Act 2020 (NSW) s 37, a person who carries out construction work has a duty to exercise reasonable care to avoid economic loss caused by defects, owed to each owner and each subsequent owner, with damages as if the duty were established by the common law. "Construction work" includes building work and supervision (s 36). The duty is non-delegable (s 39) and cannot be contracted out of (s 40). At common law, a builder of a house owes a subsequent purchaser a duty (Bryan v Maloney (1995) 182 CLR 608), but a commercial purchaser able to protect itself may not be owed one (Woolcock Street Investments Pty Ltd v CDG Pty Ltd (2004) 216 CLR 515).

  **Issue**

  Three issues arise. First, how each business's loss from the damaged water main should be classified and, for any pure economic loss, whether Apex owed Crumb & Co, FreshPak and Linehaul Freight a duty of care. Second, whether Apex breached any duty and caused the losses. Third, whether Hannah can recover the cost of underpinning her house from Apex, with which she has no contract.

  **Rule**

  Pure economic loss (PEL) is economic loss not resulting from physical injury to the person or property; consequential loss flowing from property damage is recoverable on ordinary foreseeability. PEL is still "harm" under CLA s 5, but foreseeability is not enough: because of indeterminate liability, the relationship with contract and loss caused by lawful commerce, a duty arises only if the salient features support one. For PEL caused by an act, McHugh J in Perre v Apand Pty Ltd (1999) 198 CLR 180 asked about foreseeability, indeterminacy, the defendant's autonomy, the plaintiff's vulnerability and the defendant's knowledge; in Caltex Oil v Dredge 'Willemstad' (1976) 136 CLR 529 a duty was owed for the cost of transporting oil by road after a pipeline was damaged. Breach falls under s 5B and causation under s 5D. For defective buildings in NSW, the Design and Building Practitioners Act 2020 ("DBPA") s 37 imposes a statutory duty owed to subsequent owners; at common law, Bryan v Maloney (1995) 182 CLR 608 and Woolcock Street Investments Pty Ltd v CDG Pty Ltd (2004) 216 CLR 515 mark the line between home buyers and commercial buyers.

  **Application**

  *Classifying the losses.* Apex's description of "mere financial losses" is only partly right. Crumb & Co's six days of lost profits are PEL: nothing it owned was damaged, as with profits lost when a cut cable shuts a business. FreshPak's $9,000 of spoiled stock is property damage. Its $25,000 of lost profits are harder: FreshPak will say that, having suffered property damage, its losses are consequential; but the profits were lost because there was no water to operate, exactly as Crumb & Co's were, not because the stock spoiled. On the better view they are PEL. Linehaul's $14,000 is PEL suffered at one further remove — a loss from a customer being unable to trade.

  *Duty — FreshPak's stock.* For property damage, foreseeability is typically enough. An excavator operator holding the service plans could readily foresee that striking the estate's only water main would damage property dependent on it. A duty is owed for the $9,000.

  *Duty — Crumb & Co and FreshPak's profits.* Working through McHugh J's questions in Perre: (1) Loss to the businesses served by the main was plainly foreseeable — Apex had the plans showing what the main supplied. (2) A duty would not impose indeterminate liability: the class is the twelve units served by the main, defined by the pipe itself, much as the 20 km rule defined the class in Perre. A small, ascertained class is not the problem; an undefinable one is. (3) A duty not to drive an excavator through a mapped water main does not unreasonably burden Apex's legitimate activity; this is not a loss caused by lawful competition. (4) The tenants were vulnerable: they had no control over Apex's excavation and no contract with it through which to protect themselves, just as the Perres had no control over the seed their neighbours planted. (5) Apex knew, from the plans, exactly whom the main served. Caltex Oil supports the claim: there, loss from the interruption of a damaged pipeline, rather than damage to the plaintiff's own property, attracted a duty. Weighing these features, Apex owed Crumb & Co and FreshPak a duty for their lost profits.

  *Duty — Linehaul Freight.* Linehaul is different. It is outside the estate, and its loss arises only because one of its forty customers could not trade. If Apex owed a duty to FreshPak's carrier, it would equally owe one to every supplier, carrier, customer and contractor of every tenant — an undefinable class of exactly the kind that makes liability indeterminate. Apex had no knowledge of Linehaul, and Linehaul, a commercial operator with forty customers, is far better placed to protect itself against one customer's interruption through its own contracts. No duty is owed to Linehaul.

  *Breach and causation.* Under s 5B, the risk of striking the main was foreseeable and not insignificant, and a reasonable operator would have followed the plans: the burden was minimal, the probability of damage high once the plans were ignored, and the harm to twelve businesses serious. The operator's conduct was a breach. Under s 5D(1)(a), but for the strike the units would have had water and FreshPak's plant would not have failed; the losses within the class are of the kind the breach made likely, so they fall within the scope of liability (s 5D(1)(b)).

  *Hannah.* Begin with the statute. Apex built the house and supervised the pouring of the footings, both "construction work" (s 36). Under s 37 it owed a duty to exercise reasonable care to avoid economic loss caused by defects arising from that work, owed not only to the Los but to each subsequent owner — so Hannah needs neither a contract nor a common law duty. Pouring footings without the compaction the design required is a failure to take reasonable care, and the cracking and underpinning cost are economic loss caused by that defect. Apex cannot shift the duty to its site supervisor (s 39) or rely on any exclusion in its contract with the Los (s 40). Hannah is entitled to damages as if the duty were a common law duty (s 37(3)). The common law gives the same answer: like Mrs Maloney in Bryan v Maloney, Hannah is a later owner of a house who discovered a latent foundation defect, and the builder owed her a duty to take reasonable care. Woolcock is distinguishable — there a commercial purchaser could have protected itself, whereas Hannah is a homeowner whose pre-purchase inspection could not reveal the defect.

  **Conclusion**

  FreshPak can recover its $9,000 of spoiled stock on ordinary foreseeability. Applying Perre and Caltex Oil, Crumb & Co ($18,000) and FreshPak (for its $25,000 of lost profits) were owed a duty for their pure economic loss as members of a small, ascertained class known to Apex and unable to protect themselves; Apex breached it and caused their losses. Linehaul Freight's claim should fail: extending a duty further down the commercial chain would create indeterminate liability. Hannah can recover the $85,000 under DBPA s 37, and would also be owed a duty at common law under Bryan v Maloney.
