  A theory can plausibly be general and descriptive in aim, but whether Hart's succeeds on both criteria is doubtful.
  In the Postscript Hart claims a theory both general, not tied to any legal system or culture, and descriptive,
  morally neutral with no justificatory aims (CL 239), carried out through concepts such as rules of recognition and
  change and internal and external points of view (CL 240). These claims are criteria to assess the theory on its own
  terms. Finnis offers the strongest counterargument to descriptiveness: making and complying with law involves
  rational judgment that cannot be assessed without premises about true human goods. Dickson adds a subtler pressure:

  judgment. Generality faces a separate test: if Hart's paradigm is the legal systems of modern states, his concepts may
  not travel, as Twining's call for cross-cultural concepts suggests. On balance, a theory can avoid moral
  justification yet still involve evaluation, so 'descriptive' should be read narrowly, and generality must be
  earned, not assumed.
