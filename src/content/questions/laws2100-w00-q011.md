  In chapter IX of *The Concept of Law* Hart argued that, given certain simple truisms about human beings and the world they live in, rules restricting the use of violence and requiring respect for property and promises are "indispensable features of municipal law". These truisms, he said, "disclose the core of good sense in the doctrine of Natural Law", and "it is in this form that we should reply to the positivist thesis that 'law may have any content'" (CL 199). The positivist thesis he had in mind was Kelsen's: for Kelsen the content of individual laws is irrelevant to their validity, which depends only on the chain of validity.

  Hart insisted that this was a natural necessity in the world as it is, not a conceptual connection between law and morality, just as he treated coercion as a natural rather than a conceptual feature of law. He also thought that even the most cruel dictatorship needs some members to accept its rules voluntarily, and that unless the rules protect at least them it would be a suicide pact. Finnis responded that Hart should have called it the minimum content of *positive* law.

  Critically evaluate the following statement:

  "Hart's minimum content of natural law concedes more to natural law than a positivist can afford."

  In your essay, explain exactly what Hart claims and what kind of necessity he relies on, and ask whether a positivist can accept the argument without accepting a necessary connection between law and morality. Draw on material from more than one week of the unit, engaging with Hart and at least two of Kelsen, Fuller, Finnis and Austin. Take a clear position, support it with reasons, and deal with the strongest counterargument to your view.
model: |
  **Thesis**

  The statement is wrong. Hart's minimum content concedes that any legal system which lasts will contain rules protecting persons, property and promises, and that those rules coincide with basic social morality. But the concession is about the content that law will in fact have, given facts about human beings, not about what law must be to count as law. It is exactly the kind of connection the separation thesis permits: contingent in the conceptual sense, however reliable in practice. What the argument costs is not positivism, but Kelsen's stronger claim that law may have any content — and that is a claim a positivist can afford to lose.

  **Argument**

  *What Hart claims.* Hart's positivism is "the simple contention that it is in no sense a necessary truth that laws reproduce or satisfy certain demands of morality, though in fact they have often done so" (CL 185–186). The minimum content does not contradict it. Hart argues that, given the setting of natural facts and aims — which make sanctions "both possible and necessary in a municipal system" — "this is a natural necessity", and minimum forms of protection for persons, property and promises are "similarly indispensable features of municipal law" (CL 199). His version is empirical and draws on Hobbes and Hume: given how human beings are, a society without such rules would not survive.

  *Natural, not conceptual, necessity.* The key distinction is between what is necessary given the world we live in and what is necessary to the concept of law. Hart treats coercion in the same way. The command theory of Austin and the coercive-order theory of Kelsen made sanctions part of the definition of law; Hart replaced that with a natural necessity — in our world, municipal systems need sanctions. The minimum content has the same structure. It explains why legal systems are as they are without building morality into the test of legal validity, which remains the rule of recognition.

  *The target is Kelsen, not positivism.* For Kelsen, the content of individual laws is irrelevant; only the chain of validity matters. "Law may have any content" is therefore a thesis about Kelsen's pure theory, which answers "the question of what the law is, not what it ought to be" (1941). Hart can deny that law may have *any* content in practice while insisting that no particular content is a condition of validity. Positivism as Hart states it — the social sources thesis (CL 269) — survives intact.

  *How little it concedes.* Hart's own lecture illustration makes the point. Even a cruel dictatorship must protect the members who accept its rules, or it would be a suicide pact. That shows how minimal the minimum content is: it can be satisfied by a system that protects only its elite. That is consistent with Hart's view that the principles of legality are "compatible with very great iniquity" (CL 207). The minimum content is a floor for survival, not a standard of justice.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest counterargument is that Hart has conceded the natural lawyer's method, whatever he says about validity. By explaining the content of law through human goods — survival, security, the reliability of promises — Hart has shown that one cannot understand law without asking what it is for. Finnis's point is that description requires evaluation: "no theorist can give a theoretical description and analysis of social facts without also participating in the work of evaluation" (NLNR 3). Once Hart admits that survival is the aim that makes certain rules indispensable, he has adopted a view of law's point, and the only question is whether survival is the right point. Fuller would add that the procedural side is also indispensable: laws that are secret, retroactive or impossible to comply with fail to guide conduct at all, and Hart concedes the inner morality of law. On this view Hart has admitted two necessary connections, one substantive and one procedural, and a positivist who admits two can hardly deny a third. Finnis's remark that Hart should have called it the minimum content of *positive* law can be read as a challenge: either it is about positive law only, in which case it is a trivial observation, or it concedes a natural law point.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument misreads Finnis's challenge. Calling it the minimum content of positive law is not a trivial description; it states Hart's thesis correctly. Rules against violence, theft and promise-breaking are features that positive law must, as a natural necessity, contain; they are not moral criteria that a rule must pass to be law. That is not trivial: it corrects Kelsen and explains the overlap between law and social morality that every legal theory must account for. And it is not a natural law point, because a rule that violates morality in other respects — or protects only the elite — is still law on Hart's view.

  Nor does taking survival as a premise commit Hart to natural law methodology. Hart gives explanatory priority to the internal point of view, but treats it as an amalgam: allegiance "may be based on many different considerations: calculations of long-term interest; disinterested interest in others; an unreflecting inherited or traditional attitude; or the mere wish to do as others do" (CL 203). Survival is an aim almost everyone shares, not a contested moral judgment about human flourishing. A theory can rely on it without participating in the evaluation Finnis has in mind. The concession to Fuller is also limited: Hart accepts the inner morality, then denies that it guarantees justice.

  So the minimum content concedes something to natural law — a "core of good sense" (CL 199) — but not a necessary connection between law and morality. It costs Kelsen's claim that law may have any content, and positivism can afford that loss. Indeed, by explaining why law and morality so often coincide without making the coincidence a condition of validity, it strengthens Hart's positivism rather than weakening it.
