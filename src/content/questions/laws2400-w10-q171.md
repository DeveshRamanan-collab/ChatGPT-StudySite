prompt: Kofi worked for three employers over 20 years. Only his second employer negligently exposed him to silica dust; the dust at the other workplaces involved no negligence. He develops a disease caused by cumulative inhalation of silica dust, and the science cannot say which inhalation caused it. Advise Kofi on factual causation against the second employer.
model: |-
  The issue is whether the second employer's negligent exposure caused Kofi's disease when science cannot identify which inhalation was responsible.

  First, the CLA probably does not apply: s 3B(1) excludes civil liability in dust disease proceedings under the Dust Diseases Tribunal Act 1989 (NSW), so the common law governs (as in Amaca v Booth). The 'but for' test is inconclusive here: an evidential gap where the science cannot identify the cause.

  The key question is whether the employer's conduct contributed to the harm, or merely increased the risk. Increased risk is probabilistic and forward-looking and is not enough in Australia (Amaca v Ellis); Fairchild's risk-based exception has not been adopted. Material contribution to harm is causal and must be more than negligible. In Bonnington Castings v Wardlaw, a negligent contribution to cumulative silica dust that was more than de minimis was enough; it need not be the sole or main cause. In Booth, contribution was inferred from expert evidence that cumulative exposure contributes to the disease.

  Kofi's disease is caused by cumulative inhalation, and part of his dust came from the negligent employer. If expert evidence shows that exposure made a more than negligible contribution, Bonnington applies. If his evidence shows only an increased risk, Ellis is against him.

  Kofi is likely to succeed if he proves, on the balance of probabilities, a more than negligible contribution.
