prompt: Explain the distinction between personal immunity (ratione personae) and functional immunity (ratione materiae) for state officials in international law.
model: |-
  Both immunities belong to the state, not the official: they exist so that officials can perform their functions (*Arrest Warrant*), and both come from customary international law. They differ in what they attach to.

  Functional immunity (ratione materiae) attaches to the act. It covers only official acts performed with the state's authority while in office, and any official can hold it — for example, a soldier who kills an enemy soldier in battle. Because it protects the state's acts, it continues after the official leaves office, and only the state can waive it.

  Personal immunity (ratione personae) attaches to the office. It is complete, covering private as well as official acts, but only the most senior officials hold it: heads of state, heads of government and foreign ministers. It lasts only while the person is in office; afterwards only functional immunity remains, for official acts. In *Arrest Warrant* a Belgian warrant for the serving Congolese foreign minister breached his personal immunity, with no exception for international crimes.

  Neither immunity applies in the official's own state's courts, and both are procedural: immunity does not mean impunity.
