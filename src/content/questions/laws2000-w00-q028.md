prompt: International law recognises several grounds upon which a state may assert prescriptive jurisdiction over conduct occurring outside its territory. Identify and briefly explain THREE such grounds.
model: |-
  Where conduct has a foreign element, a state must justify its prescriptive jurisdiction on a recognised ground. Three grounds reach conduct outside its territory.

  1. Active nationality. A state may prosecute its nationals for acts committed abroad. States largely decide who their nationals are; ships and aircraft take the nationality of their state of registration, and dual nationals may face jurisdiction from both states.

  2. The protective principle. A state may assert jurisdiction over acts done abroad by non-nationals that pose a genuine threat to a vital security interest — counterfeiting its currency, espionage, plotting a coup, or attacks on its diplomats.

  3. Universality. Some offences are so serious that any state may prosecute them without any link to the place, offender or victim — originally piracy, now genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity and torture (e.g. Geneva Convention IV art 146). Without a treaty basis it is controversial, so states usually require the accused's presence.

  Passive personality (the victim's nationality) is a further ground, but it is controversial and best treated as subsidiary. Where several states have a ground, the state with the closest link is usually best placed.
