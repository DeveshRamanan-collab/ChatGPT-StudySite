model: "It is defensible in its weak form, not its strong one. On the strong natural law thesis, moral soundness is a necessary element of legal validity, so unjust \"laws\" are not legally valid; for Radbruch, only extremely unjust laws. That collides with the fact, which positivists stress, that unjust rules are identified and enforced as law — Hart's two simple things: a rule's immorality does not make it not a rule of law. Finnis reads the maxim weakly: unjust laws are not laws in their central case or focal meaning, as a fallacious argument is not properly an argument. They are morally and legally defective, but not thereby invalid. He also separates senses of \"obligation to obey\": empirical liability to sanction may remain while legal obligation in the moral sense fails. The counterargument is that the weak thesis concedes so much to positivism that the maxim loses its bite. On balance it keeps a real claim — that law's central case is just law, and injustice defeats law's moral claim on us — and that is defensible."
