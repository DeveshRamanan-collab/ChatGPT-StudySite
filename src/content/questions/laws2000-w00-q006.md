  **Issue**

  The issues are: (1) whether a customary rule prohibiting bottom trawling on high-seas seamounts has formed; (2) whether Kerrova and Arvelle are bound by it; (3) whether the environmental group can obtain an injunction in the Federal Court on the basis of that rule; and (4) whether the rule can support a criminal prosecution of Southern Deep's managing director.

  **Rule**

  A rule of customary international law requires two elements: a general practice of states — consistent repetition of behaviour when confronted with the same facts — and opinio juris sive necessitatis, the belief that the practice is legally required rather than a matter of courtesy, convenience or tradition. In *North Sea Continental Shelf* the ICJ required practice, "including that of States whose interests are specially affected", to be "extensive and virtually uniform", while accepting that this could occur over a short period. In *Nicaragua* the Court held that practice need not be in "absolutely rigorous conformity with the rule"; it suffices that conduct is generally consistent and that departures are treated as breaches rather than as a new rule. By contrast, in the *Asylum Case*, "so much uncertainty and contradiction ... fluctuation and discrepancy" meant no custom existed. Soft law, such as General Assembly resolutions, does not itself bind, but may evidence practice or opinio juris.

  A state that has persistently and consistently objected to a rule from the very beginning of its formation is not bound by it. In the *Fisheries Case* the Court added that, even had the 10-mile bay rule been customary, Norway, which had always opposed its application to its coast, would not be bound.

  In Australian law two doctrines compete: transformation (custom becomes law only when adopted by Parliament or the courts) and incorporation (custom is automatically a source of law unless there is a contrary rule). Either way, a customary rule cannot be adopted where it is inconsistent with legislation, and clear Australian legislation prevails over international law (*Polites*). International law may influence the common law (Brennan J in *Mabo (No 2)*), but criminal norms are different: in *Nulyarimma v Thompson* the Federal Court held that genocide, even as a customary and jus cogens prohibition, is not an offence in Australian law without legislation (Wilcox J).

  **Application**

  *(1) Has a rule formed?* The practice is extensive: about 70 states have banned seamount trawling. Crucially, they include nearly every state with a significant deep-sea fleet — the specially affected states — which is what *North Sea Continental Shelf* requires. Eight years is short, but that case accepts that a short period may suffice if the practice is extensive and virtually uniform. The handful of vessels caught trawling do not defeat uniformity: as in *Nicaragua*, their flag states prosecuted them and publicly called the trawling "a violation of the international prohibition", so the departures were treated as breaches, confirming the rule. This is quite unlike the contradiction in the *Asylum Case*.

  Opinio juris is the harder element. Many governments said the ban was "required by international law", which is direct evidence of a belief in legal obligation. Others called it "sound environmental policy", which is evidence of convenience rather than obligation, and those states' practice should carry less weight. The General Assembly resolutions do not bind, but they evidence a developing belief of obligation. On balance, given the specially affected states' statements and the treatment of departures as violations, a customary rule has probably formed, though the mixed statements make the conclusion arguable.

  *(2) Kerrova and Arvelle.* Kerrova has voted against every General Assembly resolution from the first and has stated its objection in every relevant forum. It objected from the beginning of the rule's formation, persistently and consistently, and so, like Norway in the *Fisheries Case*, it is not bound. Arvelle is in a different position. It was silent for six years and objected only after most of the bans were in place — that is, after the rule had arguably crystallised. The persistent objector rule allows a state to opt out only by objecting before crystallisation and maintaining that objection, so Arvelle's late objection does not protect it. If the rule exists, Arvelle is bound.

  *(3) The injunction.* Whichever doctrine applies, a customary rule cannot be adopted into Australian law where it is inconsistent with legislation. The High Seas Fishing Permits Act expressly authorises a permit holder to fish "by any method specified in the permit", and Southern Deep's permit specifies bottom trawling. The Act is clear, so the presumption that statutes accord with international law does not help: clear legislation prevails (*Polites*). The customary prohibition cannot become part of the common law in a way that contradicts the statute, and the injunction should fail. That Australia may be in breach of its international obligations by permitting the trawling is a separate question at the international level, not a basis for domestic relief.

  *(4) The prosecution.* The request for prosecution faces an even stronger obstacle. *Nulyarimma* holds that criminal norms are different: even a customary jus cogens prohibition such as genocide does not create an offence in Australian law without legislation declaring it a crime. A customary prohibition on seamount trawling is plainly weaker than the prohibition of genocide. Absent legislation creating the offence, the DPP cannot charge Southern Deep's managing director.

  **Conclusion**

  A customary rule prohibiting bottom trawling on high-seas seamounts has probably formed: practice is extensive and includes the specially affected states (*North Sea Continental Shelf*), departures are treated as breaches (*Nicaragua*), and many states express a belief of legal obligation, though the "policy" statements make opinio juris contestable. Kerrova is a persistent objector and not bound (*Fisheries Case*); Arvelle objected too late and is bound. In Australia, the rule cannot be adopted inconsistently with the clear terms of the High Seas Fishing Permits Act, so the injunction should fail (*Polites*); and a customary norm cannot ground a criminal charge without legislation (*Nulyarimma*), so the prosecution request should also fail.
