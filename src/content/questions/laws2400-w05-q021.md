  Briefly explain the three elements a plaintiff must establish to succeed in an action for negligence, and explain why negligence requires proof of damage when trespass to the person does not.
model: |
  A plaintiff in negligence must establish three elements: first, that the defendant owed them a duty of care; second, that the defendant breached that duty, by creating a foreseeable, not-insignificant risk of harm that a reasonable person would have taken precautions against; and third, that the breach caused the plaintiff compensable harm, assessed through factual causation and the scope of liability (whether it is appropriate that liability extend to that harm). Compensable harm may be physical, psychiatric or economic. Negligence requires proof of damage because it is classified as an action on the case, rather than an action in trespass. Trespass to the person torts, such as battery and assault, are actionable per se: the plaintiff need only prove the unlawful interference itself, because the law treats bodily integrity and liberty as interests worth protecting even where no measurable loss results. Negligence protects a broader and more diffuse range of interests, and imposing liability for merely careless conduct without any resulting harm would expose defendants to liability far beyond what is proportionate; damage is therefore built into the cause of action itself, so a defendant is not liable in negligence for careless conduct that, as it happens, causes no harm at all.
