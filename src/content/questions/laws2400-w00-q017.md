  rule: |
    Volenti is a complete defence. The defendant must show that the plaintiff perceived the risk, fully appreciated its nature and extent, and voluntarily accepted that specific risk; awareness of the general nature of a risk is not enough (Banovic v Perkovic; Carey v Lake Macquarie City Council). Effective knowledge extends to acceptance of the materialisation of the risk (Carey; ICI v Shatwell). Courts are generally reluctant to allow the defence against passengers who rely on an intoxicated driver, and some legislation excludes it in that situation (eg Motor Accidents Act 1988 (NSW) s 76).

    Contributory negligence results in a partial reduction of damages to the extent that is "just and equitable having regard to [the plaintiff's] share in the responsibility for the damage" (Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1965 (NSW) s 9(1)). The standard is that of a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position, judged on what they knew or ought to have known (CLA s 5R). There must be a causal link between the plaintiff's lack of care and the injury (Caswell v Powell Duffryn Associated Collieries), and the reduction reflects the whole conduct and relative blameworthiness of each party (Pennington v Norris).

    Under CLA s 50, if the plaintiff was intoxicated to the extent that their capacity to exercise reasonable care and skill was impaired (s 50(1)), damages are not to be awarded unless the court is satisfied the injury is likely to have occurred even if they had not been intoxicated (s 50(2)); if so, contributory negligence is presumed unless the intoxication did not contribute in any way to the cause (s 50(3)), and damages are reduced by 25% or a greater percentage (s 50(4)). The section does not apply if the intoxication was not self-induced (s 50(5)).

    Illegality: where the plaintiff and defendant were involved in a criminal enterprise when the plaintiff was injured and the court cannot determine an appropriate standard of care, the defence is available (Gala v Preston); it is not available where the court can determine the standard (Progress & Properties Ltd v Craft).
model: |
  **Issue**

  Tariq admits duty and breach, so the claim turns on his three defences: (1) whether Jordan voluntarily assumed the risk; (2) whether Jordan was contributorily negligent — by boarding, by refusing the life jacket, and through his own intoxication under the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") s 50 — and by how much his damages should be reduced; and (3) whether illegality bars the claim because the two were on their way to steal fish.

  **Rule**

  Volenti is a complete defence requiring proof that the plaintiff perceived the risk, fully appreciated its nature and extent, and voluntarily accepted that specific risk, not merely its general nature (Banovic v Perkovic; Carey v Lake Macquarie City Council; ICI v Shatwell). Courts are reluctant to apply it to passengers of intoxicated drivers. Contributory negligence reduces damages to the extent that is just and equitable (Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1965 (NSW) s 9(1)), judged by the standard of a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position (CLA s 5R), requiring a causal link (Caswell v Powell Duffryn Associated Collieries) and a comparison of the whole conduct of each party (Pennington v Norris). Section 50 adds a statutory sequence for intoxicated plaintiffs. Illegality is available where, in a joint criminal enterprise, the court cannot determine a standard of care (Gala v Preston), but not where it can (Progress & Properties Ltd v Craft).

  **Application**

  *Volenti.* Jordan plainly perceived that Tariq was drunk — he said so. But volenti requires more than awareness of a general danger. Tariq must show that Jordan fully appreciated the nature and extent of the specific risk that materialised — a high-speed crossing of a dark lake with the lights off, ending in a collision with a buoy — and voluntarily accepted it. Jordan's words "just go slow" point the other way: he expected care, and did not accept its absence. The decisions to switch off the lights and to drive fast were made only after Jordan was aboard. Courts are generally reluctant to allow volenti against passengers who rely on an intoxicated driver; the flowchart's example of statutory exclusion, Motor Accidents Act 1988 (NSW) s 76, is not reproduced in the materials and it cannot be assumed to reach a boat, but the courts' reluctance applies regardless. Volenti is likely to fail.

  *Contributory negligence — boarding.* Under s 5R the question is what a reasonable person in Jordan's position, knowing what he knew, would have done. He had watched Tariq drop the keys twice and stumble, and judged him "wasted". A reasonable 22-year-old would not have got into a speedboat at night with him. That was a failure to take precautions against the very risk that materialised, and it was causally linked to his injuries: had he stayed on the jetty he would not have been thrown from the boat (Caswell).

  *Contributory negligence — the life jacket.* Refusing a life jacket looks careless, but the causal requirement defeats this part of Tariq's argument. The medical evidence is that a life jacket would not have prevented either injury, so the refusal did not contribute to the damage and cannot justify any reduction.

  *Intoxication — CLA s 50.* After six beers, Jordan's capacity to exercise reasonable care was probably impaired (s 50(1)), and nothing suggests the intoxication was not self-induced (s 50(5)). Section 50(2) is the real danger for Jordan: the court must not award damages unless satisfied that his injuries are likely to have occurred even if he had not been intoxicated. Tariq will argue that a sober Jordan would never have boarded with a drunk driver. Jordan will answer that he agreed to the outing before the drinking affected his judgement about it, that he would have gone anyway, and that the crash was entirely the product of Tariq's driving — a sober passenger would have been thrown out just the same. If the court is not satisfied, Jordan recovers nothing. If it is satisfied, s 50(3) presumes contributory negligence unless his intoxication did not contribute in any way to the cause; since drink plausibly affected his decision to board, the presumption is unlikely to be rebutted, and s 50(4) requires a reduction of at least 25%.

  *Apportionment.* The reduction must be just and equitable having regard to Jordan's share in the responsibility (s 9(1)), examining the whole conduct of each party and their relative blameworthiness (Pennington v Norris). Tariq was the driver; he was more intoxicated; he chose to cross a dark lake at high speed with the lights off and hit an obstacle he would ordinarily have avoided. Jordan's fault lay in going along. Tariq's share is much the larger, so the reduction should sit at or modestly above the 25% statutory floor.

  *Illegality.* Two questions decide this defence. First, were the two men involved in the criminal enterprise when Jordan was injured? They were on their way to take fish from the farm's pens; Tariq will say the journey was part of the enterprise, Jordan that no theft had yet been attempted. Second, can the court determine the relevant standard of care? Tariq's best point, under Gala v Preston, is that the lights were switched off precisely to avoid detection: the manner of driving was shaped by the crime, making it difficult to say what care one participant in a stealthy night raid owes another. Jordan's answer, under Progress & Properties Ltd v Craft, is that the court can readily determine the standard — the care of a reasonable person driving a boat across a lake at night — and that the collision came from an impaired driver failing to see a buoy, not from anything peculiar to the enterprise. The better view is that a standard can be set, so the defence fails; but because the dark, fast crossing was part of the plan, this is a real risk to the claim.

  **Conclusion**

  Volenti should fail: Jordan perceived that Tariq was drunk but did not fully appreciate and accept the specific risk that materialised, and courts are reluctant to apply the defence to such passengers. The illegality defence probably fails because a court can set the standard of care for driving a boat, though the lights-off crossing gives Tariq a genuine argument. Jordan's damages will be reduced for contributory negligence in boarding with an obviously drunk driver — not for refusing the life jacket, which caused nothing. The greatest risk is s 50: if the court is not satisfied his injuries were likely even had he been sober, he recovers nothing; if it is, his damages are reduced by at least 25%.
