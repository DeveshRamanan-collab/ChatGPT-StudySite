  Explain the two elements required to establish a rule of customary international law, and analyse the
  statement: "in addition to being consistent and widespread, practice must unfold over a long period of time
  for a new customary rule to be established."
model: |
  Customary international law requires two elements. First, a general practice among states — consistent
  repetition of a particular behaviour when states are confronted with the same facts (the objective element).
  Second, acceptance of that practice by states as legally binding — opinio juris sive necessitatis — a belief
  that the conduct is legally required, not merely a matter of courtesy, convenience or tradition (the
  subjective element). Neither is sufficient alone: consistent but merely habitual conduct, like diplomatic
  ceremonial or sending humanitarian aid, is not custom without a belief of legal duty, and a stated belief
  unsupported by consistent practice is equally insufficient.

  The statement that practice must unfold over "a long period of time" should be rejected in its absolute
  form. North Sea Continental Shelf held that "the passage of only a short period of time is not necessarily,
  or of itself, a bar to the formation of a new rule of customary international law", provided that, within
  the period in question, however short, state practice — including that of states whose interests are
  specially affected — is "extensive and virtually uniform" in the sense of the rule invoked. This departs
  from the traditional view that custom needs practice exercised over very long periods, sometimes hundreds of
  years. What matters is the intensity, extent and uniformity of the practice within whatever period exists,
  not the sheer length of that period as such — a rule can, in principle, crystallise quickly if the practice
  supporting it (particularly among the states most affected by the rule) is sufficiently strong.

  Consistency and uniformity remain necessary, however: Nicaragua v United States of America confirms that
  practice need not be in absolutely rigorous conformity, but a state's inconsistent conduct must generally be
  treated as a breach of the rule rather than as evidence of a new one for the rule to survive, and the Asylum
  Case shows that sufficiently uncertain, contradictory practice will defeat a custom claim regardless of how
  long it has continued. Duration, then, is one input into whether practice is sufficiently established, but
  it is not an independent, fixed requirement with a minimum length attached.
