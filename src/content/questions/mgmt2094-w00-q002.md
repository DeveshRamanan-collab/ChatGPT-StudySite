model: |
  **Thesis**

  The statement is wrong on both of its halves. Design thinking does start from people, but it does not simply ask customers what they want: it studies what they do, feel and struggle with, because the needs that matter are often ones customers cannot put into words. Nor does it simply give them what they asked for: it reframes the problem, generates many ideas, and tests rough prototypes with users before anything is delivered, while checking that a solution is feasible and viable as well as desirable. Design thinking is human-centred, not customer-directed, and the difference is where its value lies.

  **Argument**

  *Three lenses, not one.* Tim Brown's definition matches "people's needs" with "what is technologically feasible" and "what a viable business can convert into consumer value". Desirability is one lens of three. The Week 8 version of the same idea is the three circles — desirable, possible, viable — and the jetpack shows why the first circle is not enough: it has long been desirable, but the technology is only now arriving and it is extremely expensive. A process that gave customers what they wanted would stop at the first circle.

  *Unarticulated needs.* Human-centred design works on the premise that a deep understanding of users' unarticulated needs, gained through empathy, inspires creativity and innovation. Empathetic design observes users in their own environments, closer to anthropology than to market science. The reading separates affective empathy (a mirrored emotion) from cognitive empathy (perspective taking: stepping into another person's worldview, beliefs and fears). Temple Grandin noticed that livestock reacted badly to being led in a straight line and designed curved corrals that reduced stress, panic and injury; others had assumed that is just what cows do. The four roadblocks to empathy — idealism, egoism, realism and circumstantialism — explain why asking is unreliable: people see things as they expect or want them to be. Henry Ford's line makes the point: had he asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses.

  *Reframing the brief.* Brown and Wyatt describe design thinking as overlapping spaces — inspiration, ideation and implementation — rather than orderly steps. In the inspiration space the team goes into the world to observe what people actually do, because people often cannot say what they need. Shanti, near Hyderabad, fetches free borehole water that periodically makes her family sick rather than buy from the Naandi Foundation's nearby, affordable treatment centre. Habit is not the reason: she cannot carry the 5-gallon jerrican the centre requires, her husband works until after it closes, and the monthly card buys more water than she needs. The centre made clean water very well, but its designers had not considered the needs of everyone in the community. Kara Pecknold, asked to build a website for Rwandan weavers, found they had little access to computers and reframed the brief. In both cases progress came from changing the question, not from taking the order.

  *Prototype, then test.* Meinel and Leifer's tangibility rule says "prototypes are communication media", and the reading's process runs empathy, define, ideate, prototype, test. VisionSpring found that children upset by traditional eye tests were at ease when they screened each other — something learned only by trying. Week 10's lean start-up makes the same move: Blank says entrepreneurs execute on guesses and must test their hypotheses against facts, and "the facts live outside the building".

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest objection comes from the other side. Verganti argues that it is important not to engage with the customer too much, because most customers are not trained to see possibilities in the far future; Jobs said customers don't know what they want until you show them. Verganti's foreword adds that people search for new meanings, not just better solutions — families buy candles that last 30 hours to dim the light and perfume a room, not to illuminate it — and that meaning can be designed and proposed. On this view design thinking is not about customers' wants at all, but about proposing what customers have not imagined.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument also defeats the statement, but it overcorrects. Gemser describes two streams of research — IDEO going into the customer's world, Verganti keeping a distance — and her own conclusion is to "get out of the ivory tower", engage stakeholders at all levels and stand in the shoes of the actual customer. The streams agree on the essential point: customers are a source of insight, not of specifications. Grandin observed; Pecknold reframed; Verganti proposes meanings people will value. None of them simply asked and delivered.

  So the statement should be rejected. Design thinking keeps people at the centre, as the human rule requires, but its work is to discover needs customers cannot state, to reframe the brief, and to test proposals until they are desirable, feasible and viable. Asking customers what they want is where poor design thinking stops; good design thinking starts there and keeps going.
