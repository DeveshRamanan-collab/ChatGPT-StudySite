model: A descriptive theory can be morally neutral in its aims, but it cannot avoid judgements of importance, and Finnis argues those judgements are evaluative. Hart claims a theory that is general and descriptive, morally neutral and without justificatory aims. Finnis replies that no theorist can describe and analyse social facts without participating in evaluation (NLNR 3). He credits Hart and Raz with a decisive break from the naive methods of Bentham, Austin and Kelsen, through attention to practical point, central case and focal meaning, and viewpoint (NLNR 6). But which case is central, and from what viewpoint? Hart privileges the internal point of view yet treats it as an amalgam of attitudes (CL 203); Finnis says Hart should have chosen its central case, the practically reasonable person. The counter is that one can identify what participants treat as important without endorsing it. On balance Finnis shows that description needs selection; whether selection must be moral evaluation is the real question, and that is where an essay should take its stand.
