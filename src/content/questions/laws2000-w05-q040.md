model: "Monism sees national and international law as part of one single normative legal order. International law has direct effect (incorporation) and holds the same legal authority as national law, so courts can apply it directly; inconsistencies must be resolved, frequently in favour of international law. It is often associated with the civil law tradition. Germany is a clear example: art 25 of the Basic Law makes the general rules of international law an integral part of federal law that take precedence over the laws and directly create rights and duties. Dualism sees the two as distinct legal orders. Only national law has force domestically, so international law must be transformed into domestic law to have effect; inconsistencies do not arise because the systems have different subjects and purposes, and national law usually has priority. It is often associated with the common law tradition. These are ideal types: most systems blend them, as the Dutch Constitution does by requiring parliamentary approval (art 91) while giving published treaty provisions binding effect and priority over statutes (arts 93–94). Australia is primarily dualist: treaties must be transformed (Dietrich), and clear Australian law prevails."
