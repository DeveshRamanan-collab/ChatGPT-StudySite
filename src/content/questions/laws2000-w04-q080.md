prompt: What role does recognition by other states play in determining statehood? Discuss the two theories and the motivations for recognising or not recognising an entity.
model: |
  Recognition is the act by which a state officially acknowledges the existence of another state or government.
  It is a complex mixture of politics and law. Two theories explain its role. Under the declaratory theory, the
  most widely held today, recognition merely acknowledges a pre-existing statehood: an entity meeting Article 1 of
  the Montevideo Convention is a state, and recognition is symbolic. Under the constitutive theory, recognition is
  a precondition: it creates the state and confers legal personality — no recognition, no existence.

  Motivations are often political. States sometimes recognise entities that do not meet all the criteria, and
  refuse entities that do. Recognition still has legal consequences: it obliges the recognising state to respect
  the other's sovereignty and not interfere, and cannot be retracted. Collective recognition through the UN needs
  a Security Council recommendation (subject to veto) and two-thirds of the General Assembly, so non-membership
  does not mean non-recognition. Non-recognition also serves a legal function where the criteria were achieved
  illegally (Friendly Relations Declaration 1970; Kosovo Advisory Opinion 2010). The TRNC meets the criteria but,
  created through Turkey's 1974 invasion, is recognised only by Turkey, and Security Council resolutions 541 and
  550 declared its declaration invalid. Kosovo, Palestine and Western Sahara show how contested statehood remains.
