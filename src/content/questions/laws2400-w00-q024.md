  Dr Mehta and Dr Hughes are dentists in practice together in Parramatta.

Dr Mehta's expert says the technique is "promising" and is used by some dentists in Germany and Italy. Both experts agree that, apart from Dr Mehta and one colleague in Brisbane, no dentist in Australia uses it. After Aisha's injury, Dr Mehta stopped using it.

  Ben, 31 and in good health, had root canal treatment performed by Dr Hughes. Dr Hughes did not prescribe antibiotics afterwards. Ben developed a serious infection and spent three days in hospital. The expert evidence shows that Australian dentists are divided. A substantial body of practitioners, supported by a national clinical guideline, does not prescribe antibiotics after routine root canal treatment in healthy patients, because of concern about antibiotic resistance. Another substantial body routinely prescribes them. Ben's expert, who belongs to the second group, says that withholding antibiotics is "irrational", because infections like Ben's are well known.

  Both dentists say they acted as competent professionals and cannot be liable.

  Assume that each dentist owed the patient a duty of care and that, if breach is established, it caused the patient's harm. Advise Aisha and Ben whether Dr Mehta and Dr Hughes breached their duties.

  rule: |
    At common law the standard for a professional "is that of ordinary skilled person exercising and professing to have that special skill"; the High Court rejected the Bolam principle that the standard is set by professional practice (Rogers v Whitaker (1992) 175 CLR 479).

    CLA s 5O incorporates a modified Bolam principle. A professional does not incur a liability in negligence arising from the provision of a professional service if it is established that the professional acted in a manner that, at the time, was widely accepted in Australia by peer professional opinion as competent professional practice (s 5O(1)). Peer professional opinion cannot be relied on if the court considers it irrational (s 5O(2)). Differing peer professional opinions widely accepted in Australia do not prevent any one or more of them being relied on (s 5O(3)). Peer professional opinion does not have to be universally accepted to be widely accepted (s 5O(4)).

    Section 5O operates as a defence. The defendant bears the onus of establishing its preconditions: that he was practising a profession, in a manner widely accepted in Australia by peer professional opinion as competent professional practice. Where s 5O is made out, it supplants the analysis otherwise required by s 5B; if it is not, ss 5B and 5C apply (Peterkin v Tadros [2026] NSWSC 1185 [151]–[154]).

    Under s 5B(1), a person is not negligent in failing to take precautions against a risk of harm unless the risk was foreseeable, the risk was not insignificant, and a reasonable person in the person's position would have taken those precautions, considering probability, seriousness, burden and social utility (s 5B(2)). The risk of harm is identified first, as a class of injury (Benic v NSW [2010] NSWSC 1039). Under s 5C, (b) the fact that a risk could have been avoided by doing something in a different way does not of itself give rise to liability, and (c) later action that would have avoided the risk does not of itself give rise to liability or constitute an admission.
model: |
  **Issue**

  Two issues arise. First, whether Dr Mehta can rely on the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") s 5O for using the rapid-extraction technique on Aisha and, if not, whether he breached his duty under ss 5B and 5C. Second, whether Dr Hughes can rely on s 5O for not prescribing antibiotics to Ben, where peer opinion is divided and Ben's expert calls the practice irrational.

  **Rule**

  At common law a professional is judged against the ordinary skilled person exercising and professing to have that special skill, not simply against what the profession does (Rogers v Whitaker (1992) 175 CLR 479). Section 5O now provides a modified Bolam defence: a professional is not liable if they acted in a manner widely accepted in Australia by peer professional opinion as competent professional practice (s 5O(1)), unless the court considers that opinion irrational (s 5O(2)); differing opinions may each be relied on (s 5O(3)); and wide acceptance need not be universal (s 5O(4)). The defendant bears the onus of its preconditions; if they are made out, s 5O supplants s 5B; if not, ss 5B and 5C apply (Peterkin v Tadros [2026] NSWSC 1185 [151]–[154]).

  **Application**

  *Aisha — s 5O first.* Because Dr Mehta relies on s 5O, it is applied first, and he bears the onus (Peterkin). He is practising a profession, and extracting a tooth is a professional service. The difficulty is the second precondition. He must show that the rapid-extraction technique was widely accepted in Australia by peer professional opinion as competent professional practice. His own expert describes the technique as "promising", which is not the same as competent professional practice, and points only to its use by some dentists in Germany and Italy. The section speaks of acceptance in Australia, and overseas use does not satisfy it. In Australia the technique is used by Dr Mehta and one colleague. Section 5O(4) means acceptance need not be universal, but two practitioners out of a whole profession cannot be described as wide acceptance. The defence fails at its preconditions, so there is no need to consider irrationality under s 5O(2).

  *Aisha — ss 5B and 5C.* Because s 5O fails, the ordinary analysis applies (Peterkin). Following Benic, the risk of harm is fracture of the jaw from force applied during extraction of an impacted tooth.

  Section 5B(1)(a): Dr Mehta knew, or ought to have known, that a technique applying considerably more force to the jaw carries a risk of fracture. That is not far-fetched or fanciful (Wyong Shire Council v Shirt).

  Section 5B(1)(b): the risk was not insignificant. Weighing obviousness, likelihood and seriousness (Rail Corporation NSW v Donald): to a dentist it is obvious that more force means more risk to the jaw; the technique increases the likelihood of exactly this harm; and a fractured jaw is serious, as Aisha's surgery and six weeks on a liquid diet show.

  Section 5B(1)(c) and (2): the precaution is to use the standard sectioning technique. The probability of fracture was increased by the very feature that made the technique fast. The seriousness of a fractured jaw is considerable. The burden of the precaution was about 25 extra minutes per patient — a cost in convenience and income, but a modest one. The social utility of the faster technique is mainly Dr Mehta's ability to see more patients, which carries little weight against the risk to each patient. Measured against the common law standard of the ordinary skilled dentist (Rogers v Whitaker), and weighing these considerations together, a reasonable dentist in Dr Mehta's position would have used the standard technique.

  Two points under s 5C. First, Aisha cannot succeed merely by showing that the tooth could have been removed another way: s 5C(b) says that does not of itself give rise to liability. Her case rests instead on the s 5B weighing above. Secondly, Dr Mehta's decision to stop using the technique after her injury does not of itself give rise to liability and is not of itself an admission (s 5C(c)), so it is left out of account. On the s 5B analysis alone, Dr Mehta breached his duty.

  *Ben — s 5O.* Dr Hughes is practising a profession, and the aftercare decision formed part of a professional service. He must establish that not prescribing antibiotics after routine root canal treatment in a healthy patient was widely accepted in Australia by peer professional opinion as competent professional practice. The evidence of a substantial body of Australian practitioners, supported by a national clinical guideline, establishes that.

  Ben's answer is that another substantial body takes the opposite view. Section 5O(3) deals with that directly: differing peer professional opinions widely accepted in Australia do not prevent one of them being relied on. And s 5O(4) confirms that the opinion need not be universally accepted. The existence of a rival school does not defeat the defence.

  Ben's stronger argument is irrationality under s 5O(2). But it is the court, not Ben's expert, that must consider the opinion irrational, and the expert's label largely reflects his own school's disagreement. The opinion Dr Hughes relies on rests on a reasoned clinical concern — antibiotic resistance — and is reflected in a national guideline. The fact that infections are well known shows only that the practice accepts some risk, not that it is irrational. On these facts a court is unlikely to find the opinion irrational.

  If s 5O is made out, it supplants the s 5B analysis, and Dr Hughes is not liable (Peterkin). Only if a court found the opinion irrational would the case fall back to ss 5B and 5C, where the social cost of antibiotic resistance would itself be weighed among the considerations in s 5B(2).

  **Conclusion**

  Dr Mehta cannot rely on s 5O: a technique used by two practitioners in Australia, and praised only as "promising", is not widely accepted in Australia by peer professional opinion as competent professional practice, and overseas use does not count. On the ordinary ss 5B and 5C analysis, a reasonable dentist would have used the standard technique; the time saved does not justify the increased risk of fracture. Dr Mehta breached his duty to Aisha, and his later change of practice is irrelevant. Dr Hughes, by contrast, can probably rely on s 5O: his practice was widely accepted by a substantial body of Australian dentists and a national guideline; the rival school does not prevent reliance on it (s 5O(3)); and a reasoned concern about antibiotic resistance is unlikely to be found irrational. If the defence is made out, it supplants s 5B and Ben's claim fails.
