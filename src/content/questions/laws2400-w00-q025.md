  rule: |
    Under CLA s 5D(1), causation comprises (a) factual causation — the negligence was a necessary condition of the occurrence of the harm — and (b) scope of liability — it is appropriate for the scope of the negligent person's liability to extend to the harm so caused. Section 5D(1) treats them as separate and distinct issues (Adeels Palace v Moubarak (2009) 239 CLR 420), and s 5D(1)(a) is a statutory statement of the "but for" test (Strong v Woolworths Ltd (2012) 246 CLR 182). The plaintiff must prove any fact relevant to causation on the balance of probabilities (s 5E). For scope of liability, the court considers whether or not and why responsibility for the harm should be imposed on the negligent party (s 5D(4)).

    In March v Stramare (1991) 171 CLR 506 a truck parked in the middle of a six-lane road in the early morning was hit by a drunk, speeding driver. The High Court held that the truck owner's negligence was a cause of the injury: the chain is not broken where "the defendant's wrongful conduct has generated the very risk of injury resulting from the negligence of the plaintiff or a third party".

    A novus actus interveniens is a later event — the plaintiff's own act, a third party's act or a natural event — that exacerbates the original harm or causes new harm. If the chain is broken, the original wrongdoer is not liable for harm after the break. In McKew v Holland Hannen & Cubitts Ltd [1969] 3 All ER 1621 the plaintiff, whose injured leg was prone to give way, went down a steep staircase without a handrail, unaided. His unreasonable conduct was a novus actus, and the defendant was not liable for the second injury.

    Where tortfeasors are jointly and severally liable for the same damage, the plaintiff may recover the whole amount from any one of them. A tortfeasor held liable for the full amount may seek contribution from another tortfeasor (Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1946 (NSW) s 5(1)(c) and s 5(2)). Contribution does not affect the plaintiff, and the risk that one tortfeasor cannot pay lies with the other tortfeasors, not the plaintiff.

  **Issue**

  Three issues arise: (1) whether FreshCo's negligence and Dev's negligence each caused Zara's fractured pelvis, and whether her inattention or Dev's speeding breaks the chain from FreshCo; (2) whether either defendant is liable for the wrist fracture, or whether Zara's decision to use the fire stair broke the chain; and (3) from whom Zara can recover, given that Dev is bankrupt.

  **Rule**

  Under the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA") s 5D(1), causation has two elements: factual causation, a necessary condition of the harm (s 5D(1)(a)), and scope of liability (s 5D(1)(b)), considered by asking whether and why responsibility should be imposed (s 5D(4)). They are separate and distinct (Adeels Palace v Moubarak), and s 5D(1)(a) is the statutory "but for" test (Strong v Woolworths), proved on the balance of probabilities (s 5E). The chain is not broken where the defendant's conduct generated the very risk of injury from the negligence of the plaintiff or a third party (March v Stramare), but a plaintiff's own unreasonable later conduct may be a novus actus (McKew v Holland Hannen & Cubitts). Concurrent tortfeasors liable for the same damage are jointly and severally liable, with contribution between them under the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1946 (NSW) s 5.

  **Application**

  *1. The pelvis — Dev.* The question under s 5D(1)(a) is whether, but for Dev's speeding, Zara would have been injured. The collision expert says that at 50 km/h Dev would have stopped before reaching her. On the balance of probabilities (s 5E), his speeding was a necessary condition of the collision. Dev's complaint that Zara swerved without warning does not change that answer. It may matter to contributory negligence, in a later topic, but it does not show that the collision would have happened anyway.

  *The pelvis — FreshCo, factual causation.* But for the truck stopped across the bicycle lane, Zara would have continued along the lane and would not have swerved into the traffic lane. FreshCo's negligence was therefore also a necessary condition of her injury. FreshCo's argument that Zara and Dev "caused" the accident misunderstands the test: harm can have more than one necessary condition, and the existence of other causes does not stop FreshCo's negligence from being one of them. The truck did not touch Zara, but it does not need to; the question is whether the harm would have occurred without the negligence, and it would not.

  *The pelvis — FreshCo, does the chain break?* FreshCo's real argument is that the negligence of Zara and Dev should relieve it of responsibility. Under the CLA, that is a question of scope of liability (s 5D(1)(b) and (4)), kept separate from factual causation (Adeels Palace), and March v Stramare remains the guiding authority. The facts are close. In March, a truck parked in the middle of a road in the early morning was hit by a drunk, speeding driver, and the High Court held the truck owner liable because the truck generated the very risk of injury that materialised through another's negligence. Here, a truck parked across a bicycle lane, in darkness, without hazard lights, generated precisely the risk that a cyclist would see it late, swerve into the traffic lane and be struck by a car — including a car driven carelessly. Zara's inattention and Dev's speeding are the kinds of negligence that made FreshCo's conduct dangerous, not extraneous events. It is appropriate for FreshCo's liability to extend to the pelvis fracture.

  *2. The wrist.* Factual causation is satisfied for both defendants: but for the pelvis fracture, Zara would not have been on crutches with a weak leg, and the leg would not have given way. The question is scope of liability.

  McKew is almost on all fours. There, a plaintiff whose injured leg was prone to give way went down a steep staircase without a handrail, unaided, and his unreasonable conduct was held to be a novus actus. Zara also went down a steep stair without a handrail on a leg she knew was weak, and in addition she had a clear instruction from her physiotherapist not to, a lift available, and an unlit stairwell. Zara will say she was on crutches, not unaided, and that she was hurrying to a medical appointment that her injury made necessary. She may also point out that not every later careless act severs the chain (March v Stramare). But this was not a momentary lapse forced on her by the injury; it was a deliberate choice, against clear advice, to take an obvious risk to save a few minutes. Asking, as s 5D(4) requires, whether and why responsibility should be imposed, there is no good reason why FreshCo or Dev should answer for a fall Zara chose to risk when a safe alternative was waiting. The wrist fracture is probably outside the scope of both defendants' liability.

  *3. Recovery and contribution.* FreshCo and Dev are each liable for the same damage — the pelvis fracture and its consequences. They are jointly and severally liable, so Zara may recover the whole amount from either of them, and she should sue FreshCo, which can pay. FreshCo may then seek contribution from Dev under the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1946 (NSW) s 5(1)(c) and s 5(2). That is a matter between the tortfeasors alone: it does not reduce what Zara recovers, and the risk that Dev, as a bankrupt, cannot pay his share falls on FreshCo, not on Zara. The textbook notes that proportionate liability provisions can qualify this for property damage and economic loss claims, but Zara's claim is for personal injury.

  **Conclusion**

  Both FreshCo's negligence and Dev's negligence were necessary conditions of the pelvis fracture. Following March v Stramare, neither Zara's inattention nor Dev's speeding takes that harm outside the scope of FreshCo's liability, because the unlit truck across the bicycle lane generated the very risk that materialised. The wrist fracture is probably not recoverable from either defendant: like the plaintiff in McKew, Zara chose to go down a steep stair without a handrail, against clear advice, and that unreasonable conduct breaks the chain. Zara should recover the whole of her damages for the pelvis fracture from FreshCo, which may seek contribution from Dev, bearing the risk of his bankruptcy itself.
