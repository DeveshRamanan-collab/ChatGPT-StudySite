  **Issue**

  Three questions arise. First, whether the Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) is a treaty at international law and, if so, whether it presently binds Australia and Veloria. Second, whether Veloria can deny that it is bound by pointing to its own constitutional requirement of parliamentary approval. Third, whether Kestrel Lithium can enforce the MoU against the Commonwealth in the Federal Court.

  **Rule**

  Article 2(1)(a) of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) defines a treaty as "an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or in two or more related instruments and whatever its particular designation". Four elements follow: an agreement between states; in written form; governed by international law; and the label is irrelevant. Courts look past labels to substance: in *Aegean Sea Continental Shelf* a press communiqué was capable of being a binding international agreement; in *Qatar v Bahrain* signed minutes recording commitments the parties had consented to were a treaty; and in *Somalia v Kenya* a signed, dated MoU registered with the UN was held to be a treaty at international law — even though Australia's own DFAT practice treats an MoU as "less than a treaty".

  Who may bind a state is a matter for its own domestic law; in Australia, s 61 of the Constitution gives the executive the treaty-making power. Article 7 VCLT presumes that heads of state, heads of government and foreign ministers have authority; everyone else should produce full powers. Consent to be bound (arts 11–17) is distinct from entry into force, which occurs "in such manner and upon such date as [the treaty] may provide or as the negotiating States may agree" (art 24). Pending entry into force, art 18 obliges a state that has signed subject to ratification, or has consented pending entry into force, to refrain from acts that would defeat the treaty's object and purpose.

  Under art 46 a state may not invoke a violation of its internal law on competence to conclude treaties as invalidating its consent "unless that violation was manifest and concerned a rule of its internal law of fundamental importance" (*Cameroon v Nigeria*).

  Domestically, Australia is primarily dualist. Ratification of a treaty "has no direct legal effect upon domestic law" until specific legislation implements it (*Dietrich v The Queen*), and international law is not "a back door means of importing an unincorporated convention" (Mason CJ and Deane J, *Teoh*).

  **Application**

  *Is the MoU a treaty?* The document is an agreement between two states, Australia and Veloria, concluded through their ministers. It is in writing. The critical element is whether it is governed by international law. Several features point strongly that way: Clauses 2 and 3 use mandatory language ("shall ensure", "shall provide") rather than aspirational wording; Clause 9 expressly subjects disputes to international law rather than either party's domestic law; and Veloria registered the document with the UN Secretariat — the same feature that weighed in *Somalia v Kenya*. The heading "Memorandum of Understanding" is not decisive: art 2(1)(a) says the designation does not matter, and *Aegean Sea* and *Qatar v Bahrain* show that even informal documents can be treaties if the parties consented to be bound. DFAT's practice of treating MoUs as "less than a treaty" reflects Australia's own classification, but it cannot control the document's status at international law generally, particularly where the text itself points the other way. On balance, the MoU is likely a treaty.

  *Authority to sign.* Neither the Minister for Resources nor Veloria's Trade Minister is a head of state, head of government or foreign minister, so under art 7 neither is presumed to have authority; each should have produced full powers. The facts do not say whether they did. This is a point to confirm. On the Australian side, the omission of a National Interest Analysis, Executive Council approval and JSCOT tabling means Australia's own treaty-making process was not followed, but those are domestic procedures; they do not of themselves prevent the instrument from being a treaty.

  *Is it in force?* Even if the MoU is a treaty, Clause 11 sets its own mode of entry into force: an exchange of diplomatic notes confirming internal procedures. Under art 24, entry into force happens as the treaty provides. Australia has sent its note; Veloria has not. The treaty is therefore not yet in force, and neither party is bound by the detail of Clauses 2 and 3. However, art 18 may apply to Veloria as a signatory whose consent was, in substance, subject to completing its internal procedures: it must refrain from acts that would defeat the treaty's object and purpose until it has made clear its intention not to become a party. Selling its entire lithium output for five years arguably defeats the MoU's central purpose of priority supply. Against that, Veloria's new government has now said publicly that it regards the MoU as non-binding, which may amount to making clear its intention not to become a party, ending the art 18 obligation from that point.

  *Veloria's constitutional argument.* Ordinarily a state cannot rely on its own internal law to escape a treaty, protecting the other party's reliance (*Cameroon v Nigeria*). Here, however, the parliamentary-approval requirement is constitutional (plausibly "of fundamental importance") and was published in the gazette and routinely cited in negotiations — facts that suggest the violation would be objectively evident to Australia acting in good faith, and therefore "manifest". Veloria's art 46 argument is stronger than in the usual case, though the exception remains narrow.

  *Kestrel's proceedings.* Even if the MoU were a treaty in force, it would create no rights enforceable in Australian courts. Australia is dualist: under *Dietrich*, a treaty has no direct effect until implemented by legislation, and nothing suggests any legislation implements this MoU. *Teoh* confirms that international law cannot be used as a back door to import an unincorporated agreement. Kestrel's reliance on Clause 2 does not change this.

  **Conclusion**

  The MoU is probably a treaty at international law despite its label, given its mandatory language, international-law dispute clause and UN registration (*Somalia v Kenya*). It is not yet in force, because Clause 11 requires an exchange of notes that Veloria has not completed (art 24). Veloria may have breached its art 18 obligation not to defeat the treaty's object and purpose, but its public repudiation likely ends that obligation going forward. Veloria's art 46 argument is unusually strong because its constitutional rule appears manifest and fundamental. Kestrel's proceedings will fail: an unimplemented treaty has no direct effect in Australian law (*Dietrich*; *Teoh*). The government should confirm whether full powers were produced and, for future instruments, follow the NIA, Executive Council and JSCOT steps.
