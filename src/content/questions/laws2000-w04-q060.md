model: |
  The criteria are in Article 1 of the Montevideo Convention on Rights and Duties of States (1933): a permanent
  population, a defined territory, government, and capacity to enter into relations with other states. Although
  the Convention is regional with few parties, it is widely regarded as codifying customary international law.

  Population: no minimum is prescribed (Nauru about 12,000, China about 1.4 billion); what matters is a core
  population living there permanently, and the criterion does not relate to nationality. Territory: states cannot
  exist without territory, but there is no minimum size (the Vatican, about 0.5 sq km). Boundaries must be defined
  but need not be fixed or undisputed — North Sea Continental Shelf [46] says there is no rule that land frontiers
  must be fully delimited. Effective government: the most tricky; a coherent and effective exercise of public
  power, independent of any external entity (a colony would not qualify). Once statehood is established, a
  presumption of continuity means ineffective government (Somalia, Syria) does not end it, and no form of
  government is prescribed. Capacity for international relations: it arises from independence; Scotland may meet
  the other three criteria but cannot conduct international relations on its own.
