  **Thesis**

  The statement is partly right. Foster J's first argument and Handy J's approach cannot be understood without a theory of what law is: one appeals to the conditions under which positive law exists at all; the other treats legal forms as instruments of practical wisdom, much as the realists did. But the disagreement between Truepenny CJ, Keen J and Foster J's second argument is largely a disagreement about interpretation and the judicial office within a shared picture of law as the rules validly enacted by the legislature. On that axis the case can be decided without first settling what law is. The statement overstates how far jurisprudence decides adjudication, a lesson the unit teaches elsewhere with Raz and Finnis.

  **Argument**

  *Foster J's first argument: a theory of law.* Foster J says positive law is predicated on the possibility of human coexistence in society; *cessante ratione legis, cessat et ipsa lex*; the men were in a state of nature, so § 12-A did not apply to them. This is a claim about when there is law at all. It echoes the natural law idea that law has a point that conditions its existence, and it parallels Hart's own minimum content of natural law — rules protecting persons, property and promises as a natural necessity given the setting of human life. Foster J goes further than Hart: if the conditions fail, the law ceases. That is a theory of law, and it cannot be evaluated without one.

  *Handy J: a theory of law and of the judicial office.* Handy J treats forms and abstract concepts as instruments and decides by common sense and public opinion. This matches the realist claims that law in books differs from law in action, that legal concepts are not neutral, and that judges do or should decide by policy. It carries Holmes' thought that "the life of the law has not been logic; it has been experience". Handy J's judgment is therefore an application of a realist theory, not only a reading of § 12-A.

  *Truepenny CJ, Keen J and Foster J's second argument: interpretation within one picture.* The three remaining positions share an assumption: § 12-A is valid law made by the legislature, and the question is what it requires. Keen J says the legislature's supremacy requires plain meaning — "not the conjectural purpose of the rule, but its scope". Truepenny CJ agrees, leaving the rigours of the law to be mitigated by clemency. Foster J's second argument reads in an exception by analogy with self-defence: "a man may break the letter of the law without breaking the law itself". That is a question of "intelligent and unintelligent fidelity" to the same statute.

  Hart's account of rules shows these views need not diverge on the nature of law. A rule may have "exceptions incapable of exhaustive statement", and "a rule that ends with the word 'unless…' is still a rule" (CL 139). Real courts do what Foster J proposes: in *Church of the Holy Trinity v United States* a statute banning the importation of aliens under contract to perform labour "of any kind" was held not to apply to an English priest, and *Riggs v Palmer* can be read as reading in an exception for the beneficiary who murdered the testator. A positivist can accept those outcomes as interpretation of valid law. The dispute between Keen J and Foster J is about how a judge should read statutes and what legislative supremacy requires — about the judicial office — rather than about what makes a rule law.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest counterargument is Fuller's own. He wrote the case to bring out "divergent philosophies of law and government", and in the Hart–Fuller exchange on vehicles in the park, which Schauer calls "a debate within a debate", Fuller asked whether we can interpret a word in a statute without knowing its aim — his example was the misdemeanour of sleeping in a railway station. On that view, the choice between plain meaning and purpose is not separable from the nature of law. If law is the enterprise of subjecting conduct to the governance of rules, and that enterprise has a point, then a judge who ignores purpose misunderstands what law is. Dworkin supports this from another direction: principles such as that no one may profit by their own wrong have weight, and treating them as part of the law (TRS 22–24) is itself a theory of law.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument shows that interpretive views can be motivated by theories of law; it does not show that they must be. Schauer's question is the right one: do Hart and Fuller disagree about the concept of law, or about what judges do and should do? A positivist can be a purposivist about interpretation, and a natural lawyer can counsel plain meaning out of respect for legislative authority. The Week 7 contrast between Raz and Finnis teaches the same thing: the positivist Raz calls legal reasoning an instance of moral reasoning, while the natural lawyer Finnis calls it largely technical reasoning. Specific claims defended by specific arguments are separable, though not disconnected, from an author's general view of the nature of law.

  Tatting J's withdrawal is the best evidence. He sees that the statute has more than one purpose and that the self-defence exception has another explanation and an uncertain scope. His difficulty is not that he lacks a theory of law; it is that the materials point in more than one direction — the penumbra in Hart's sense.

  So the statement is right about Foster J's first argument and Handy J, and wrong about the rest. The case cannot be decided without a view about the judicial office and about interpretation; it can, for most of the judges, be decided without first deciding what law is.
