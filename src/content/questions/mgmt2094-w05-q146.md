model: |-
  Markman would reject the slogan: we should find more descriptions of the box. Most creative solutions are found
  inside the collective memory of the people working on a problem, and the statement of the problem is the cue to that
  memory. Change the description and different knowledge is retrieved. His Dyson example shows it: thinking about
  vacuum bags recalls bags, but "separate the dirt from the air" recalls other ways of separating particles from air,
  such as industrial cyclones. For the supermarket, a specific statement — "how do we sell more bruised fruit?" —
  retrieves close knowledge such as discounts and displays. Abstract statements — "how do we match the food we hold to
  the food people will eat in time?" or "how do we give surplus food a second use?" — can cue distant knowledge, perhaps
  from logistics or hospitality. Markman's point is to write several of each, not to hunt for one ideal statement. The
  limit is that redescription only works if someone in the group already knows something relevant, so a diverse team
  matters; it is cheap and repeatable, but not a substitute for knowledge.
