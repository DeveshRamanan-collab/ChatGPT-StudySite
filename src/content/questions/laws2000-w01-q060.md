  The Peace of Westphalia (1648) — the Peace Treaties of Munster and Osnabruck, ending the Thirty
  Years' War — is treated as a turning point because it supplied something the earlier natural-law and
  jus gentium debates had not: a single, clear source of authority for organising the world. Before
  1648, thinkers such as Grotius distinguished natural law (universal, existing 'within nature') from
  jus gentium (human-made law, 'variable in time and place', whose validity rested on states' consent
  rather than natural reason), and Vitoria had used natural law to argue that even native populations
  of the New World were entitled to its protection. These theories asked what higher or shared standard
  should govern relations between communities, but did not by themselves organise the world into
  discrete, authoritative units. Westphalia did exactly that: it organised 'territory and individuals
  into sovereign states' out of a 'need for a system that contained a clear source of authority', and is
  described as the 'birth of the notion of sovereignty' — an authority with absolute and indivisible
  power over people and territory. In that sense Westphalia did not so much refute jus gentium as give
  it a new, state-shaped container: once sovereign states existed as the basic units, jus gentium's
  state consent could operate between clearly defined sovereigns rather than the overlapping political
  and religious authorities (the Holy Roman Empire, the Catholic Church) of the late Middle Ages.
