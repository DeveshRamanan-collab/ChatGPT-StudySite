  The statement mistakes one moment in the ideation space for the whole method. Brown and Wyatt (2010) describe design thinking as a human-centred approach that uses consumer insights in depth and rapid prototyping to get beyond the assumptions that block effective solutions. Its process is a system of overlapping spaces, not orderly steps.

  Inspiration comes first: a brief, then observing what people actually do. Shanti's case shows why. The centre was close, affordable and well known, and habit was not the reason she stayed away; she could not carry its 5-gallon jerrican, and she would not buy a monthly card for more water than she needed. Observing her day would reframe the brief from "produce clean water" to "get clean water into homes like hers".

  Ideation is where the sticky notes belong: a diverse, T-shaped team synthesises those insights and brainstorms while deferring judgement — say, containers shaped for carrying on the head or hip, or buying smaller amounts. Implementation then prototypes the favourites with villagers and refines them, as VisionSpring did with eye screening.

  So brainstorming is one part of one space. The method's real demands are field observation before ideas and prototyping after them, and its biggest obstacle, Brown and Wyatt say, is fear of failure — which sticky notes do not solve.
