model: |
  Graham Barclay Oysters v Ryan shows that establishing a duty of care, and even a reasonably foreseeable and
  significant risk of harm, does not automatically mean that duty has been breached. Ryan and other consumers
  contracted Hepatitis A after eating oysters grown and distributed by Graham Barclay from a lake contaminated
  by faecal matter following a storm; the "depuration" process the grower used reduced, but did not
  eliminate, the viral load in the oysters. The trial judge found a breach of duty by all three defendants
  (Graham Barclay, the Great Lakes Council and the NSW government), but on appeal the outcome shifted: the
  Full Federal Court overturned the finding against the Council, and the High Court, by majority, ultimately
  held that the NSW government did not owe a duty of care at all, and that although Graham Barclay did owe a
  duty of care to consumers, that duty was not breached. Gummow and Hayne JJ explained why: although a risk
  of injury to oyster consumers was reasonably foreseeable, and the risk was of significant magnitude given
  the illness it could cause, it was not possible "to eliminate entirely the risk of contamination of
  oysters" grown at the lake. Critically, "the degree of probability of its occurrence" could not justify the
  "difficult, expensive and inconvenient alleviating action" the consumers argued should have been taken. In
  other words, breach in negligence is not established simply by showing a risk was foreseeable and serious;
  it requires weighing the probability of the risk eventuating against the cost, difficulty and
  inconvenience of the precautions that would have been needed to guard against it. Where, as in Graham
  Barclay Oysters, that burden is disproportionate to the probability of harm, a defendant can owe a duty of
  care, face a real and serious risk, and yet still not be in breach for failing to eliminate it entirely.
