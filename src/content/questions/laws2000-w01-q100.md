model: |
  Calling international law a 'living process' means it is not fixed once written down, but changes and
  evolves as states' practices and expectations change, particularly in response to new interests and
  technologies — and international environmental law shows exactly this kind of gradual change.
  Before the 1960s there was not much of an international legal framework for the environment at all.
  The United Nations then built momentum in stages: it hosted conferences (the UN Conference on the
  Human Environment, 1972), created programs and agencies (the UN Environment Program, and other
  agencies not dedicated to the environment, such as the Food and Agriculture Organization), and, through
  the General Assembly, produced documents such as the Rio Declaration (1992) that were not binding --
  because the General Assembly cannot itself create binding obligations — but that created the
  groundwork states later turned into binding treaties: the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change
  (1992), the Kyoto Protocol (1997), and the Paris Agreement (2015), followed by the ICJ's 2025 Advisory
  Opinion. The ozone example makes the same point numerically: the 1985 Vienna Convention and 1987
  Montreal Protocol did not eliminate ozone-depleting emissions overnight, but emissions fell sharply
  once those instruments took effect, and the UN now expects full recovery by 2040. In each case, the
  law developed through a named mechanism — non-binding UN documents building momentum, then binding
  treaties and protocols — rather than appearing all at once, which is what makes 'living process' more
  than just a metaphor.
