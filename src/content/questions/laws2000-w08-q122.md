model: |-
  Issue: when can the ICJ hear a contentious case between states, given that it has no compulsory jurisdiction? Law:
  only sovereign states may be parties (art 34 ICJ Statute); international organisations, including the UN, cannot be
  parties but can ask for advisory opinions (arts 65–68). Jurisdiction rests on the consent of states — to the
  specific case or to a category of disputes it falls within. Art 36 gives four ways: (1) a special agreement
  (compromis) submitting a particular dispute; (2) becoming party to a treaty specifying that disputes can be brought
  before the Court, often once other means have failed — e.g. art IX of the Genocide Convention; (3) a declaration
  under art 36(2) accepting jurisdiction over future legal disputes with another state accepting the same obligation
  (the optional clause); and (4) forum prorogatum, where a state that had not consented when the application was filed
  consents afterwards. A legal dispute must also exist at the date proceedings are instituted. Application: find the
  route the facts supply and check that it covers this dispute. Conclusion: if a route is satisfied the Court has
  jurisdiction, and it decides any disagreement about that itself (art 36(6)).
