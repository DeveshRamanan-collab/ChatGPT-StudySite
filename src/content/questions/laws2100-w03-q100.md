model: |-
  Hart's account is better as an explanation of existing legal systems, though Kelsen's answers a question Hart sets
  aside. Both concepts perform the same function: explaining validity and the unity of a legal order. For Hart, a rule is
  valid if it passes all the tests provided by the rule of recognition (CL 103); validity is membership, and the rule
  itself is a social rule whose existence is a complex, empirical question of fact, neither valid nor invalid but simply
  accepted, like the standard metre (CL 109). Its content may differ between jurisdictions. For Kelsen, validity is
  binding force; the chain of authorisation runs back to the historically first constitution, and the basic norm — always
  'who laid down the first constitution ought to be obeyed' — must be presupposed, a 'juristic hypothesis' or 'fiction'.
  The counterargument is that only Kelsen explains why norms bind: a fact about official practice cannot on its own
  generate an ought, and Kelsen insists validity cannot be a fact. But Hart's account ties the ultimate rule to what
  officials actually do, and he avoids Kelsen's view that law and morals cannot meaningfully conflict (CL 293). On balance
  Hart's account is better at describing legal systems, and that is what a descriptive theory is for.
