model: |-
  The issue is whether Hal's later negligence severs the chain of causation between Fay's negligence and Gil's injury, taking the injury outside the scope of Fay's liability under CLA s 5D(1)(b).

  Where one negligent act is followed by another, whether the later negligent act severs the causal connection is a matter of 'fact and degree' (Mahony v J Kruschich (Demolitions) Pty Ltd). Negligence of a third party, or even the plaintiff, does not necessarily sever the chain (Mahony; Burns v Pearce). This is especially so where the defendant's wrongful conduct has generated the very risk of injury resulting from the later negligence, and the injury occurs in the ordinary course of things (March v E & MH Stramare Pty Ltd; see, eg, Club Italia (Geelong) Inc v Ritchie).

  Fay's negligence forced pedestrians onto the road. The very risk that created was that a pedestrian on the road would be hit by a vehicle, including a carelessly driven one. Gil's injury is that risk coming home, and it happened in the ordinary course of things. Hal's carelessness is not a deliberate, volitional intervention of the kind that ordinarily severs the chain.

  Conclusion: Hal's negligence probably does not break the chain; Gil's injury is likely within the scope of Fay's liability (Hal may also be liable to Gil).
