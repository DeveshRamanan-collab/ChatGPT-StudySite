  Using Chapman v Hearse and Glass JA's explanation, explain why "reasonable foreseeability" cannot be treated as a single, uniform test across a negligence claim.
model: |
  Although the phrase "reasonable foreseeability" recurs throughout a negligence claim, it asks a different question at each of the three stages at which it appears, moving, in Glass JA's words, "from the general to the particular". At the duty stage, illustrated by Chapman v Hearse, the question is whether injury to a class of persons of which the plaintiff was one was reasonably foreseeable as a possibility — the emphasis is on the relationship between the parties, not on the precise sequence of events. In Chapman v Hearse itself, Chapman could not have foreseen the exact chain of events that led to Dr Cherry's death, but the High Court held this did not matter: it was enough that a collision on a dark, wet highway might foreseeably bring bystanders, such as a doctor rendering aid, into danger. At the breach stage, foreseeability instead focuses on the defendant's own conduct: whether the kind of carelessness charged against the defendant might foreseeably cause damage of some kind. At the remoteness stage, the focus shifts again, this time to the plaintiff's actual damage: whether the kind of damage suffered was itself a foreseeable outcome of that carelessness. Treating foreseeability as one uniform test would risk applying the wrong standard at the wrong stage — for example, wrongly requiring the precise manner of injury to be foreseeable at the duty stage, which Chapman v Hearse specifically rejects, or ignoring that breach and remoteness ask about the defendant's conduct and the plaintiff's damage respectively, not about the relationship between the parties. Recognising the three distinct inquiries is therefore essential to correctly structuring a negligence problem answer.
