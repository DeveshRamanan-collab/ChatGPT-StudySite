  Both provocation and contributory negligence are generally unavailable to reduce a defendant's liability
  for an intentional tort, and a shared policy idea connects their rejection: the qualitative difference
  between a defendant's deliberate wrongdoing and the plaintiff's own conduct, whether provocative or
  merely careless. In Fontin v Katapodis, Fontin argued that Katapodis's provocation (striking him with a
  T-square) justified reducing the damages payable for his own disproportionate response of throwing glass
  at Katapodis's face. The High Court held that provocation cannot reduce an award of compensatory damages,
  although it may still help reduce or prevent an award of exemplary damages — so provocation is not
  entirely without effect, but it cannot cut into the plaintiff's basic compensatory entitlement. Outside
  Queensland and Western Australia, provocation is not recognised as a tort defence at all. Similarly, in
  Horkin v North Melbourne Football Club, the trial judge initially reduced the club's liability by 30% for
  contributory negligence, based on Horkin's intoxication and failure to leave when asked. The Victorian
  Supreme Court reversed this, holding it unfair and unjust to reduce liability for an intentional battery
  on this basis: an intentional wrong and the plaintiff's carelessness differ "not merely in degree but in
  kind", carrying a markedly different social condemnation. The connecting policy idea is that the law
  treats deliberate, intentional wrongdoing as categorically more serious than a plaintiff's own
  provocative words or careless conduct, so a defendant who chooses to respond with an intentional tort
  should not be able to shift responsibility for that choice onto the plaintiff's lesser fault, whether
  that fault takes the form of provocation or simple carelessness.
