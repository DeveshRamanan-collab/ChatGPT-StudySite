  **Issue**

  Two separate issues arise. First, whether Callum's act of swinging the tyre iron at Priya as she cycled past constitutes the tort of assault. Second, whether Callum's arm striking Noor during his epileptic seizure amounts to trespass to the person (battery), given that he had no conscious control over the movement.

  **Rule**

  Assault is a direct and intentional act by the defendant that creates in the plaintiff a reasonable apprehension of an imminent physical interference with their person. The elements are: a positive act; direct interference; an intentional act; reasonable apprehension in the plaintiff of imminent harmful or offensive contact; and no lawful justification. Critically, the intention required is only the intention to cause apprehension of a battery, not an intention to actually commit the battery — an important distinction from battery's own fault element (Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd (2001) 53 NSWLR 98). Apprehension is assessed from the plaintiff's own position, but objectively: would a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position have apprehended the threat? Imminence does not mean immediate; a threat is imminent if there is no effective means of escape before it can be carried out, even if the harm would only occur a little later (Zanker v Vartzokas (1988) 34 A Crim R 11).

  Separately, every form of trespass to the person requires a voluntary, positive act by the defendant. A merely passive act will not suffice, and conduct performed during an epileptic fit is expressly recognised as not being a positive, voluntary act, along with conduct caused by the force of a third party or a mere omission. If the defendant did not voluntarily perform the interfering act, trespass cannot be made out no matter how directly the harm followed, and regardless of the physical contact and injury that in fact resulted.

  **Application**

  *Priya's claim — assault.* Callum's act of reaching for the tyre iron and swinging it towards Priya as she rode past was a positive, voluntary and direct act; the iron passed close enough that only her own reflexive action avoided contact, and the act was directed at her without any intervening event. The act was plainly intentional in the relevant sense — Callum meant to swing the iron towards Priya while shouting a threat — and, applying Rixon v Star City, it does not matter whether Callum actually intended or expected to make contact; it is enough that he intended to create the apprehension of imminent harmful contact, which the accompanying threat to "smash her head in" only reinforces. Reasonable apprehension is assessed objectively from Priya's position: a cyclist who sees a metal bar swung towards her head from a passing car, accompanied by a violent threat, would reasonably apprehend imminent harmful contact — and her reaction (losing control of the bike and falling) is itself strong evidence that she did apprehend exactly that. On imminence, there is no real difficulty here, since the swing occurred as Callum drew level with her and the iron missed by only centimetres; even applying Zanker v Vartzokas's broader principle that imminence does not require the harm to be truly instantaneous, so long as there is no effective means of escape before it is carried out, this was, if anything, an even clearer case of an imminent threat than Zanker itself, since the danger was immediate rather than merely impending. There is no lawful justification available to Callum on these facts — nothing about Priya's earlier swerve, however careless, could justify a retaliatory threat of violence with a weapon. All the elements of assault are made out.

  It is worth noting that Priya may also have a claim in battery or negligence for her fall and the resulting grazed knee and bruised hip, since the fright Callum's assault caused directly led to her losing control of the bike, but the question specifically asks about "the incident with the tyre iron," which is properly analysed as an assault: no contact between the iron and Priya's body occurred, so battery (which requires actual physical contact) is not made out for the iron itself, but the consequential injury from the fall would be a foreseeable consequence flowing from the assault for the purposes of damages.

  *Noor's claim — trespass to the person.* Noor's claim requires Callum's arm striking her to have been a voluntary, positive act. On the facts, it was not: Callum suffered a sudden, unexpected epileptic seizure with no warning, and his arm flailed involuntarily as a direct physical symptom of that seizure, striking Noor without any conscious decision, intention or control on his part. This is precisely the situation the law treats as lacking the voluntary act trespass requires — conduct occurring during a seizure is expressly not a positive act, regardless of how directly it caused contact or how real the resulting injury (bruising to Noor's arm) was. Unlike Priya's case, where Callum consciously chose to reach for the iron and swing it, Callum had no ability to choose, direct, or prevent the movement of his arm during the seizure. The fact that the contact was direct, and that Noor suffered actual physical injury, does not overcome the absence of a voluntary act; trespass to the person simply cannot be made out without one, however unfortunate the outcome for Noor.

  It is also relevant that Callum's medical history — a seizure-free decade and recent medical advice that a recurrence was very unlikely on his current medication — supports the conclusion that this was a genuinely involuntary and unforeseen event, reinforcing rather than undermining the absence of any voluntary act or, indeed, any prior fault on Callum's part in placing himself in a position where a seizure might occur.

  **Conclusion**

  Priya should succeed in an action against Callum for assault: his swing of the tyre iron was a direct, intentional act that created a reasonable and objectively justified apprehension of imminent harmful contact, and no lawful justification is available. She should recover compensatory damages for her fright and consequential injuries from the fall, and given the deliberate, threatening and gratuitous nature of Callum's conduct, aggravated damages are also available. Noor, by contrast, cannot succeed in trespass to the person against Callum: the arm movement that struck her occurred during an epileptic seizure and was not a voluntary, positive act, which is fatal to any trespass claim regardless of directness or the injury she suffered. Noor's only realistic avenue, if any, would lie outside trespass altogether — for example, in negligence, if it could somehow be shown that Callum unreasonably placed himself at risk of a seizure in a position where he might harm a passer-by — but nothing on these facts suggests that avenue is available to her either, given the medical reassurance he had received.
