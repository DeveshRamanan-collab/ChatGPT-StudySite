  Trace how the test for duty of care evolved from Donoghue v Stevenson to the modern Australian salient features approach, explaining why Australian courts rejected the English tests along the way.
model: |
  Donoghue v Stevenson established the neighbour principle in 1932: a person must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions they can reasonably foresee would injure their "neighbour" — anyone closely and directly affected by their conduct. This became the starting point for duty of care, applied in Grant v Australian Knitting Mills to a manufacturer/consumer relationship. From the 1960s, English courts expanded negligence into new territory: Hedley Byrne v Heller recognised duty for negligent misstatement causing pure economic loss, and Home Office v Dorset Yacht found a duty owed by a public authority in control of third parties. The House of Lords then tried to generalise duty of care into fixed tests: the two-stage Anns v Merton test (proximity, then policy limits) and later the three-stage Caparo test (foreseeability, proximity, fairness). Australian courts rejected both. Sutherland Shire Council v Heyman declined to follow Anns, and Caparo has never been incorporated into Australian law. Instead, Australian courts in the 1980s treated proximity itself as the organising principle — Deane J's "touchstone and control" of the categories in which a duty is recognised — before the High Court moved, from the late 1990s, to the "salient factors" approach: foreseeability remains the first step, but whether a duty arises in a novel case now depends on weighing a non-exhaustive, context-specific list of factors (control, vulnerability, reliance, proximity, coherence and so on) rather than applying one fixed formula. This reflects the High Court's view that duty of care is not a general duty, but a contextual one.
