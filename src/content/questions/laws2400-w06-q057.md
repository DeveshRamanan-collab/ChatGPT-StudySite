  Using Romeo v Conservation Commission (NT) and ss 5F–5H of the Civil Liability Act, explain how an occupier
  of land can owe entrants a duty of care while still not being in breach of it for failing to warn of, or

model: |
  Romeo v Conservation Commission (NT) draws a sharp distinction between the existence of a duty of care and
  its scope. The High Court accepted, as Kirby J and Toohey and Gummow JJ both recognised, that the Commission
  owed entrants to the reserve a general duty to take reasonable care against reasonably foreseeable risks of
  injury; foreseeability and proximity were plainly satisfied on the facts. But Kirby J stressed that "the
  critical question is commonly the measure or scope of that duty," not whether a duty exists at all. Toohey
  and Gummow JJ held that this duty did not extend to fencing off an area of natural beauty where the presence
  of a cliff was obvious, because the nature of a danger must be assessed "according to its obviousness and
  the care ordinarily exercised by the public" — a test drawn from Brennan J's dissent in Nagle. Because the
  cliff edge was plainly visible and the risk of falling was obvious to a reasonable entrant, a warning sign
  would have served no purpose for someone already aware of the danger, and fencing an entire two-kilometre
  stretch of coastline was not a reasonable response to an obvious risk. The Civil Liability Act now codifies
  this reasoning. Section 5F defines an "obvious risk" objectively, as one that would have been obvious to a
  reasonable person in the plaintiff's position, even if it has a low probability of occurring or is not
  conspicuous. Section 5G presumes the plaintiff was aware of any such obvious risk unless they prove
  otherwise on the balance of probabilities, effectively placing the practical burden on the plaintiff.
  Section 5H then removes the proactive duty to warn of an obvious risk altogether, subject only to narrow
  exceptions: where the plaintiff specifically requested advice about the risk, where a written law requires
  a warning, or where the defendant is a professional and the risk is one of death or personal injury arising
  from a professional service. Together, Romeo and ss 5F–5H mean an occupier can owe a general duty of care to
  entrants and yet not breach it, because the specific step the plaintiff says should have been taken (a
  fence or a warning) was never required once the danger was obvious.
