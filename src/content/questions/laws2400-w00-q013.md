  Advise Mei whether she can recover her $120,000 loss from the Council, from Grant, or from Coastline Realty.

  **Issue**

  Mei's $120,000 loss raises three questions: (1) whether Riverbend Shire Council owed her a duty of care in giving information about road-widening and, if so, breached it and caused her loss, taking account of the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA"); (2) whether Grant owed her a duty for his remarks at the barbecue; and (3) whether Coastline Realty is liable under s 18 of the Australian Consumer Law ("ACL").

  **Rule**

  Classify the loss first. Mei's loss — the fall in the value of her land, with nothing physically damaged — is pure economic loss: economic loss not resulting from physical injury to the person or property. CLA s 5 still treats it as "harm", because harm includes economic loss. But reasonable foreseeability, while always necessary, is not enough: as Dawson J said in Esanda Finance Corp v PMH (1997) 188 CLR 241, the law requires in addition "a special relationship between the parties".

  Liability for negligent words begins with Hedley Byrne v Heller [1964] AC 465: a duty may arise where the inquirer trusts the speaker to take care and reasonably relies, and the speaker knows or ought to know of the reliance (Lord Reid). The salient features — including indeterminacy, knowledge of reliance, reasonable reliance, assumption of responsibility, special skill and vulnerability — are weighed as a whole. In Shaddock & Associates Pty Ltd v Parramatta City Council (1981) 150 CLR 225 a council that told an intending purchaser, by phone and then by certificate, that no road-widening proposal affected land owed a duty: the duty covers information as well as advice and needs no special skill. In San Sebastian Pty Ltd v Minister (1986) 162 CLR 340 relying on a statement of intention was not reasonable; in Tepko Pty Ltd v Water Board (2001) 206 CLR 1 there was no duty where the authority neither knew nor ought to have known of reliance on a reluctant "ball-park" figure.

  Part 5 of the CLA modifies the common law for public authorities; breach is governed by s 5B and causation by s 5D. Separately, ACL s 18 prohibits conduct in trade or commerce that is misleading or deceptive or likely to mislead or deceive: no duty of care or negligence need be proved, though a causal link is still needed.

  **Application**

  *The Council — duty.* Shaddock is almost on all fours. Mei, an intending purchaser, asked a specific factual question about a specific lot and said why. The Council was the only body that could know of its own resolution, so Mei could not protect herself by checking elsewhere: she was vulnerable. The Council knew of her reliance because she told it — unlike the Board in Tepko, which did not know its estimate would go to a bank. Its answer was confirmed in writing at her request, which meets the doubt in Shaddock about a phone call alone. The statement was one of present fact, not of intention as in San Sebastian, so relying on it was reasonable. There is no indeterminacy: Mei is one identified purchaser, not an undefinable class like the lenders in Esanda. On balance, the Council owed Mei a duty of care.

  *The Council — CLA Part 5.* The Council is a "public or other authority" under s 41. Section 42 limits its functions by the resources reasonably available, protects its general allocation of resources from challenge, and lets it rely on compliance with general procedures. None of this helps it: Mei does not ask the Council to spend more money, only to answer from the register it already kept, and its own procedure manual required that check, which the officer did not make. Sections 43 (breach of statutory duty), 43A (special statutory powers), 44 (failure to regulate) and 46 (exercise of functions) are not engaged by a negligence claim over information that anyone could give.

  *The Council — breach.* Under s 5B(1)(a) the risk that an inaccurate answer would cause a purchaser serious financial loss was one the Council knew or ought to have known of, and under s 5B(1)(b) it was not insignificant: Mei had told it she was buying. Considering s 5B(2), the probability of harm if the register went unchecked was high; the likely seriousness was great — a buyer's life savings; the burden of checking an existing register was slight; and while answering planning enquiries has real social utility, that utility depends on the answers being checked. A reasonable council would have checked (s 5B(1)(c)). The Council breached its duty.

  *The Council — causation.* Under s 5D(1)(a) the question is whether the negligence was a necessary condition of the loss — whether Mei would not have bought had she been told the truth. Because this turns on what she would have done, s 5D(3)(a) requires it to be decided subjectively in light of all the circumstances, and s 5D(3)(b) makes her later statement that she "would never have bought" inadmissible, because it is not against her interest. Her conduct speaks for her anyway: she asked specifically about road-widening, insisted on written confirmation, and needed the lot's full depth for her house. Factual causation is likely made out. Scope of liability (s 5D(1)(b), (4)) raises no difficulty: the loss is exactly what accurate information was meant to prevent.

  *Grant.* Grant spoke "between sausages" at a barbecue to someone he had just met — the paradigm of the "social or informal occasion" on which, Lord Reid said, there can be no duty. Although he has a surveyor's background, he did not undertake to apply that skill for Mei, and his comment that he could not "see the council touching that road for years" was a prediction about the Council's future conduct, not information he held; as in San Sebastian, relying on such a statement is not reasonable. He owed no duty. Nor can Mei use s 18, because Grant was not acting in trade or commerce.

  *Coastline Realty.* An agency marketing land for sale in the course of its business acts in trade or commerce. Its brochure stated as a fact that no road-widening proposal affected the lot; that was false and, on its face, misleading or deceptive. Because s 18 needs neither a duty nor negligence, the agency's honesty and its reliance on the vendor are not of themselves an answer. Mei must still show a causal link, though nowhere near the extent required at common law; she read the brochure before contacting the Council, so she has a real argument.

  **Conclusion**

  Mei's strongest claim is against the Council: on Shaddock it owed her a duty for pure economic loss caused by negligent misstatement, CLA Part 5 does not displace that duty, it breached s 5B by not checking its own register, and on the circumstances — not her after-the-event statement — its negligence was a necessary condition of her $120,000 loss. She has no claim against Grant, whose remarks were a casual social prediction made outside trade or commerce. She also has a claim against Coastline Realty under ACL s 18, which needs no duty of care, subject to the causal link. She may run both claims, but cannot recover the same loss twice.
