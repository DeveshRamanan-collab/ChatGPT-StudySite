  Positivists have long said that the existence of law is one thing and its merit or demerit another. Austin put it in those words; Hart restated the point as "the simple contention that it is in no sense a necessary truth that laws reproduce or satisfy certain demands of morality, though in fact they have often done so" (CL 185–186).

  Critics have attacked the claim from several directions. Fuller argued that law is the enterprise of subjecting human conduct to the governance of rules, and that the enterprise has an inner morality — laws must be general, public, prospective, clear, coherent, practicable and stable, and official action must be congruent with them. Finnis argued that no theorist can describe and analyse social facts "without also participating in the work of evaluation" (NLNR 3), and that in its central case law's authority depends on its justice. Dworkin denied that the question of what the law is can be kept apart from the question of what it ought to be.

  **Thesis**

  The statement is half right. If the separation thesis is read, as Hart reads it, as the denial that "it is … a necessary truth that laws reproduce or satisfy certain demands of morality" (CL 185–186), then Fuller's procedural natural law and the strong reading of *lex iniusta non est lex* do not defeat it: Fuller asserts a connection Hart concedes, and the strong thesis meets Hart's reply that law and morals must be able to conflict. But the statement is wrong about Finnis and Dworkin. Their blow lands, not on the separation thesis as a claim about legal validity, but on legal positivism as a methodology — the claim that law can be described in a morally neutral way. Stating the separation thesis precisely therefore protects it only by narrowing what it claims.

  **Argument**

  *The thesis at its strongest.* Austin: "The existence of law is one thing; its merit and demerit another" (PJD 157). Green and Adams state positivism as "the thesis that the existence and content of law depends on social facts and not on its merits". In 'Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals' Hart insists that Bentham and Austin "were not dry analysts fiddling with verbal distinctions while cities burn". They never denied that many legal rules mirror moral rules, or that moral principles may enter a legal system through explicit provisions. They asserted two simple things: a rule's violating morality does not show that it is not law, and a rule's being morally desirable does not show that it is law (599). The separation thesis, properly stated, denies a necessary connection; it does not deny every connection.

  *Six claims, not one.* Hart's chapter IX breaks the "necessary connection" into six claims: power and authority, the influence of morality, interpretation, criticism of law, principles of legality and justice, and validity and resistance. Of the influence of morality and the principles of legality he says that, if this is what the necessary connection means, its existence should be conceded. This is the key to evaluating the statement. A critic lands a blow only if the connection they assert is one Hart denies.

  *Fuller.* Fuller asked Hart to "concern himself more closely with a definition of law that will make meaningful the obligation to fidelity to law" (635), and restated the debate as the distinction "between order and good order" (644). His eight principles are an inner morality: morality within law, not an external standard for evaluating it. Rex's failure is not that his laws are unjust but that nobody could comply with them. Hart's response is concession, not denial: perhaps "a minimum of justice is necessarily realized whenever human behaviour is controlled by general rules publicly announced and judicially applied" (CL 206), but the inner morality "is unfortunately compatible with very great iniquity" (CL 207). On this line the statement is right. Fuller's connection is real, but it is the fifth of Hart's six claims, one Hart accepts, and it says nothing about the content of the rules. A system can be general, public and prospective and still be wicked.

  *Strong and weak natural law.* The strong natural law thesis — moral soundness is a necessary element of legal validity — does contradict separation. But it meets Hart's reply. One reason Hart speaks of a rule of recognition rather than a basic norm is "to avoid any commitment to Kelsen's view of the conflict between law and morals" (CL 293): Hart wants to be able to say that a rule is valid law and too iniquitous to obey. On the strong thesis that sentence is incoherent, which is a cost to the strong thesis rather than to Hart. Finnis's weak thesis is different. Unjust laws are "not laws" only in the way that a fallacious argument is not properly an argument: measured against the central case they are defective, but that "is not to say they are legally invalid". So the weak thesis does not deny what Hart asserts either. Here too, the statement holds.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest objection is that the precise statement of the thesis hides where the natural law critics are actually aiming. Finnis says that "no theorist can give a theoretical description and analysis of social facts without also participating in the work of evaluation" (NLNR 3). Hart himself selects a central case — the union of primary and secondary rules — and gives explanatory priority to the internal point of view, yet treats that viewpoint as an amalgam of "calculations of long-term interest; disinterested interest in others; an unreflecting inherited or traditional attitude; or the mere wish to do as others do" (CL 203). Finnis's point is that by what criteria a case is central, and from what viewpoint, cannot be answered without asking what practical reasonableness really requires. If so, Hart's descriptive method already relies on evaluation.

  Dworkin presses the same point from a different direction. The "ruling theory of law" combines positivism with utilitarianism and assumes that the two are independent (TRS xii); Dworkin rejects that assumption, and with it the separation of the is question from the ought question. For Dworkin a general theory of law must be normative as well as conceptual. On this view the separation thesis is not refuted on its own terms; it is shown to rest on a methodological choice that is itself contestable.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument succeeds, but only against a claim the statement does not make explicit. It is important to separate the three senses of legal positivism: a general theory of law (the social facts and separability theses), a methodology (legal theory as a morally neutral, descriptive enterprise), and a political theory (moral criteria of validity are undesirable). Finnis's and Dworkin's arguments bite on the second. They do not show that an unjust statute passed in the proper form is not valid law in the system that enacted it; indeed Finnis's own weak thesis concedes its validity. Hart's minimum content of natural law shows how much he can grant without giving up the first sense: rules protecting persons, property and promises are a "natural necessity" (CL 199), not a conceptual connection.

  So the correct conclusion is that the separation thesis survives as a claim about the existence and validity of law, and survives Fuller and the weak natural law thesis comfortably. The statement nonetheless overreaches. Its word "none" assumes that the only interesting question is the one Hart chose to answer. The deeper challenge of Finnis and Dworkin is that choosing which features of law are important enough to describe is itself an evaluative act. Stated precisely, the separation thesis wins the argument about validity by leaving that challenge unanswered.
