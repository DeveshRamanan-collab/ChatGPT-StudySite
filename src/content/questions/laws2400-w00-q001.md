  **Issue**

  Two questions arise. First, whether Marco committed the tort of battery against Levi in (a) the single punch, and (b) the bar stool strike. Second, whether Marco can rely on self-defence, at common law and under the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW) ("CLA"), to excuse either or both acts.

  **Rule**

  Battery is a direct and intentional (or negligent) act of the defendant that causes physical contact with the body of the plaintiff without consent or other lawful justification. The elements are: a voluntary and positive act; direct interference; intentional or negligent contact; physical interference with the plaintiff's body; and that the touching was unlawful. Any touching without a lawful excuse is unlawful, however slight, because every person's body is inviolate (Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd (2001) 53 NSWLR 98). Once Levi proves the direct interference, the onus shifts to Marco to disprove fault or establish a defence such as self-defence.

  At common law, Zecevic v DPP (1987) 162 CLR 645 requires the defendant to have had a reasonable apprehension of the need to use force to defend himself, and to have used force that was a proportionate response to that threat. Proportionality is assessed strictly: in Fontin v Katapodis (1962) 108 CLR 177, the defendant, struck across the arm with a wooden T-square, responded by throwing a glass at the plaintiff's face; the High Court held this response was "out of all reasonable proportion to the emergency" he faced, and self-defence failed even though some response might have been justified.

  The CLA now codifies and modifies the common law. Section 52 provides that a person incurs no liability for conduct carried out in self-defence, but only where the conduct responded to was itself unlawful, the defendant believed the conduct necessary to defend himself or another, and the response was reasonable in the circumstances as he perceived them. NSW v McMaster [2015] NSWCA 228 illustrates the section applied to an officer who shot a man running towards him with a metal rod, finding both limbs satisfied on those facts. Section 53 gives a narrow residual discretion to award limited damages, excluding non-economic loss, only in exceptional circumstances where refusing damages would be harsh and unjust — even where the response was not reasonable. Although s 3B generally excludes intentional torts done with intent to injure from the Act, s 3B(1)(a)(ii) expressly preserves Part 7 (self-defence), so Marco can still invoke ss 52–53 despite having intentionally struck Levi.

  **Application**

  *The elements of battery.* Both the punch and the stool strike were voluntary, positive, direct and intentional acts causing physical contact with Levi's body. The only live issue for each is whether the contact was unlawful — that is, whether self-defence excuses it.

  *The punch.* Levi's unprovoked grabbing and shouted threat to "smash his face in" gave Marco a reasonable apprehension that he was about to be struck. With Levi holding him against the bar and no means of retreat, a single punch to break free and neutralise the immediate threat was a proportionate response to what Levi was doing at that moment. This satisfies Zecevic's two limbs. Under the CLA, Levi's grabbing and threatening conduct was itself unlawful (an assault and battery on Marco), satisfying s 52(1)(a); Marco believed the punch necessary to defend himself, and that belief, and the response itself, were reasonable in the circumstances as he perceived them. Self-defence therefore succeeds for the punch, both at common law and under s 52, and Marco incurs no liability for that act. That Levi's underlying suspicion about the rumour was mistaken is irrelevant: self-defence is judged by what Marco reasonably perceived and responded to, not by whether Levi's provocation was objectively justified.

  *The bar stool strike.* This is a different act, assessed separately. By the time Marco picked up the stool, Levi had released his grip, staggered backwards, and was dazed against the bar; staff and other patrons were already intervening. There was no continuing threat of force from Levi requiring any further response, let alone one involving a weapon capable of causing serious injury. This mirrors Fontin v Katapodis: just as throwing glass at a face was a wildly disproportionate response to a blow from a T-square, swinging a heavy glass bar stool into the head of a man who had already disengaged and was no longer a threat is disproportionate to any apprehension Marco could reasonably still hold. The first limb of Zecevic (reasonable apprehension) is doubtful once Levi had backed away, and even if some residual apprehension remained, the second limb (proportionality) plainly fails.

  The same conclusion follows under the CLA. Section 52(1)(a) is still satisfied, since Levi's earlier conduct was unlawful, but s 52(2) requires that Marco's response be reasonable in the circumstances as he perceived them. Marco says he was still frightened, but an honest belief is not enough — the response itself must also be objectively reasonable on the facts as perceived, and escalating to a weapon against a man already staggering away from a stunning blow was not a reasonable response to any threat then facing him. Self-defence under s 52 therefore fails for the stool strike.

  Section 53's discretion to award limited damages despite an unreasonable response is unlikely to assist Marco either: it requires exceptional circumstances where refusing damages entirely would be harsh and unjust, and nothing here — a deliberate escalation to a weapon against an already-disabled opponent — suggests the sort of exceptional case s 53 contemplates. Section 54 (barring damages where the plaintiff's own conduct was a serious offence) is also unlikely to assist Marco: while Levi's initial grabbing and threat could constitute an offence, it did not "materially contribute" to the separate stool-strike injury in the way s 54 requires, because by then Levi's conduct had already ceased.

  **Conclusion**

  Marco has a complete defence to Levi's claim in relation to the punch: it was a proportionate, reasonable response to an ongoing threat, satisfying both the common law test in Zecevic v DPP and s 52 of the CLA. However, the bar stool strike is a separate act of battery for which self-defence fails at common law (following the disproportion reasoning in Fontin v Katapodis) and under s 52, because by that point Levi no longer posed a threat and swinging a weapon at his head was not a reasonable or proportionate response. Levi should succeed in an action for battery limited to the bar stool strike, and may recover compensatory damages for his fractured cheekbone, concussion and laceration, with aggravated damages also available given the deliberate and gratuitous nature of that particular act once the initial confrontation had ended. Levi cannot recover for the punch itself, as it was fully justified.
