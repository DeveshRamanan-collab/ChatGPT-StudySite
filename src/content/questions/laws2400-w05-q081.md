  The materials list several established categories of duty of care: a driver of a vehicle owes a duty to other road users; an employer owes a duty to its employees; a doctor owes a duty to their patients; a manufacturer owes a duty to consumers or users of its products, as recognised in Donoghue v Stevenson and applied in Grant v Australian Knitting Mills; and a solicitor owes a duty to exercise due care for their clients. Classifying a case as falling within one of these established categories simplifies a negligence problem answer because it avoids the need for a full "salient features" analysis. Where the relationship between plaintiff and defendant has already been recognised by prior case law as giving rise to a duty of care, a student can simply identify the category and cite the relevant authority — for example, "this is a manufacturer/consumer case, an established category since Donoghue v Stevenson" — rather than working through the longer, more fact-intensive list of salient factors reserved for novel cases outside the paradigm of physical injury or property damage. This saves time in a timed exam and focuses the answer on genuinely contested elements. It is important, however, to check the facts do not pull the case away from the paradigm — as in Chapman v Hearse, where an ordinary road-accident category still raised a live duty question because of its unusual chain of events — before treating the classification as settled.
