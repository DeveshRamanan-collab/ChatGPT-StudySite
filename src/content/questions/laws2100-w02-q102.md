model: |-
  I agree, with one qualification: the habits/rules distinction is the centre of Hart's case, but it works only
  because Austin's sovereignty depends on habits. Austin's sovereign is the determinate superior whom the bulk of
  society habitually obeys and who habitually obeys no one else; so the whole theory rests on the habit of obedience.
  Chapter IV attacks that pillar: it cannot explain the continuity or the persistence of law, nor fit real systems where
  no one has legally unlimited power or stands above the law. The deeper point is the distinction. Habits and rules
  both show convergence of behaviour, but deviation from a rule is criticised, the rule is a good reason for the
  criticism, and social rules have an internal aspect. Habits "cannot confer rights or authority on anyone" (CL 60). The
  counterargument is that Chapter III's objections — power-conferring rules, range, custom — do independent damage.
  They do, but each is an instance of the same gap: without the idea of a rule, the theory cannot explain legal
  obligation. On balance the distinction is the root, and the other objections are its symptoms.
