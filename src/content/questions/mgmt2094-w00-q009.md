  **Thesis**

  R&D spending can buy invention; it cannot by itself buy innovation. Innovation, in the unit's definition, is invention plus commercialisation, and the evidence in the materials is that the harder half is the second one. Spending more on R&D is therefore neither sure nor sufficient: Dow Corning spent heavily on world-class science while its share of revenue from new products halved. Money helps — slack resources are a genuine source of innovation — but what turns spending into innovation is a commercialisation process, a strategy and innovation model that direct the spending, and the organisational change to make both stick.

  **Argument**

  *Invention is not innovation.* Week 7 defines invention as a novel product, device, process or concept, requiring knowledge, competence and financial aid; innovation is the introduction of a newer and better solution that meets new requirements or existing market needs, and it also requires knowledge about market needs and trends. Week 8's table adds that invention may not be commercialised, can have a non-economic motive and is usually restricted to the R&D centre, while innovation results in commercialisation, has an economic motive, spreads across the organisation and brings organisational change. R&D budgets fund the first column. The statement assumes the second follows automatically.

  *Dow Corning.* The Week 9 guest lecture shows that it does not. Dow Corning spent 10% of revenue on R&D, and universities used its scanning electron microscope. Yet its New Product Market Share — the percentage of revenue from newly commercialised products — fell from around 25% in the 1970s and 80s to 13% by 1995, and a new product took on average four years to commercialise. Leadership concluded that the way it commercialised products needed to improve. Its first attempt, a consultant-designed process in 1997, failed because poor change management practices led to poor take-up. What worked was Design for Six Sigma: a structured process that researched customers, translated their needs into critical-to-customer requirements, explored alternative concepts and ran experiments alongside economic data. Its pilot, the ArmorAll Deck Protector, produced over 4,500 tonnes and US$40 million of revenue in 12 months. Even then the project stopped when ArmorAll lost a legal battle over its TV commercials: no commercialisation process can control a customer's poor risk management.

  *Spending without a guaranteed return.* Lego's investment shows the limits of money from another side. It wants a brick from plant-based or recycled materials by 2030 that still clicks together, keeps its colours and survives hard use; around 200 alternatives have been tried and most have fallen short, and its chief executive said it was difficult to know the financial implications. The spending serves a clear goal of innovation, reducing environmental damage, but the result depends on the three circles: desirable, possible and viable. Disney's interactive division, which lost more than $1 billion over five years in the red, shows that investment in new technology can run for years without a return.

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the statement is that some innovations need large, patient investment. Week 8 lists slack resources as a source of innovation: abundance lets a firm afford innovations, bear the cost of instituting them and absorb failures. Collis warns that some ideas cannot be evaluated in quick, cheap experiments — Tesla's lithium ion batteries would never have got off the ground had they been expected to demonstrate immediate success. Iger's lesson from the interactive division was not to stop investing but that "if you're going to win big, you have to get in early". On this view, cutting R&D to save money is the surest way to stop innovating.

  **Evaluation**

  The counterargument shows that money is necessary for some innovations, not that it is the surest route. The lecture itself calls slack double-edged: abundant resources let an organisation experiment and fail until it finds the right solution, but they can also make it feel unthreatened, and firms at the top that felt unbeatable did not invest in innovation, and failed. BCG's six innovation models show successful innovators spending in very different ways. Reckitt Benckiser, a fast follower, spends minimal energy up front on consumer insight and ideation and invests downstream in product testing, and its flat structure maximises speed to market. Innovators fall short, BCG says, by pursuing the wrong model for their context or by failing to support a good model with the capabilities it requires — not by underspending. Even Collis's case for patient investment comes with his insistence that strategy act as a screen and a yardstick, so that money goes to the opportunities that fit.

  So the statement should be rejected. More R&D can produce more inventions, and some innovations cannot happen without it. But the surest way to become more innovative is to fix the route from laboratory to market: measure it, as Dow Corning measured New Product Market Share; build a commercialisation process people will actually take up; and direct the spending with a strategy and an innovation model that suit the firm.
