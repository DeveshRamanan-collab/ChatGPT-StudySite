  **Thesis**

  The statement takes a partial truth too far. People do differ: the Whole Brain Model and the four creative thinking preferences show that individuals prefer some modes of thinking and avoid others. But the unit's theories and readings point overwhelmingly to creativity as a normal human capacity that depends on preparation, practice, knowledge and environment — all things an organisation can build. Even the apparent gifts and accidents in the materials turn out to rest on prepared minds. An organisation should hire for a mix of thinking styles, but it should also train, prepare and organise creativity, because that is where most of it comes from.

  **Argument**

  *Grace and accident, examined.* Creativity by grace treats creativity as a divine gift; on this view, as the lecturer put it, you are either super creative or you are not, and nothing can be done about it. Creativity by accident sees it arising by chance. The readings qualify both. Sadler-Smith, citing Cropley, notes that Becquerel could not have discovered radioactivity from the chance coming together of uranium salts and photographic paper in a drawer had he not already been deeply immersed in the relevant research: "chance favours the prepared mind". Wallas' model makes Preparation the first stage, and it depends on domain-specific knowledge. Helmholtz's "happy ideas" came on slow walks up wooded hills only after he had investigated a problem "in all directions"; Poincaré's ideas "arose in crowds" after fifteen days of fruitless work, and still had to pass verification. What looks like grace or luck is the visible end of long, conscious effort.

  *Association and cognition.* Creative association — applying procedures from one area to another — underlies divergent thinking techniques such as lateral thinking and brainstorming, and it can be practised. Darwin, a biologist, drew on Malthus' political economy. Cognitive creativity treats creativity as a normal human activity, about reasoning, recognition and understanding. Claman agrees: everyone has creativity when confronted with new problems or new ideas; it is "not just for the talented few", but it fades when people get bored or discouraged and a groove becomes a rut. Her remedies — think new, look for intersections, capitalise on obstacles, share what you know — are habits, and habits can be built.

  *Creativity can be trained.* Week 1's creativity gym describes three stages: starting off (learning and developing the skills, including knowing the industry you are entering), being proficient (training your mind and getting inspiration) and becoming an expert (going into creative mode by default, then receiving feedback). The individual competencies — exploring problems, building up willpower, self-awareness, openness to failure and feedback — can be developed; the lecturer said some people are naturally curious and others can develop it by exploring problems in a systematic way. Mark built a furniture business on knowledge gained in an earlier clothing business and three years working for the owner of a recycled-timber furniture business: "my university was having worked in the industry".

  **Counterargument**

  The strongest case for the statement is that individual differences are real and stable. The Whole Brain Model profiles each person as preferring, being competent in or avoiding each quadrant; the four preferences — clarifier, ideator, developer, implementer — describe different natural styles. Week 11's individualistic school holds that innovations result from unique individual talents and that innovators are born, and Week 5's psychoticism theory says all creative people have a disposition that forms the basis of creative personalities. If some people are reliably ideators and others are not, hiring ideators looks efficient.

  **Evaluation**

  Individual differences are better evidence for building teams than for giving up on training. The lecturer's advice on the Whole Brain Model is to be aware of a quadrant you avoid and consciously push yourself towards it when the problem needs it, and the four preferences are meant to be combined, so that a team solves a problem more effectively by "taking into account the different paths to the solution". Wallas' model, too, "needs time and a variety of people". Week 11 sets the individualistic school beside the social deterministic school, in which innovation occurs when conditions are right, as when Covid moved teaching, work and services online. And Week 1's team competencies — supporting others' ideas, psychological safety, shared visions — matter only if creativity is something a group produces, not something each hire brings fully formed.

  So the statement should be rejected. Organisations should hire for a mix of preferences, but creativity is mostly made: through preparation and domain knowledge, practised habits, diverse teams and environments in which people feel safe to take risks. An organisation that hires talent and never develops it will find that its talent, too, ends up in a rut.
