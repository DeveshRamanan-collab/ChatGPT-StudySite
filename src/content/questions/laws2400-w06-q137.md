  Wicks v State Rail Authority (NSW) establishes the correct order of analysis for a psychiatric harm claim
  under Part 3 of the Civil Liability Act, and explains why getting that order wrong risks reading one
  provision divorced from its statutory context. Two police officers attended the scene of the Waterfall
  train derailment, where State Rail had admitted negligence, and after spending several hours assisting the
  injured, both later suffered psychiatric injury. At first instance and on appeal, the case had been treated
  as turning entirely on s 30(2)(a) — whether the officers had "witnessed, at the scene, the victim being
  killed, injured or put in peril." The High Court held this approach skipped an important anterior question:
  section 32 defines or controls what would otherwise be a duty of care arising at common law, and so it must
  be considered before the limitation in s 30(2), because s 30 only becomes relevant once a relevant duty of
  care has actually been found to exist. Applying that order here, s 32(1) asks whether the defendant ought
  to have foreseen that a person of normal fortitude might, in the circumstances of the case, suffer a
  recognised psychiatric illness if reasonable care were not taken, having regard to the four circumstances in
  s 32(2): whether the harm resulted from a sudden shock, whether the plaintiff witnessed the victim being
  killed, injured or put in peril at the scene, the plaintiff's relationship to the victim, and any
  pre-existing relationship between plaintiff and defendant. Once a duty is established, s 31 requires that
  the harm consist of a recognised psychiatric illness before any damages can be awarded for pure mental harm.
  Only then does s 30(2) become relevant, limiting recovery for pure mental harm arising from shock to
  plaintiffs who witnessed the victim being killed, injured or put in peril at the scene, or who are a close
  member of the victim's family. On the facts, the High Court held the officers did witness victims being
  injured or put in peril at the scene, because the survivors of the derailment remained in peril until they
  had been rescued to safety — it would be wrong to confine "being put in peril" to the instant of the
  derailment itself, since not every such event begins and ends in a moment. The order matters because
  analysing s 30(2) first, in isolation, risks treating a limitation on an existing duty as though it were the
  test for whether a duty exists at all; applying s 32 first keeps the focus on foreseeability as the true
  basis of the duty, with ss 30 and 31 operating only as further constraints once that duty has already been
  established.
