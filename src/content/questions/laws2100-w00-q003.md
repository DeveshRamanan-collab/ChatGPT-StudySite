  Austin defined law as a species of command: a wish that another shall do or forbear, backed by an evil to be incurred on non-compliance, issued by a superior with the might to enforce it. The gunman obliges his victim, but the victim has no obligation; power-conferring rules, laws that bind their makers, and customary law do not fit the model of orders backed by threats; and a habit of obedience cannot explain the continuity and persistence of law.   **Thesis**

  The statement gets the charge the wrong way round. Hart did not change the subject: he criticised Austin on Austin's own metatheoretical criterion, that a descriptive theory of positive law must fit the facts of real legal systems, and showed that the command theory fails it. It is Schauer's objection that changes the question — from what is necessary to the concept of law to what is most salient in the experience of law. Answered on that level, the objection shows that coercion is a salient feature of many legal systems, which Hart never denied. It does not show that a theory which treats coercion as inessential fails to fit the facts.

  **Argument**

  *Three kinds of question.* A claim can be judged only once we know which kind it is. "Is coercion a necessary feature of law?" is conceptual. "Is law as it is experienced mostly coercive?" is empirical. "What should a theory of law capture?" is metatheoretical. Much of this debate consists of answers to one question offered as refutations of answers to another.

  *Austin and Kelsen at their strongest.* Austin defines law *per genus et differentiam*: laws properly so called are a species of commands, and a command combines a wish, an evil to be incurred on non-compliance, and its expression. Command, duty and sanction are "inseparably connected"; superiority means might. Kelsen keeps the coercive core without the sovereign: "the law is a coercive order" (p 1), and coercion distinguishes it from other normative orders. Hart, charitably, states the doctrine "in its strongest form" (CL 18): general orders backed by threats, issued by a sovereign habitually obeyed, supreme and independent (CL 24–25). Legal obligation becomes the gunman "situation writ large" (CL 82).

  *Hart's objections are about fit.* The gunman obliges the clerk to hand over the money, but the clerk has no obligation to do so; yet a tax evader, who faces a similar threat, is still under an obligation (CL 82). A theory that cannot mark that difference fails to describe how legal systems actually talk and work. The chapter III objections are of the same kind. Power-conferring rules — the rules for making a contract, a will or a marriage — impose no duty; an invalid will is a nullity, not a punishment. Legislators are bound by their own laws, which a sovereign issuing commands to others cannot explain. Customary law is not created by a sovereign at all, and Austin's reply that customs are tacit orders strains the facts. In chapter IV Hart shows that a habit of obedience cannot explain the continuity and persistence of law, because "habits are not 'normative': they cannot confer rights or authority on anyone" (CL 60). Each objection says: this is how real legal systems are, and the theory does not fit them. As Hart's method puts it, if a descriptive theory does not fit the facts, you cannot change the reality of law — you change the theory. That is not changing the subject.

  **Counterargument**

  Schauer's objection is that Hart's victory came at a price. "The phenomenon of law as it is experienced is overwhelmingly coercive." Most people, most of the time, meet the law as a threat: the fine, the licence cancelled, the prison. A theory that builds everything on the internal point of view of those who accept the rules may therefore "fail to 'fit the facts'" — the very criterion Hart used against Austin. And Schauer adds that "the focus of legal theory is dependent on a conception of what a legal theory is supposed to accomplish" (p 16). If a theory should capture law's salient features as well as its necessary ones, Hart's account is incomplete.

  **Evaluation**

  The objection is serious, but it proves less than the statement claims. First, Lamond's distinction between necessary and salient features does the work. Among the views he lists, coercion is the genus of law (Austin), the mark of a distinctive social ordering (Kelsen), the most salient aspect of law (Bentham, Holmes, Raz), what law regulates (Bobbio), or what legal practice exists to guide and constrain (Dworkin). Lamond concludes that coercion "is not the most salient aspect of law – at best it is the most salient aspect of certain legal systems" (p 56), and that the right to regulate coercion falls within law's distinctive claim to authority. So one can grant Schauer that coercion is salient in the systems we know without conceding that it belongs to the concept.

  Second, Raz's society of angels tests the conceptual claim directly. Angels comply with the rules, so coercion is idle; yet coordination problems remain, and something recognisable as law would solve them. If a legal system without coercion can be imagined, coercion is not a necessary feature of the concept of law.

  Third, Hart's theory does not ignore coercion; it relocates it. Given the natural facts about human beings, sanctions are "both possible and necessary in a municipal system" (CL 199) — a natural necessity, not a conceptual one, in exactly the way Hart treats the minimum content of natural law. His union of primary and secondary rules has room for sanctions as the content of some primary rules and for rules of adjudication that authorise their imposition. What it refuses is to make them the definition of law.

  So the statement fails. Hart answered Austin's question on Austin's terms. Schauer's reply is best understood as asking a different, metatheoretical question: whether legal theory should describe what is necessary to law or what is most prominent in our experience of it. That question is worth asking, and it is a fair criticism of the emphasis of *The Concept of Law*. But it is Schauer, not Hart, who has changed the subject, and on the original subject Hart's theory fits the facts better than Austin's.
