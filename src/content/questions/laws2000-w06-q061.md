model: |
  State officials enjoy immunities because they personify the state and need protection to perform their
  functions; in Arrest Warrant the ICJ said a foreign minister's immunities are "not granted for their personal
  benefit, but to ensure the effective performance of their functions". The source is customary international law,
  and because the immunity belongs to the state only the state can waive it.

  Functional immunity (ratione materiae) attaches to the act: it covers only official acts done with official
  authority, 'under the colour of authority', and continues after the official leaves office. A soldier who kills
  an enemy soldier on the battlefield is covered. Personal immunity (ratione personae) attaches to the office: it is
  complete, covering private acts too, but only for very high-ranking officials — heads of state, heads of
  government and foreign ministers (Djibouti v France) — and only while in office.

  So a sitting Greek president who drives drunk in Australia and kills someone is immune there, though Greece's own
  courts could try them. Once they leave office, personal immunity ends and functional immunity does not cover a
  private holiday act, so they could be arrested and prosecuted in Australia.
