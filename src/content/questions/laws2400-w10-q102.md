prompt: With reference to the CLA and common law explain the difference between factual causation and legal causation. What was the issue in Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital [1969] 1 QB 428 that meant that the Hospital was not liable in negligence for the death of Mr Barnett?
model: |-
  Causation has two elements, and both must be established (CLA s 5D(1)). Factual causation is a question of fact: did the defendant's act or omission in fact cause the harm? At common law it is the 'but for' test — would the plaintiff have suffered the damage but for the defendant's negligence (March v Stramare)? Under s 5D(1)(a) it is whether the negligence was a necessary condition of the occurrence of the harm, which is the same test in statutory language (Strong v Woolworths). It is proved by evidence, on the balance of probabilities (s 5E).

  Legal causation — remoteness, or scope of liability under s 5D(1)(b) — is a legal question involving policy: given that the breach caused the harm, should the defendant be held legally responsible for it? Section 5D(4) requires the court to consider whether and why responsibility should be imposed. Section 5D(1) treats the two as separate and distinct issues (Adeels Palace v Moubarak).

  In Barnett the duty doctor negligently refused to see the deceased, who had drunk tea poisoned with arsenic. The issue was factual causation: even timely treatment would not have saved his life. The negligence was therefore not a 'but for' cause of the death, and the Hospital was not liable.
