### Slide page 19

- International Law DEFINING INTERNATIONAL LAW International law is different from national legal systems. Unlike in a domestic legal system… ― there is is no universally compulsory procedure for the settlement of international disputes. ― there is neither a legislative nor an executive branch in the international legal system. International law rules become legally binding when there is sufficient consensus & practice among states to generate a rule of customary international law or when states agree to be bound by a treaty. ― sovereign States are the makers of international law & its ‘enforcers’ (if, when they choose so).
- However, ‘enforcement’ is irrelevant to the binding quality of international law as a system of law.

- PART B A BRIEF HISTORY OF INTERNATIONAL LAW

### Slide page 23

- International Law A BRIEF HISTORY • ‘Nomos’ Ancient Greece (the idea of restricting natural freedoms for the sake of expediency and self- interest) • “Jus naturale…Jus civile…” Ancient Roman Law • Thucydides, History of Peloponnesian War • “Right, as the world goes, is only in question between equals in power, while the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must.” • “Sovereign Equality” (Art 2 UN Charter)

### Slide page 24

- International Law A BRIEF HISTORY Early modern international law • Europe, late Middle Ages (15th and 16th centuries) • Political and religious forces: Holy Roman Empire & the Catholic Church • Theories of natural law (Jus Naturale): intrinsic values that govern relations between individuals and their social surrounding. Universal and applicable across time and place. • Jus gentium: rules of law common to all nations

### Slide page 25

- International Law A BRIEF HISTORY Early modern international law (cont.) • Colonialism • Francisco de Vitoria (1483–1546) : the waging of war against the natives of the New World was not justified under natural law. • 17th and 18th centuries: dawn of a modern international legal system (clearer distinction between jus naturale and jus gentium) • Jesuit Francisco Suárez (1548–1617): A Treatise on Laws and God the Lawgiver • Hugo Grotius (1583–1645): The Freedom of the Seas and On the Law of War and Peace • Emmerich de Vattel (1714–1767): Law of Nations

### Slide page 26

- International Law A BRIEF HISTORY Peace of Westphalia 1648 • ‘Beginning’ of the international state system • Peace of Munster and the Treaties of Munster and Osnabruck • The idea was to organise territory and individuals into sovereign states • Need for a system that contained a clear source of authority • Birth of the notion of ‘sovereignty’

### Slide page 27

- International Law A BRIEF HISTORY The 19th century • Positivism: the only true source of law is State will. Not fairness, not divine justice but consent to be bound by a legal obligation. • States can choose which legal obligations they are bound to. • 1865 International Telegraph Union • 1874 Universal Postal Union • 1856 Declaration of Paris: set legal limits to the capture of private property at sea • 1868 Declaration of St Petersburg: banned the use of explosive bullets. • Hague Conferences in 1899 and 1907: led to the adoption of conventions on the conduct of war and established a Permanent Court of Arbitration.
- • Colonialism continues… at the outbreak of the World War I in 1914, 90 per cent of Africa was under European control.

### Slide page 28

- International Law A BRIEF HISTORY The Interwar Period • 1919: Creation of the League of Nations • 1928: Treaty of Paris (also known as the Kellogg–Briand Pact) established an obligation that states should refrain from going to war as a means of settling their international controversies and as an instrument of national policy. • 1920: Permanent Court of International Justice (PCIJ), (*dissolved in 1946 , now International Court of Justice (ICJ))

### Slide page 29

- International Law A BRIEF HISTORY The period after the end of the Second World War • 1945-1946: Nuremberg Trials prosecuting top German officials for international crimes • 1946: League of Nations was replaced by the United Nations (UN) • UN Charter: Respect for the principle of equal rights and self-determination of peoples and on the sovereign equality of all UN member states; ban on the use of force • UN Security Council & General Assembly • 1946: International Law Commission (ILC) to promote the ‘progressive development of international law and its codification’ • 1960: Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples
