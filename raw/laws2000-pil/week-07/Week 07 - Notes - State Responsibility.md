### Slide page 4

- State Responsibility - INTRO Rules of ‘state responsibility’ indicate • Circumstances in which a state’s responsibility for the violation of an international obligation is triggered and • The consequences this entails, including how other states may respond to this. Rules of State responsibility are of a secondary nature • Primary rules (found in treaty law or customary int’l law) determine the substantive legal obligations (e.g. non-use of force). • Rules of State responsibility determine the legal consequences arising from a breach of those obligations.
- General regime of state responsibility • Concerns states only • Not a specialised regime 4

### Slide page 5

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY LAW ON STATE RESPONSIBILITY Studied and developed by the International Law Commission (‘ILC’) over more than 40 years. • Now codified and developed in the ILC’s Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally • Wrongful Acts, adopted on 10 August 2001 (‘ARSIWA’). The 2001 ILC Articles provide the practical and conceptual structure within which issues of State • responsibility can be examined. Not binding but many articles appear to codify CIL. • 5

### Slide page 6

- International Law INTERNATIONALLY WRONGFUL CONDUCT Internationally wrongful act “every internationally wrongful act of a State entails its international responsibility” (Art. 1 ARSIWA). Two elements of an internationally wrongful act An internationally wrongful act exists when conduct consisting of an act or omission is attributable to a State and constitutes a breach of an international obligation owed by that State. (Art. 2 ARSIWA). 6

### Slide page 8

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY INTERNATIONALLY WRONGFUL ACT Attribution Articles 4-11, Chapter III of ARSIWA (deal with attribution) In principle, States are only responsible for their own actions, not for those of private individuals. However, under certain circumstances, a State can be found to be in breach of its international obligations in relation to actions of private individuals. e.g., Corfu Channel Case (UK v Albania) [1949] ICJ para 22: a State may not ‘allow knowingly its territory to be used for acts contrary to the rights of other States’ 8

ALBANIA), ICJ, 1949. ✓ In October 1946, two British cruisers and two destroyers entered the North Corfu Strait and traveled into Albanian waters. ✓ The passage was regarded as safe: it had been swept in 1944 and check-swept in 1945. ✓ Two ships struck a mine and were gravely damaged. ### Slide page 10

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY - A TTRIBUTION A TTRIBUTION Acts by an organ of the state Article 4, ARSIWA The conduct of any State organ shall be considered an act of that State under international law, 1. whether the organ exercises legislative, executive, judicial or any other functions, whatever position it holds in the organization of the State, and whatever its character as an organ of the central Government or of a territorial unit of the State. An organ includes any person or entity which has that status in accordance with the internal law of 2. the State. 10

### Slide page 11

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY - ATTRIBUTION A TTRIBUTION Acts by an organ of the state Application of the Genocide Convention (Bosnia) ICJ 2007 “… according to the Court’s jurisprudence, persons, groups of persons or entities may, for purposes of international responsibility, be equated with State organs even if that status does not follow from internal law, provided that in fact the persons, groups or entities act in “complete dependence” on the State, of which they are ultimately merely the instrument.
- In such a case, it is appropriate to look beyond legal status alone, in order to grasp the reality of the relationship between the person taking action, and the State to which he is so closely attached as to appear to be nothing more than its agent: any other solution would allow States to escape their international responsibility by choosing to act through persons or entities whose supposed independence would be purely fictitious. … However, so to equate persons or entities with State organs when they do not have that status under internal law must be exceptional…” (paras 392-393) 11

### Slide page 12

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY - A TTRIBUTION A TTRIBUTION Acts by entities exercising governmental authority Article 5, ARSIWA ‘… a person or entity which is not an organ of the State under article 4 but which is (i) empowered by the law of that State to exercise elements of the governmental authority shall be considered an act of the State under international law… (ii) provided the person or entity is acting in that capacity in the particular instance. This provision applies to situations where State functions have been delegated to private entities: e.g., o privatisation of military and police functions.
- Delegation of state powers to private parties means that their actions would still qualify as ‘exercise of o governmental authority’. 12

E.g., Sending a health service section to another state to fight a disease outbreak. 13

### Slide page 14

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY - A TTRIBUTION A TTRIBUTION For acts ultra vires “The conduct of an organ of a State or of a person or entity empowered to exercise elements of the governmental authority shall be considered an act of the State under international law if the organ, person or entity acts in that capacity, even if it exceeds its authority or contravenes instructions..” (Art. 7 ARSIWA). ### Slide page 18

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY - A TTRIBUTION Effective control test Application of the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, (Bosnia and Herzegovina v Serbia and Montenegro) (Judgement) ICJ Reports 43 2007.
- “It must next be noted that the ‘overall control’ test has the major drawback of broadening the scope of State responsibility well beyond the fundamental principle governing the law of international responsibility: a State is responsible only for its own conduct … a State’s responsibility can be incurred for acts committed by persons or groups of persons [only if ] … they are attributable to it under the rule of customary international law reflected in article 8 cited above (paragraph 398).
- This is so where an organ of the State gave the instructions or provided the direction pursuant to which the perpetrators of the wrongful act acted or where it exercised effective control over the action during which the wrong was committed. In this regard the ‘overall control’ test is unsuitable, for it stretches too far, almost to breaking point, the connection which must exist between the conduct of a State’s organs and its international responsibility. 18

### Slide page 29

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY: CIRCUMSTANCES PRECLUDING WRONGFULNESS Force majeure Art 23(1), ARSIWA “The wrongfulness of an act of a State not in conformity with an international obligation of that State is precluded if the act is due to force majeure, that is the occurrence of an irresistible force or of an unforeseen event, beyond the control of the State, making it materially impossible in the circumstances to perform the obligation.” Involuntary, unintentional conduct, beyond the control of the State ➔ No one should be legally required to perform the impossible ➔ See Rainbow Warrior Case, France-New Zealand Arbitration Tribunal, 82 ➔ I.L.R.

### Slide page 31

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY: CIRCUMSTANCES PRECLUDING WRONGFULNESS Necessity Art 25, ARSIWA “Necessity may not be invoked by a State as a ground for precluding the wrongfulness of an act not in conformity with an international obligation of that State unless the act: (a) is the only way for the State to safeguard an essential interest against a grave and imminent peril; and (b) does not seriously impair an essential interest of the State or States towards which the obligation exists, or of the international community as a whole.” Relates to rare and truly exceptional situations: only way to avert a ‘grave and imminent peril’ is to refrain from performing another legal obligation which is considered to be of a lesser weight of urgency.
- (see Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros Project 1997 paras 49-55.) 31

### Slide page 32

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY: CIRCUMSTANCES PRECLUDING WRONGFULNESS Compliance with peremptory norms Art 26, ARSIWA “Nothing in this chapter [ on circumstances precluding wrongfulness] precludes the wrongfulness of any act of a State which is not in conformity with an obligation arising under a peremptory norm of general international law”. 32

### Slide page 33

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY: CONSEQUENCES OF INTERNATIONAL WRONGFUL CONDUCT Obligation to cease unlawful conduct and not repeat it Art. 30, ARSIWA Cessation and non-repetition “The State responsible for the internationally wrongful act is under an obligation: (a) to cease that act, if it is continuing; (b) to offer appropriate assurances and guarantees of non-repetition, if circumstances so require.” ➔ See Construction of a Wall (ICJ Advisory Opinion): Israel ‘must offer appropriate assurances and guarantees of non-repetition’ 33

### Slide page 34

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY: CONSEQUENCES OF INTERNATIONAL WRONGFUL CONDUCT Obligation to make full reparations for the injury caused by the unlawful act Art. 31, ARSIWA Reparation “1. The responsible State is under an obligation to make full reparation for the injury caused by the internationally wrongful act. 2.
- Injury includes any damage, whether material or moral, caused by the internationally wrongful act of a State.” ➔ Factory at Chorzow case, 1928 PCIJ “the purpose of such reparation is to as far as possible, wipe all the consequences of the illegal act and re-establish the situation which would, in all probability, have existed if that act had not been committed…” ➔ Three forms of reparation: restitution (Art 35 ARSIWA), compensation (Art. 36 ARSIWA), and satisfaction.(Art. 37 ARSIWA) 34

### Slide page 36

- International Law STATE RESPONSIBILITY: WHO MAY INVOKE A STATE’S INTERNATIONAL RESPONSIBILITY? The injured State(s) The rule is that only the State(s) whose individual rights have been violated are entitled to invoke international responsibility (demand cessation…reparation, etc.) Non-injured States If an erga omnes norm has been violated (e.g.. non-use of force principle, prohibition of aggression and genocide, and … breaches of international environmental law.) Art.
- 48, ARSIWA ”Any State other than an injured State is entitled to invoke the responsibility of another State in accordance with paragraph 2 if: (a) the obligation breached is owed to a group of States including that State, and is established for the protection of a collective interest of the group; or (b) the obligation breached is owed to the international community as a whole.” ➔ Barcelona Traction Case (Belgium v Spain) ICJ (1970): obligations erga omnes. 36
