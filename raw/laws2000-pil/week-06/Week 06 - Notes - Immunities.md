### Slide page 5

- International Law STATE IMMUNITY The international law of immunities sets limits on when a state may exercise jurisdiction within its own territory. Grounded in the fundamental principle of sovereign equality, which reflects the independence of states => also juridically equal, therefore no state may exercise jurisdiction over another without consent That is, the domestic courts of one state may decline to exercise jurisdiction if the respondent in the case is another state (incl. cases concerning foreign states’ officials or property)

### Slide page 6

- International Law STATE IMMUNITY Immunity for Adjudicative Jurisdiction vs Immunity from Enforcement Jurisdiction • Adjudicative Jurisdiction relates to the power of a court to adjudicate on matter. In other words, to look into the merits of the subject matter and render a decision based on the applicable legal rules and principles. • Enforcement Jurisdiction concerns the implementation of whatever judgment is rendered. So, the question of enforcing arises, only after a) adjudicative jurisdiction has been established and b) a judgment entailing some sort of enforcement measures (e.g. confiscation of certain assets) is rendered.
- → In practice,: ― State immunity from adjudication means that a state cannot be sued before a foreign court unless it gives its consent. ― State immunity from enforcement means that the judgment which has been rendered cannot be enforced upon the state due to immunity.

### Slide page 7

- International Law STATE IMMUNITY • Immunity acts as a procedural barrier to a court’s adjudicatory jurisdiction. • State immunity is a principle of customary international law – a principle well established in State practice and in the jurisprudence of national and international courts.
- There have been a few efforts to codify this understanding: → Initial work undertaken by the International Law Commission (ILC) Draft Articles on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and their Property (1991) → Resulted in the UN Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and their Property (2004) => comes into force once there are 30 ratifications (as of 2026, 25 ratifications/accession have been received) https://treaties.un.org/pages/showDetails.aspx?objid=0800000280057946&clang=_en

### Slide page 8

- International Law STATE IMMUNITY UNITED NATIONS CONVENTION ON JURISDICTIONAL IMMUNITIES OF STATES AND THEIR PROPERTY (2004) General Principles Art 5: ‘A State enjoys immunity, in respect of itself and its property, from the jurisdiction of the courts of another State Art 6: modalities for giving effect to state immunity (refraining from exercising jurisdiction before its courts) Art 7: no immunity if state expressly consents to the exercise of foreign jurisdiction (e.g., by agreement) Art 8: no immunity if state participates in court proceedings Art 9: no immunity in counterclaims (arising from proceedings initiated by a state or otherwise participated in) 8

- 2. Immunities of state officials

### Slide page 22

- International Law IMMUNITIES OF STATE REPRESENTATIVES State representatives enjoy certain immunities from national jurisdiction because they ‘personify’ the State on whose behalf they act and because they need protection from national jurisdiction in order to fulfil their functions as state representatives. As the ICJ held in the Arrest Warrant case of 2011 between Congo and Belgium: “In customary international law, the immunities accorded to Ministers for Foreign Affairs are not granted for their personal benefit, but to ensure the effective performance of their functions on behalf of their respective States.
- … In the performance of these: functions, he or she is frequently required to travel internationally, and thus must be in a position freely to do so whenever the need should arise. He or she must also be in constant communication with the Government, and with its diplomatic missions around the world, and be capable at any time of communicating with representatives of other States…”

### Slide page 23

- International Law IMMUNITIES OF STATE REPRESENTATIVES State officials/representatives of the state (individuals) ➔ Head of state ➔ Ministers ➔ Government officials / Civil servants The extent of immunity offered to various representatives differs substantially depending on the office they hold (function they serve) and whether they acted in official capacity.

### Slide page 24

- International Law IMMUNITIES OF STATE REPRESENTATIVES Functional immunity (ratione materiae) – (this immunity relates not to the person itself but the function that the official performs, it’s about the act itself being an official act of the state ) o Immunity only in relation to official acts taken while in office o Continues after official leaves office. o Based on customary international law o As long as an act is performed by an individual with official authority (‘under the colour of authority’) it is an ‘official’ act. And for the act the individual is immune from criminal prosecution before foreign national courts.
- o Can be waived only with state’s consent Personal immunity (ratione personae) - (protection you enjoy because of the office you hold – this being a personifying the state) o Complete immunity, even in relation to acts of a private nature o Applies only to very high-ranking state officials (i.e., in particular, heads of state, heads of government, foreign ministers) o Heads of state & Foreign Ministers enjoy privileged status in international law (see Certain Questions of Mutual Assistance in Criminal Matters (Djibouti v.
- France) 2008, ICJ found this is customary international law) o Temporal limit: only for the duration of the officials’ time in office (after which they continue to enjoy functional immunity) o Can be waived by the state

### Slide page 25

- International Law IMMUNITIES OF STATE REPRESENTATIVES Question: Would a State representative be immune from domestic criminal proceedings for alleged serious violations of international law, such as war crimes or crimes against humanity? ➔ Under the current state of international law: both functional and personal immunities continue to apply, even in cases of serious international crimes (e.g. war crimes, crimes against humanity, genocide) - see e.g. Arrest Warrant of 11 April 2000 (Democratic Republic of Congo v Belgium) (Judgment) [2002] ICJ Rep 3 paras 58 et seq.
- - ICJ ruled that current state practice does not support existence of an exception to immunity from foreign criminal jurisdiction in situations concerning the commission of international crimes…However, it noted that immunity does not mean impunity… ➔ This case concerns an incumbent foreign minister (i.e. personal immunity).

### Slide page 26

- International Law IMMUNITIES OF STATE REPRESENTATIVES Arrest Warrant of 11 April 2000 (Democratic Republic of Congo v Belgium) (Judgment) [2002] ICJ Rep 3 paras 58-61. “The Court emphasizes, however, that the immunity from jurisdiction enjoyed by incumbent Ministers for Foreign Affairs does not mean that they enjoy impunity in respect of any crimes they might have committed… Immunity from criminal jurisdiction and individual criminal responsibility are quite separate concepts. While jurisdictional immunity is procedural in nature, criminal responsibility is a question of substantive law.
- Jurisdictional immunity may well bar prosecution for a certain period or for certain offences; it cannot exonerate the person to whom it applies from all criminal responsibility.” Criminal prosecution of the individual is still possible: 1. Domestic prosecution in their own countries: such persons enjoy no criminal immunity under international law in their own countries and may thus be tried by those countries' courts in accordance with the relevant rules of domestic law. 2.
- Their countries can waive their immunity: these persons will cease to enjoy immunity from foreign jurisdiction if the State which they represent or have represented decides to waive that immunity. 3. Once the individual leaves their functions, other states with jurisdiction could try them: That applies in respect of acts committed before or after their period of office and for acts committed during that period of office in a private capacity. 4.
- International Criminal Courts may also be able to prosecute: an incumbent or former Minister for Foreign Affairs may be subject to criminal proceedings before certain international criminal courts, where they have jurisdiction. e.g. the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia, the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda, and the International Criminal Court.

- PART B 1. Diplomatic Immunities

### Slide page 29

- International Law DIPLOMATIC IMMUNITIES Functions of diplomatic missions include the representation, promotion and protection of the interests and nationals of the sending state.
- Diplomats and consular officials– benefit from a special legal protection regime codified in two conventions (prior to 1961 entirely governed by customary international law or through bilateral treaties): - Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (VCDR, 1961) - Vienna Convention on Consular Relations (VCCR, 1963) ICJ, Teheran Hostage, 1980, para 86 “the rules of diplomatic law … constitute a self-contained regime, which on the one hand, lays out the receiving State’s obligations regarding the facilities, privileges and immunities to be accorded to diplomatic missions and, on the other, foresees their possible abuse by members of the mission and specifies the means at the disposal of the receiving State to counter any such abuse.”

### Slide page 30

- International Law DIPLOMATIC IMMUNITIES To whom do diplomatic immunities apply?
- • Diplomatic agents (e.g., ambassadors but also lower-level diplomatic agents) • Consular agents (who represent the state in administrative or technical matters) • Members of the family of a diplomatic agent forming part of the household enjoy immunity from jurisdiction, unless nationals of the receiving state • Members of the administrative and technical staff of the mission (subject to greater exceptions) along with family members making up their households, if not nationals or permanent residents of the receiving State • Members of service staff who are not national or permanent residents enjoy immunity in respect to acts performed in the course of their official duties

### Slide page 31

- International Law DIPLOMATIC IMMUNITIES Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961), Art. 31: A diplomatic agent shall enjoy immunity from the criminal jurisdiction of the receiving State (personal immunity).
- …shall also enjoy immunity (personal immunity) from its civil and administrative jurisdiction, except in the case of: (a) A real action relating to private immovable property situated in the territory of the receiving State, unless he holds it on behalf of the sending State for the purposes of the mission; (b) An action relating to succession in which the diplomatic agent is involved as executor, administrator, heir or legatee as a private person and not on behalf of the sending State; (c) An action relating to any professional or commercial activity exercised by the diplomatic agent in the receiving State outside his official functions.

### Slide page 32

- International Law DIPLOMATIC IMMUNITIES Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961) • Article 29: The person of a diplomatic agent is inviolable (e.g. cannot be arrested). • Article 30: The private residence of a diplomatic agent, his papers and correspondence, are also inviolable. • Article 41: A diplomatic agent has a duty to respect the laws and regulations of the receiving State. They also have a duty not to interfere in the internal affairs of the state.

- International Law DIPLOMATIC IMMUNITIES Vienna Convention on Consular Relations (VCCR, 1963) o Immunities are more restricted. o Limited personal inviolability from arrest or detention (e.g., can be arrested for a ‘grave crime’) (Art. 41, VCCR) o Immunity ratione materiae only with respect to criminal and civil matters (Art. 43, VCCR). 33
