Hybrid and Other Tribunals — Classification
The ad hoc ICTY and ICTR were located far from the countries they served.
A need was recognised for other tribunals to address serious crimes committed in other parts of the world.
Treaty-based, ‘hybrid’ courts with both national and international elements were proposed to help create efficient, more locally based courts to address serious international crimes.
Hybrid or internationalised criminal tribunals are part national and part international in nature. This can be discerned from:
- the way they were established (e.g. agreement between the host State and the UN);
- their subject matter-jurisdiction (both international crimes and national crimes); and
- their staff (generally, international and national judges and practitioners are employed in hybrid, internationalised or mixed criminal tribunals).

Hybrid and Other Tribunals — The Special Court for Sierra Leone
In an attempt to promote justice and end impunity for the atrocities committed by warring factions in Sierra Leone during its civil war, the UN and the Government of Sierra Leone jointly established the SCSL in 2002.
Its mandate was to try “persons who bear the greatest responsibility for serious violations of international humanitarian law and Sierra Leonean law committed in the territory of Sierra Leone since 30 November 1996”.
The SCSL was not a part of the Sierra Leonean legal system, but did include some aspects of Sierra Leonean law (although none of these laws were used by the SCSL).
The SCSL also employed Sierra Leoneans as staff members and had its permanent seat is in Freetown, Sierra Leone.
Not a UN body (unlike the ICTY and ICTR), it had jurisdiction over international crimes, and employed international staff, including a majority of international judges.

--- slide 28 ---
Hybrid and Other Tribunals — The Special Court for Sierra Leone
The SCSL had jurisdiction over:
- crimes against humanity,
- violations of Article 3 common to the Geneva Conventions and of Additional Protocol II,
- other serious violations of international humanitarian law,
- and certain crimes under Sierra Leonean law.
Milestones:
- The SCSL convicted nine persons, imposing sentences of terms of imprisonment ranging from 15 to 52 years.
- The SCSL was the first international(ised) criminal tribunal to try and convict persons for:
  - the use of child soldiers (AFRC trial),
  - forced marriage (an other inhumane act) as a crime against humanity (RUF trial); and
  - attacks directed against UN peacekeepers (RUF trial).
- The SCSL was the first modern ICT to indict, try, and convict a sitting head of state (Taylor trial).

Special Tribunal for Lebanon (STL)
Establishment
1. In 2005, the Government of Lebanon requested the UN to establish a tribunal to try those responsible for the 14 February 2005 attack that killed Prime Minister Rafic Hariri and 22 others
2. The STL Statute, annexed to UN Security Council Resolution 1757 entered into force on 10 June 2007
Jurisdiction
3. The STL Statute stipulated that the STL shall apply provisions of the Lebanese Criminal Code relating to terrorism, among other crimes
International Character
1. The international character of the STL was explicitly stipulated in the request submitted by the Government of Lebanon to the UN Secretary-General
2. The UN and Lebanon agreed that the STL would have a mixed composition with both Lebanese and international judges, as well as an international prosecutor

The International Criminal Court — Establishment
In 1948, when the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide was adopted, the UN General Assembly recognised the need for a permanent international court to deal with the kinds of atrocities which had just been perpetrated.
The idea of a system of international criminal justice re-emerged after the end of the Cold War.
On 17 July 1998, in Rome, a conference of 160 States established the first treaty-based permanent international criminal court.
Among other things, the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court (Rome Statute) sets out the crimes falling within the Court’s jurisdiction, certain rules of procedure, and the mechanisms for States to cooperate with the ICC.
125 countries are States Parties to the Rome Statute, representing all regions: Africa (33 States), Asia Pacific (19 States), Eastern Europe (20 States), Latin America and the Caribbean (28 States), as well as Western Europe and North America (25 States). Any notable exceptions?

The International Criminal Court — case from start to finish (https://www.icc-cpi.int/about/how-the-court-works)
International Criminal Court: Legal Process
1. Preliminary Examinations
The Office of the Prosecutor must determine:
• Whether there is sufficient evidence of crimes of sufficient gravity falling within the ICC's jurisdiction
• Whether there are genuine national proceedings
• Whether opening an investigation would serve the interests of justice and victims
2. Investigations
After gathering evidence and identifying a suspect, the Prosecution requests ICC judges to issue:
1. An arrest warrant: the ICC relies on countries to make arrests and transfer suspects to the ICC; or
2. A summons to appear: suspects appear voluntarily (if not, an arrest warrant may be issued)
3. Pre-Trial Stage
Initial appearance: Three Pre-Trial judges confirm suspect's identity and ensure suspect understands the charges.
Confirmation of charges hearings: After hearing the Prosecution, the Defence, and the Legal representative of victims, the judges decide (usually within 60 days) if there is enough evidence for the case to go to trial.
4. Trial Stage
• Before three Trial judges, the Prosecution must prove beyond reasonable doubt the guilt of the accused.
• Judges consider all evidence, then issue a verdict and, when guilty, issue a sentence. Judges can sentence a person to up to 30 years, and under exceptional circumstances, life.
• Judges can also order reparations for the victims.
5. Appeals Stage
Both the Prosecutor and the Defence have the right to appeal a Trial Chamber's decision on the verdict (decision on guilt or innocence of the accused) and the sentence.
6. Enforcement of Sentence
Sentences are served in countries that have agreed to enforce ICC sentences.

The International Criminal Court — Jurisdiction (example Genocide)
ARTICLE 6: GENOCIDE — Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court
For the purpose of this Statute, "genocide" means any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such:
A. Killing members of the group
B. Causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group
C. Deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part
D. Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group
E. Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group
KEY ELEMENT: Acts committed with intent to destroy a protected group
