- 2 Criteria of Statehood

### Slide page 19

- International Law STATEHOOD AND LEGAL PERSONALITY Article 1 of the Montevideo Convention on Rights and Duties of States (1933): The state as a person of international law should possess the following qualifications: a. a permanent population; b. a defined territory; c. government; and d. capacity to enter into relations with other states. => Widely regarded as codifying customary international law

### Slide page 20

- International Law STATEHOOD & RECOGNITION A) POPULATION • No fixed or minimum number prescribed (e.g. Nauru around 12,000 inhabitants, China around 1.4 billion) - i.e. the size of the population is irrelevant • Required is some core population that inhabits the territory on a permanent basis (but the criterion does not relate to nationality)

### Slide page 21

- International Law STATEHOOD & RECOGNITION B) DEFINED TERRITORY • Generally understood that states are territorial entities: cannot exist without a certain territory • No minimum size prescribed (e.g. Vatican with around 0.5 sq km vs. Russia with 17.1 million sq km) [For comparison: Sydney (inner city) 25 sq kms and 5+ million population] • ‘Micro states’, such as Vatican, Monaco, Nauru, Tuvalu, San Marino, Liechtenstein etc. • Defined physical boundaries, but does not mean it should be completely fixed or entirely undisputed (e.g. border disputes are a common phenomenon among states) See: North Sea Continental Shelf, ICJ Reports 1969, p.3.
- para 46: “The appurtenance of a given area, considered as an entity, in no way governs the precise delimitation of its boundaries, any more than uncertainty as to boundaries can affect territorial rights. There is for instance no rule that the land frontiers of a State must be fully delimited and defined, and often in various places and for long periods they are not…”

### Slide page 22

- International Law STATEHOOD & RECOGNITION C) EFFECTIVE GOVERNMENT • Some form of coherent and effective exercise of public power over the territory and population • State as a ‘stable political community supporting a legal order to the exclusion of others in a given area’ (Crawford) • Yet, many governments around the world have proven unable to control parts of the state’s territory (e.g. civil war, insurgence, foreign occupation etc.) Þ Fragile or failed states? (e.g.
- Somalia, Syria, Yemen, Afghanistan) Þ Once statehood is established, it carries a presumption of continuity: ineffectiveness of government does not necessarily affect its statehood (but it remains important for establishment and recognition of new states) Þ no specific form of government prescribed, e.g. both dictatorships and democracies are irrelevant to statehood

### Slide page 23

- International Law STATEHOOD & RECOGNITION D) CAPACITY TO ENTER INTO INTERNATIONAL RELATIONS • Capacity of a state to contract with other states internationally • Capacity arises from independence and recognition by other states (e.g. Scotland might meet the three other criteria but does not have the ability to enter into international relations on its own)

- 3. Recognition

### Slide page 25

- International Law STATEHOOD & RECOGNITION • Recognition is used in a broad sense referring to the act by which a state officially acknowledges the existence of another state or government. It is a complex mixture of politics and law. • The international law on recognition, is often an extension of politics. • Lack of diplomatic relations between two states does not necessarily mean absence of recognition

### Slide page 26

- International Law STATEHOOD & RECOGNITION Two Theories of Recognition Declaratory theory: • When an existing state ‘recognises’ a new state this is said to be nothing more than an acknowledgment of a pre-existing legal capacity (statehood). • The attainment of statehood by an entity is not depended on recognition by other existing states. Constitutive theory: • Recognition is seen as necessary precondition to the existence of a state. Recognition by other states that creates the new state and confers to it legal personality. • No recognition, no existence.

- 4. Modes of Territorial Acquisition

### Slide page 34

- Acquisition of Territory in International law • Establishment of sovereignty over a given piece of land (continental territory or an island). • A state can acquire title of sovereignty to new territory in a number of ways (known as ‘modes of acquisition of territory’). 1. Cession 2. Accretion 3. Effective Occupation of Terra Nullius 4. Prescription 5. Conquest (Belligerent Occupation) 34

### Slide page 35

- 1. CESSION • A State can purchase territory from another state through cession. e.g. US purchase of Alaska from Russia in 1867 and the 1917 US acquisition of the Danish West Indies from Denmark. • The state that acquires the territory cannot obtain more rights to the territory than those possessed by the ceding state. Additionally, the acquiring state must respect the potential rights of third states & indigenous population. 35

### Slide page 36

- 2. ACCRETION • New land is created gradually by nature e.g. new islands are formed by volcanic eruption. • 1986: an undersea volcano and the creation of a new island in Japan's territorial sea. 36

### Slide page 37

In the exclusive economic zone, the coastal State shall have the exclusive right to construct and to authorize and regulate the construction, operation and use of: (a) artificial islands…for the purposes provided for in article 56 and other economic purposes. 8. Artificial islands, installations and structures do not possess the status of islands. They have no territorial sea of their own, and their presence does not affect the delimitation of the territorial sea, the exclusive economic zone or the continental shelf. What is an island?? 37

### Slide page 40

- 3. Occupation of terra nullius • A state can obtain title to newly discovered territory i.e. terra nullius • Title is acquired through occupation when a state demonstrates that it exercises effective control over a territory of 'no-man's land and intends to obtain title. 40

### Slide page 41

- 4. Occupation THROUGH PRESCRIPTION • A state acquires title to territory previously under the sovereignty of another state. • Controversial mode: derives from some form of implied consent from the dispossessed state. • The element of consent means that the acts of the acquiring state must be peaceful and that sustained protests and objections by the 'old' state will prevent prescription. 41

### Slide page 42

- 5. BELLIGERENT OCCUPATION • Using force to conquer foreign territory used to be a means of acquiring new territory. • Since UN Charter 1945: a state can not acquire territory through conquest (prohibition of acquisition of territory through the use of force). • Instead, one state's military occupation of the territory of another state activates the international legal regime on belligerent occupation. • State territory shall not be the object of acquisition by force and that attempts to disrupt the territorial integrity of a state are incompatible with the purposes and principles of the Charter. e.g.
- o Iraq's attempt to annex Kuwait following 1990 invasion o Turkey’s occupation of North Cyprus following its invasion in 1974 o Russia's 2014 annexation of the Crimean Peninsula in Ukraine & Russian’s invasion of Ukraine in 2022. o Israel and Palestine 42

### Slide page 44

### Slide page 46

- International Law STATEHOOD & RECOGNITION • Right to self-determination is well established e.g. UN Charter and related UNGA resolutions, • Also see Chagos case, self-determination in the decolonisation context, only one aspect.
- • However, a right of a peoples to unilaterally secede a piece of territory from an existing state is highly contentious (see Kosovo/Serbia case 2008) • Also arising in the context of referrendum for secession/independence => examples include South Sudan (2010) and failed attempts in Quebec (1979 & 1995) and Scotland (2014), but also contested unilateral attempts in the Kurdish region of Iraq (2017) and Catalonia in Spain (2017) • Argument was made that “right to self-determination” includes the right to secede from an existing state in extreme cases when an ethnic population has been subjected to systematic discrimination and oppression by the government, and thus the declaration of independence is a legitimate exercise of their right to self-determination.
- • Supreme Court of Canada re Secession of Quebec [1998] 2 SCR 2017: The ruling addressed the question of whether Quebec had the legal right to unilaterally secede from Canada under Canadian and international law. It held that unilateral secession is not consistent with international law or the Canadian Constitution.
