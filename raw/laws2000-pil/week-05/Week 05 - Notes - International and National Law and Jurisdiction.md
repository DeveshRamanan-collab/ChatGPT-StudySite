### Slide page 8

- Two Theories: Monism and Dualism International law and National Law Monism • Sees national and international law as part of one single normative legal order • International law has direct effect (incorporation) • Holds the same legal authority as national law in legal system • Inconsistency between the two legal systems must be resolved (frequently in favour of international law) • Often associated with civil law tradition Dualism • Sees international and national law as distinct legal orders • The only law that has force at the domestic level is national law – therefore international law must be transformed into domestic law to have effect (transformation) • Inconsistencies do not arise: the systems have different subjects and fulfil different purposes • Often associated with common law tradition In reality, blends of the two theories characterise the relationship of international law to most domestic legal orders

### Slide page 16

- v2 Tasmanian Dam Case (1983) International law and National Law Commonwealth v Tasmania [1983] 158 CLR 1 Facts: In 1974 Australia became party to the World Heritage Convention. The Commonwealth passed legislation to protect part of Tasmania’s wilderness from the building of a dam. The Tasmanian Government challenged the validity of the Commonwealth legislation on the grounds that the Commonwealth had no power to legislate on State matters, such as the dam. The High Court considered whether the Commonwealth Government had the power to stop the Tasmanian Government from damming the Gordon River under Commonwealth legislation.
- HC majority held: • Even though the dam would normally be a matter for Tasmania, in light of Australia’s international obligations the Commonwealth was entitled to intervene. • Australia is a party to the World Heritage Convention, and the High Court found that being a party to such an agreement directly relates to external affairs. This allows the Commonwealth to pass laws that give effect to Australia’s international legal obligations. • However, if the Commonwealth’s use of the external power was no more than a device to exercise legislative power then it would probably not be legitimate. 16

### Slide page 21

- Customary International Law: Transformation or Incorporation? International law and National Law Recall: Customary international law is formed when there is uniform/ widespread and consistent state practice across a wide range of states and where there is evidence that this practice is maintained out of a sense of legal obligation (opinio juris) What is its effect on Australian law?
- Debate surrounds two approaches : • The doctrine of transformation – customary international law only becomes part of Australian law when formally adopted by Parliament or the courts as part of common law • The doctrine of incorporation – that customary international law is automatically a source of Australian law, unless there is a rule to the contrary Australia has not clearly adopted either approach – and both doctrines have been recognised in High Court decisions  BUT: rule of customary international law cannot generally be adopted where inconsistent with domestic legislation or essential common law doctrine

### Slide page 22

- Customary International Law and Australian Law International law and National Law Nulyarimma v Thompson [1999] 96 FCR 153 Facts: Australia is party to the 1948 Genocide Convention. Member of the Aboriginal community claimed that sponsorship of Native Title Act amendments and failure to seek World Heritage Listing of Lake Eyre region were acts of genocide. Appellants argue that genocide is a part of customary international law, and that even without legislation criminalising genocide within Australia, Australian courts can try individuals accused of genocide.

### Slide page 24

- Customary International Law and Australian Law International law and National Law Nulyarimma v Thompson [1999] 96 FCR 153 Merkel J in dissent identified criteria for customary international law to become a source of common law: • The customary rule must have ‘attained ... general acceptance by ... the community of nations’ • The courts must consider whether the rule has been ‘received into and so become a source of English law’ [to be read Australian law] .

### Slide page 31

- International Law JURISDICTION Sovereignty implies: • Sovereign equality – states are regarded as equal in international law regardless of their size, population or wealth. • Jurisdiction – states have authority to create laws and enforce them. • Territorial control – the freedom to use and dispose of the territory under the state’s jurisdiction and to perform activities deemed necessary to the population. Territory = land, territorial sea up to 12 nautical miles from the coast and airspace above land and sea.
- • Duty of non-intervention – no other state should intrude into the state’s territory – ius excludendi alios (the right to exclude others). • Sovereign immunity – the right of immunity from the jurisdiction of foreign courts for acts performed by the state in its sovereign capacity. • Diplomatic immunity – the right of state representatives acting in their official capacity to immunity from the legal system of the state in which they are operating. 31

### Slide page 41

### Slide page 44

- International Law JURISDICTION Principle of Passive Personality A state can assert its jurisdiction over an offence committed abroad on the sole ground that the victim of the offence was a national of the state = State may claim for offences committed abroad which have affected nationals of that State. Yunis was captured in international waters by the FBI and brought to the U.S. to stand trial. The US court found that the U.S. had jurisdiction. • Passive personality principle remains controversial. • Better as mere subsidiary means of jurisdiction. 44
